At the end of Chapter 1 we counted which character follows which in Shakespeare, normalised the rows, and sampled. The result was letter soup with an English accent. That table was already a language model, just a bad one. This chapter turns the idea into a proper method: a clear assumption, a principled estimator, a way to *measure* how good the model is, and a fix for the problem that makes naïve counting fail on new text.

Everything here predates neural networks, but none of it is obsolete. The evaluation metric we derive — cross-entropy — is exactly the loss every model in this course is trained to minimise, including CourseGPT. The train/validation discipline, overfitting, and the tension between memorising and generalising all appear here in their simplest form.

## The Markov assumption

The chain rule from Chapter 1 predicts each symbol from *all* previous symbols. We cannot estimate $P(x_t \mid x_{<t})$ for every possible history. Almost every long history occurs only once, if at all. So we make a simplifying assumption: only the last few symbols matter.

:::equation{#markov caption="The n-gram (Markov) assumption: condition on the previous n − 1 symbols only."}
$$
P(x_t \mid x_{<t}) \;\approx\; P\big(x_t \,\big|\, \underbrace{x_{t-\term{n}{n}+1}, \ldots, x_{t-1}}_{\term{h}{h_t}}\big)
$$
:::

```terms
n:
  label: "$n$ — the order"
  what: The length of the n-grams the model counts. It predicts from the previous $n - 1$ symbols.
  why: It caps how much history we need to store and estimate. $n = 1$ ignores context entirely (a unigram model); $n = 2$ is Chapter 1’s bigram table.
  effect: Larger $n$ gives sharper, more fluent predictions — and exponentially more parameters, most of which are never seen in training. Choosing $n$ is this chapter’s central trade-off.
h:
  label: "$h$ — the history (context)"
  what: The $n - 1$ symbols immediately before the one being predicted.
  why: Under the Markov assumption, the whole past is summarised by this short window.
  effect: Two positions with the same $h$ get exactly the same prediction, however different their longer contexts are.
```

A model like this is a **Markov chain** of order $n-1$: the state is the last $n - 1$ symbols, and each step moves to a new state with a fixed transition probability. For a bigram model over words the chain is small enough to draw:

::markov-chain

The assumption is plainly false for language. Whether a sentence needs a verb depends on its subject, which may be many words back. But it is useful. For the next three chapters it is the only kind of model we have, and the Transformer can be seen as a way of relaxing it without paying the exponential price.

## Estimating probabilities by counting

Given a corpus, how should we set $P(w \mid h)$? The obvious answer is the fraction of times $h$ was followed by $w$:

:::equation{#mle caption="Maximum-likelihood estimate of an n-gram probability."}
$$
\hat P_{\text{MLE}}(\term{w}{w} \mid \term{h}{h}) \;=\; \frac{\term{chw}{c(h, w)}}{\term{ch}{c(h)}}
$$
:::

```terms
w:
  label: "$w$ — the next symbol"
  what: A candidate for the symbol that follows the context.
chw:
  label: "$c(h, w)$ — n-gram count"
  what: How many times the n-gram $h\,w$ (the context followed by $w$) appears in the training data.
  effect: This is the only information the model uses. If $c(h, w) = 0$ the estimate is exactly zero.
ch:
  label: "$c(h)$ — context count"
  what: How many times the context $h$ was followed by *anything*, i.e. $\sum_{w'} c(h, w')$.
  why: Dividing by it turns counts into a distribution over the next symbol that sums to 1.
  effect: When $c(h)$ is small the estimate is noisy; when $c(h) = 0$ it is undefined (0/0).
```

This “obvious” estimator is also the principled one. Treat the corpus as data generated by the model and choose the parameters that make the data most probable — the **maximum-likelihood estimate** (MLE). Because the model factorises over positions, the log-likelihood splits into one independent term per context:

$$
\ell(\theta) \;=\; \sum_t \log P_\theta(x_t \mid h_t) \;=\; \sum_h \sum_w c(h, w)\,\log P_\theta(w \mid h)
$$

For each context we have to choose a distribution $p_w$ that maximises $\sum_w c_w \log p_w$ subject to $\sum_w p_w = 1$.

:::details[Derivation: the MLE of a categorical distribution is the relative frequency]
Introduce a Lagrange multiplier $\mu$ for the constraint:

$$
\mathcal{L}(p, \mu) = \sum_w c_w \log p_w \;-\; \mu\Big(\sum_w p_w - 1\Big).
$$

Setting $\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial p_w = c_w / p_w - \mu = 0$ gives $p_w = c_w / \mu$. Summing over $w$ and using the constraint, $\mu = \sum_w c_w = N$. So $p_w = c_w / N$. The objective is concave (a sum of logs), so this stationary point is the global maximum. Appendix F covers constrained optimisation and Lagrange multipliers in more depth.
:::

The picture below makes the derivation concrete for three outcomes. Every point of the triangle is a possible distribution, coloured by the log-likelihood of the counts you choose.

### Sampling from the model

To *generate* text we repeatedly sample the next symbol from $P(\cdot \mid h)$, append it, and slide the context along. Sampling a symbol from a discrete distribution needs one uniform random number $u \in [0, 1)$. Lay the probabilities end to end along the unit interval and return whichever segment $u$ lands in. This is the **inverse-CDF** method. It is also, word for word, what happens every time an LLM samples a token: Chapter 15 adds temperature, top-k and top-p on top of it.

At $n = 1$ you get letter frequencies and nothing else. At $n = 3$ words start to appear, at $n = 5$ phrases, and at $n = 8$ the text is fluent Shakespeare. The “fluent” output is a collage: typically more than half of it lies inside passages of 15 or more characters copied verbatim from the training data. Most 7-character contexts were seen only a handful of times, so the model can only replay what followed them, splicing fragments together wherever two of them share a context. That is **memorisation**, and the next section gives us the tool to measure it.

## Measuring a language model

Eyeballing samples is not a metric. We want one number that says how well a model predicts text it has *not* seen. The natural candidate is the probability the model assigns to held-out text. That probability is astronomically small and shrinks with length, so we take its logarithm and average it per symbol:

:::equation{#cross-entropy caption="Cross-entropy of a model q on held-out text, in bits per symbol."}
$$
\term{H}{H} \;=\; -\frac{1}{\term{Tn}{T}} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log_2 \term{q}{q}(x_t \mid h_t)
$$
:::

:::equation{#perplexity caption="Perplexity: cross-entropy expressed as an effective number of choices."}
$$
\term{PPL}{\mathrm{PPL}} \;=\; 2^{H}
$$
:::

```terms
H:
  label: "$H$ — cross-entropy"
  what: The average surprisal, $-\log_2 q$, of each actual next symbol under the model — the number of bits per symbol an arithmetic coder using this model would need.
  why: It is the negative log-likelihood per symbol. Minimising it on training data is maximum-likelihood estimation. Every neural model in this course is trained by minimising exactly this quantity (in nats rather than bits).
  effect: Lower is better. A uniform guess over 65 characters costs $\log_2 65 \approx 6.02$ bits. One zero-probability event makes it infinite.
Tn:
  label: "$T$ — number of predictions"
  what: How many symbols are predicted, i.e. the length of the evaluation text.
  why: Averaging makes numbers comparable across texts of different lengths.
q:
  label: "$q(x_t \\mid h_t)$ — the model’s probability"
  what: The probability our model gave to the symbol that actually came next.
  why: We score the model only on what happened, not on what it predicted for alternatives. Because probabilities sum to 1, putting mass on wrong symbols is punished automatically.
PPL:
  label: "$\\mathrm{PPL}$ — perplexity"
  what: The effective number of equally likely choices the model is weighing at each step.
  why: A perplexity of 8 means the model is as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among 8 symbols — easier to interpret than bits.
  effect: Perplexity is exponential in cross-entropy, so small improvements in bits are large improvements in perplexity.
```

This is the same quantity as Chapter 1’s entropy, with one twist. Entropy $H(p)$ averages surprisal under the *true* distribution $p$ using $p$’s own probabilities. Cross-entropy averages the *model’s* surprisal $-\log q$ over data drawn from $p$. The two are related by

$$
H(p, q) \;=\; H(p) \;+\; D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q) \;\ge\; H(p),
$$

where the Kullback–Leibler divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}} \ge 0$ measures the extra bits we pay for using $q$ instead of $p$ (Gibbs’ inequality; see Appendix E). So a model’s cross-entropy on real text is an **upper bound** on the entropy of the language itself, and a better model gives a tighter bound. This is precisely how Shannon estimated the entropy of English, with human subjects as the model.

::surprisal-text

Most surprisal is concentrated at the start of words; inside a word, a trigram model already knows what is coming. Switch to MLE and increase $n$: the passage turns red wherever the model assigns probability zero, and the average becomes infinite.

::exercise{id="cross-entropy"}

:::warning
**Always evaluate on text the model did not train on.** Cross-entropy on the training text rewards memorisation: an 8-gram model scores under 1 bit per character on text it has already seen, and infinitely badly on new text. We split TinyShakespeare into a *training* set (first 90%) and a *validation* set (last 10%). A third, untouched *test* set is reserved for final numbers you report. Chapter 25 explains what goes wrong when test data leaks into training — a real problem for today’s LLMs.
:::

## The zero-probability problem

The MLE gives probability zero to every n-gram it has not seen. How common are unseen n-grams in new text? Very common, and increasingly so as $n$ grows:

::sparsity-plot

This is Zipf’s law from Chapter 1 again, applied to n-grams: there are $V^n$ possible n-grams, the counts follow a long-tailed distribution, and any finite corpus sees only a sliver of the possibilities. For characters, about half of all validation 8-grams never occur in training. For words, even most *trigrams* are new. An unsmoothed model therefore gives probability zero to almost every realistic sentence.

## Smoothing

**Smoothing** takes a little probability mass from events we have seen and redistributes it to events we have not. There are many ways to do it. Three ideas cover most of what matters.

### Add-k: pretend you saw everything

The simplest fix pretends every possible continuation was seen $k$ extra times:

:::equation{#addk caption="Add-k smoothing (k = 1 is Laplace’s rule of succession)."}
$$
P_{\text{add-}k}(w \mid h) \;=\; \frac{c(h, w) + \term{k}{k}}{c(h) + k\,\term{V}{V}}
$$
:::

```terms
k:
  label: "$k$ — pseudo-count"
  what: The number of imaginary extra observations of every possible continuation.
  why: It guarantees no probability is zero. In Bayesian terms it is a symmetric Dirichlet prior with concentration $k$ — the posterior mean replaces the MLE.
  effect: Too small and rare events stay almost impossible; too large and every distribution flattens towards uniform, destroying what the counts taught us. Watch the dashed rows and the unseen-mass total in the figure below.
  param: { key: smooth.k, min: 0.001, max: 10, step: 0.001, value: 0.1, log: true }
V:
  label: "$V$ — vocabulary size"
  what: The number of possible next symbols (65 characters in TinyShakespeare).
  why: Adding $k$ to each of the $V$ counts adds $kV$ to the total, so the denominator must grow by $kV$ to keep the distribution normalised.
  effect: With a large vocabulary even a small $k$ hands a lot of mass to unseen events — add-k works poorly for words.
```

Laplace used add-one to answer “will the sun rise tomorrow?”. As a language model it is crude. Every unseen continuation gets the *same* share, whether it is a plausible `e` or an absurd `Q`, and with 65 symbols and short contexts the invented counts swamp the real ones.

::exercise{id="add-k"}

### Interpolation and backoff: ask a shorter context

A better source of information about unseen events is a *lower-order* model. We may never have seen `xyzzy` followed by `e`, but we know `e` is common in general. **Interpolation** mixes the estimates of all orders:

:::equation{#interp caption="Jelinek–Mercer interpolation, applied recursively down to a uniform distribution."}
$$
P_{\text{I}}(w \mid h) \;=\; \term{lambda}{\lambda}\, \hat P_{\text{MLE}}(w \mid h) \;+\; (1 - \lambda)\, P_{\text{I}}(w \mid \term{hp}{h'})
$$
:::

```terms
lambda:
  label: "$\\lambda$ — interpolation weight"
  what: How much to trust the longer context’s counts versus the recursively smoothed shorter one.
  why: Long contexts are specific but unreliable; short contexts are reliable but vague. Mixing them hedges.
  effect: "$\\lambda = 1$ is the unsmoothed MLE (zeros return); small $\\lambda$ leans on short contexts and loses specificity. In practice λ is tuned on validation data, and can depend on $c(h)$: trust contexts you have seen often."
  param: { key: smooth.lambda, min: 0, max: 1, step: 0.01, value: 0.8 }
hp:
  label: "$h'$ — the shortened context"
  what: The context with its oldest symbol dropped, e.g. `the q` → `he q`.
  why: The recursion bottoms out at the empty context (a unigram model), which is in turn mixed with a uniform distribution so nothing is ever zero.
```

**Backoff** (Katz, 1987) is the “if in doubt, ask” variant: use the long context when its count is reliable, and only otherwise back off to the shorter one, with weights chosen so everything still sums to 1.

### Kneser–Ney: discount, and count contexts instead of occurrences

The most successful classical method, **Kneser–Ney** smoothing :cite[kneser1995], adds two ideas.

First, **absolute discounting**. Good–Turing estimation showed that an n-gram seen $c$ times in training is expected to occur somewhat *less* than $c$ times in new text, by a roughly constant amount for $c \ge 2$. So subtract a fixed discount $d$ from every seen count, and hand the mass saved to the lower-order model:

:::equation{#kn caption="Interpolated Kneser–Ney smoothing."}
$$
P_{\text{KN}}(w \mid h) \;=\; \frac{\max\big(c(h, w) - \term{d}{d},\, 0\big)}{c(h)} \;+\; \frac{d \cdot \term{nplus}{N_{1+}(h\,\bullet)}}{c(h)}\; P_{\text{KN}}(w \mid h')
$$
:::

```terms
d:
  label: "$d$ — discount"
  what: A fixed amount subtracted from every observed n-gram count, typically 0.5–1.
  why: Seen counts overestimate future frequency — you only know about an n-gram because it happened to occur. The discount corrects that bias and funds the probability of unseen events.
  effect: "Larger $d$ moves more mass to the backoff distribution. Chen & Goodman’s *modified* Kneser–Ney uses three discounts, for counts of 1, 2 and 3+."
  param: { key: smooth.d, min: 0, max: 1, step: 0.01, value: 0.75 }
nplus:
  label: "$N_{1+}(h\\,\\bullet)$ — distinct continuations"
  what: The number of *different* symbols seen after context $h$.
  why: Each of those contributed one discount $d$, so $d \cdot N_{1+}(h\,\bullet) / c(h)$ is exactly the mass removed — and it goes to the lower order, keeping the distribution normalised.
  effect: Contexts followed by many different symbols (like a space) keep more mass for unseen continuations than contexts with one dominant successor (like `q`).
ncont:
  label: "$N_{1+}(\\bullet\\, w)$ — continuation count"
  what: The number of distinct contexts that $w$ has been seen to follow.
  why: When we back off, the question is not “how frequent is $w$?” but “how likely is $w$ to appear after a context we have not seen?” — which is better estimated by how many contexts $w$ completes.
```

Second, the lower-order distributions are built from **continuation counts** rather than raw counts:

:::equation{#pcont caption="The Kneser–Ney unigram: how many different contexts does w complete?"}
$$
P_{\text{cont}}(w) \;=\; \frac{\term{ncont}{N_{1+}(\bullet\, w)}}{N_{1+}(\bullet\,\bullet)}
$$
:::

The standard illustration uses words. *Francisco* is a common word, but it almost only appears after *San*. A plain unigram model would readily predict *Francisco* after an unseen context such as “I visited”. The continuation count sees that *Francisco* completes only one context, and gives it little backoff probability.

::exercise{id="kneser-ney"}

:::history{year=1953 title="Estimating the unseen, at Bletchley Park" people="Alan Turing (1912–1954) and I. J. Good (1916–2009)"}
Smoothing has a wartime origin. While breaking Enigma, Turing needed to estimate the probability that the next code-book page (a “species”) would be one never seen before. His insight, published after the war by his assistant Jack Good :cite[good1953], was that the total probability of unseen species is approximately the fraction of observations that were seen exactly once. More generally, something seen $c$ times should be re-estimated as if seen $(c+1)\,N_{c+1}/N_c$ times, where $N_c$ is the number of species seen $c$ times. **Good–Turing estimation** underlies Katz backoff :cite[katz1987], and its observation that discounts are roughly constant motivated absolute discounting and Kneser–Ney. In 1998 Stanley Chen and Joshua Goodman ran an exhaustive empirical comparison of smoothing methods :cite[chen1998]. Their “modified Kneser–Ney” was the method to beat until neural models arrived.
:::

## Choosing n: memorisation versus generalisation

We can now run the experiment that sums up the chapter. For each order $n$ and each estimator, train on the training split and measure cross-entropy on both splits.

::perplexity-curves

Read the curves carefully. They carry the most important lesson in machine learning, and they will reappear in every chapter:

- **Training loss always falls** as the model gets more expressive. An 8-gram model has seen every training context, so it predicts training text almost perfectly.
- **Validation loss falls, then rises**, for every estimator. Kneser–Ney holds out longest, but it too turns upwards eventually. Past some point, extra capacity is spent memorising accidents of the training data. That is **overfitting**.
- **The MLE is infinitely bad** on validation data for any $n > 1$. Smoothing is not a refinement; it is what makes the model usable at all.
- **The best character-level n-gram model reaches about 2.2 bits per character.** On the full validation split, Kneser–Ney bottoms out at 2.22 bits (perplexity 4.66) at $n = 6$, then gets slightly worse. The figure above evaluates a shorter window, which happens to be a little easier. Hold on to 2.22. It is the baseline every neural model in Parts II and III has to beat.

## N-grams in the wild

:::history{year=1975 title="Statistics beats rules: speech recognition at IBM" people="Frederick Jelinek (1932–2010) and the IBM Continuous Speech Recognition group"}
In the 1970s, most researchers built speech recognisers from hand-written linguistic rules. Fred Jelinek’s group at IBM treated recognition as noisy-channel decoding instead: choose the word sequence $w$ maximising $P(\text{audio} \mid w)\,P(w)$, with $P(w)$ supplied by a trigram language model estimated from text :cite[jelinek1997]. It worked better, and it set the template for statistical NLP for three decades. Jelinek is famously quoted as saying “every time I fire a linguist, the performance of the speech recogniser goes up” — probably apocryphal in that exact form, but an accurate summary of the era’s mood.
:::

N-gram models dominated speech recognition, machine translation and text input until the 2010s. At web scale, smoothing could be almost trivial. In 2007 a Google team trained what they called **“large language models”** on up to two *trillion* tokens :cite[brants2007]. They used a simple scheme named *Stupid Backoff*, which does not even produce normalised probabilities, and it matched Kneser–Ney once the data was large enough. That early lesson — more data can beat a cleverer method — returns with force in Chapter 17 on scaling laws.

N-gram models are still in use today. Fast implementations such as KenLM :cite[heafield2011] are used to *filter pre-training data*: pipelines like CCNet :cite[wenzek2020] keep web pages that a model trained on Wikipedia finds unsurprising, a cheap proxy for “well-written text”. Infini-gram :cite[liu2024infini] replaces fixed $n$ with suffix arrays over trillions of tokens, finding the longest context that has ever occurred. Its sorted-array design is a close cousin of the one in our library.

## The wall

Kneser–Ney plateaus at about 2.2 bits per character, far from Shannon’s estimate of around 1 bit. Two structural limitations prevent n-gram models from going further, and more data does not remove either of them.

1. **No generalisation between similar contexts.** Having seen *the cat sat on the*, an n-gram model learns nothing about *the dog sat on the*. The contexts are simply different keys in a table. Every context must be learned from its own occurrences. There is no notion that *cat* and *dog* are alike.
2. **Exponential cost of context.** Each extra symbol of context multiplies the number of possible contexts by $V$. Long-range dependencies — a character’s name from two lines earlier, an unclosed bracket, the subject of a sentence — are out of reach.

Both limitations have the same cure: represent contexts not as table keys but as **vectors**, so that similar contexts get similar predictions and a fixed number of parameters can serve an unbounded number of contexts. Yoshua Bengio and colleagues made exactly this argument in 2003, when they introduced the neural language model we build in Chapter 7 :cite[bengio2003]. First we need the machinery: tokens (Chapter 3), tensors (Chapter 4), and gradients (Chapters 5 and 6).
