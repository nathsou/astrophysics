## From ripples to a web

The cosmic microwave background (Chapter 27, [The Cosmic Microwave Background](/ch/cmb/)) is a photograph of the universe at an age of 380,000 years. Its temperature is the same in every direction to about one part in $10^5$. Today the same universe contains galaxies that are a million times denser than the cosmic average, clusters of galaxies, and enormous empty voids 50 Mpc across. Something turned tiny ripples into huge contrasts. That something is gravity, and this chapter is about how it did it.

The story has three acts, and they map neatly onto three kinds of mathematics:

1. **Statistics.** The initial ripples are random. We describe them with a *Gaussian random field* and its *power spectrum* $P(k)$, which is essentially the recipe for how much of each Fourier mode goes into the mixture.
2. **Linear growth.** While the ripples are small, each Fourier mode grows independently, at a rate set by the expansion history. This is a linear ODE that you can solve exactly.
3. **Nonlinear collapse.** Once the ripples reach order unity they collapse into sheets, filaments and bound *halos*. Now nothing is independent, the modes couple, and we need an N-body simulation.

The flagship figure of this chapter is that simulation: a real particle-mesh (PM) cosmological code, running entirely on your GPU, that starts from the ripples at redshift 50 and evolves them to today.

### Density contrast

The universe's mean matter density is $\bar\rho(t)$. It falls as $a^{-3}$, where $a$ is the scale factor (Chapter 25, [The Expanding Universe](/ch/expansion/); $a = 1$ today and $a = 1/(1+z)$ at redshift $z$). We care about departures from that mean, so we define the **density contrast**

$$
\delta(\vec x, t) = \frac{\rho(\vec x, t) - \bar\rho(t)}{\bar\rho(t)}.
$$

It is dimensionless, averages to zero, and cannot go below $-1$ (empty space) though it can go as high as it likes. A void has $\delta \approx -0.8$. The inside of a galaxy cluster sits at $\delta \sim 200$ to $1000$. The solar neighbourhood of the Milky Way is at $\delta \sim 10^6$. We use **comoving coordinates** $\vec x$ throughout: positions measured on a grid that expands with the universe, so a particle at rest relative to the Hubble flow keeps the same $\vec x$ forever. Physical distance is $a\vec x$.

<Aside>**Units.** Cosmologists quote lengths in $h^{-1}\,\text{Mpc}$, where $H_0 = 100h$ km/s/Mpc and $h \approx 0.68$. This hides the uncertainty in $H_0$ inside the unit. 1 Mpc = 3.26 million light-years, about 15 Milky Way diameters.</Aside>

## Gaussian random fields and the power spectrum

The initial density field is not a designed object. According to inflation, it was generated by quantum fluctuations stretched to cosmic size, and all measurements so far (Planck finds any non-Gaussianity parameter $f_\text{NL}$ consistent with zero) say it is a **Gaussian random field**. That phrase sounds exotic but it is a concept an engineer already knows: coloured noise.

### The engineer's version

Take a 3D grid of white noise: an independent standard normal random number in every cell. Fourier transform it. Each Fourier coefficient is now a complex Gaussian number with a random phase, and all $|k|$ have the same average power: that is what "white" means. Now multiply each coefficient by a real filter $\sqrt{P(k)}$ that depends only on $|k|$. Inverse transform. The result is a Gaussian random field with power spectrum $P(k)$.

That is exactly how the initial conditions of every cosmological simulation are made, including the one in this chapter. Three properties make it special:

- **Statistical homogeneity and isotropy.** The filter depends only on $|\vec k|$, so no place and no direction is special.
- **Random phases.** Each mode's phase is uniform and independent. All of the information is in the amplitudes, which is why a single function $P(k)$ describes the whole field.
- **Gaussianity.** A linear filter of Gaussian noise is Gaussian. Any single value $\delta(\vec x)$ is normally distributed, and so is any linear combination (such as the average over a sphere).

Formally, with Fourier modes $\delta_{\vec k}$, the power spectrum is defined by

<KeyEq title="The power spectrum">
$$
\langle \delta_{\vec k}\, \delta^*_{\vec k'} \rangle = (2\pi)^3\, \delta_D(\vec k - \vec k')\, \htmlData{term=P}{P(k)}, \qquad \htmlData{term=sigma}{\sigma^2(R)} = \int_0^\infty \frac{k^3 P(k)}{2\pi^2}\, W^2(kR)\,\frac{dk}{k}
$$
<Fragment slot="where"><span data-term="P">$P(k)$</span> has units of volume and measures the variance per Fourier mode. Different modes are uncorrelated, which is what $\delta_D$ (a Dirac delta) says. The combination $\Delta^2(k) = k^3P(k)/2\pi^2$ is the variance contributed per logarithmic interval of $k$. <span data-term="sigma">$\sigma(R)$</span> is the rms fluctuation of the field smoothed by a top-hat window $W$ of radius $R$. Its value at $R = 8\,h^{-1}$Mpc today, $\sigma_8 \approx 0.81$, is the standard way to quote the amplitude.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

A power-law spectrum $P \propto k^n$ is the simplest case, and it is worth building intuition with it. The figure below generates a 2D field on a $256^2$ grid. Small $n$ (very negative) piles power into long waves, and the field looks like smooth rolling hills. Large $n$ puts power into short waves, and the field looks like television static. Inflation predicts a primordial spectrum close to $n = 1$ in 3D, the *Harrison–Zel'dovich* spectrum (Planck measures $n_s = 0.965 \pm 0.004$). The matter spectrum today is not a power law at all: switch to the ΛCDM shape to see why.

### The shape of the matter power spectrum

Why does the ΛCDM spectrum turn over? The primordial spectrum from inflation is almost exactly $P_\text{prim} \propto k^{n_s}$ with $n_s \approx 1$. What we see after the universe has processed it is

$$
P(k, a) = A\,k^{n_s}\,T^2(k)\,D^2(a),
$$

where $D(a)$ is the growth factor (next section) and $T(k)$ is the **transfer function**. $T(k) = 1$ for long waves and falls off for short ones. The reason is timing. A perturbation of wavelength $\lambda$ can only grow once it fits inside the horizon, the distance light has travelled since the Big Bang. Short waves enter the horizon early, while the universe is still dominated by radiation. As we will see, in that era dark-matter perturbations barely grow. Long waves enter after matter–radiation equality at $z_\text{eq} \approx 3400$ and grow at the full rate from the start. The dividing line is the horizon scale at equality:

$$
k_\text{eq} \approx 0.073\,\Omega_m h^2\ \text{Mpc}^{-1} \approx 0.010\ \text{Mpc}^{-1},
$$

a wavelength of about 600 Mpc. Modes with $k \gg k_\text{eq}$ are suppressed by roughly $(k_\text{eq}/k)^2$ in amplitude, up to a logarithm, so $P \propto k^{n_s - 4}\ln^2 k$ on small scales. The simulation uses the classic Bardeen–Bond–Kaiser–Szalay (1986) fit for $T(k)$, a rational function of $q = k/\Gamma$ with $\Gamma \approx \Omega_m h$.

<Aside>**Why "ΛCDM-like".** The BBKS fit ignores baryons. Real transfer functions are computed with Boltzmann codes such as CAMB and CLASS, which solve the coupled photon, neutrino, baryon and dark-matter equations to 0.1% precision.</Aside>

### Baryon acoustic oscillations

Baryons add a small but famous feature. Before recombination the baryons were locked to the photons in a single fluid, and pressure made that fluid ring with sound waves: the same acoustic oscillations that give the CMB its peaks (Chapter 27). When the photons decoupled at $z \approx 1090$, each sound wave froze where it was. The result is a preferred separation, the **sound horizon** at the drag epoch, $r_d \approx 147$ Mpc. Galaxies are slightly more likely (by about 1%) to be found at that separation from each other than at 130 or 170 Mpc.

In $P(k)$ this shows up as small wiggles with period $\Delta k = 2\pi/r_d$. In the correlation function, which is the Fourier transform of $P(k)$, it is a single bump at 147 Mpc. Because $r_d$ is known from CMB physics, the BAO bump is a **standard ruler**. Measure its angular size at various redshifts and you have a distance–redshift relation. That is how the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) measures the expansion history. Its 2024 and 2025 releases, from tens of millions of redshifts, found BAO distances that, combined with the CMB and supernovae, prefer a dark energy that weakens with time at 2.8 to 4.2σ. Whether that holds up is one of the big questions of the late 2020s.

## Linear growth in an expanding universe

Now the dynamics. Treat the matter as a pressureless fluid (cold dark matter has negligible pressure on any scale we care about). In comoving coordinates, and to first order in $\delta$ and in the peculiar velocity, three equations govern it: continuity, Euler, and Poisson. Taking the time derivative of the linearised continuity equation and substituting the others gives a single equation for $\delta$. We skip the ten lines of algebra; any cosmology text has them.

<KeyEq title="Linear growth of density perturbations">
$$
\ddot{\htmlData{term=delta}{\delta}} + 2\htmlData{term=H}{H}\,\dot\delta = 4\pi G\,\htmlData{term=rhobar}{\bar\rho}\,\delta
$$
<Fragment slot="where"><span data-term="delta">$\delta$</span> is the density contrast of one Fourier mode (in linear theory each mode evolves independently, with the same equation for all $k$ larger than the horizon scale). <span data-term="H">$H = \dot a/a$</span> is the Hubble rate. <span data-term="rhobar">$\bar\rho$</span> is the mean matter density. The right-hand side is gravity pulling matter into overdensities; the $2H\dot\delta$ term is **Hubble drag**, the expansion stretching things apart.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

This is a damped harmonic oscillator with the wrong sign of the spring constant, so it has a growing and a decaying solution. It also tells us something striking: because the equation does not contain $k$, **every mode grows by the same factor**. We can write $\delta(\vec x, t) = D(t)\,\delta_0(\vec x)$, and the growth factor $D(t)$ carries the whole time dependence. The *shape* of $P(k)$ does not change in linear theory; only its amplitude does, as $D^2$.

### The matter-dominated universe: δ ∝ a

In a flat universe of pure matter (the Einstein–de Sitter model), $a \propto t^{2/3}$, $H = 2/3t$ and $4\pi G\bar\rho = \tfrac32 H^2 = 2/3t^2$. Try $\delta \propto t^p$:

$$
p(p-1) + \tfrac43 p = \tfrac23 \quad\Rightarrow\quad p = \tfrac23 \ \text{or}\ p = -1.
$$

The decaying mode $t^{-1}$ disappears quickly. The growing mode is $\delta \propto t^{2/3} \propto a$. **Perturbations grow in proportion to the scale factor.** This is a lot slower than the exponential growth of the Jeans instability in a static medium, because Hubble drag fights gravity. In a static universe an overdensity grows as $e^{t/t_\text{ff}}$; in an expanding one it only grows as a power law.

<Fermi title="Why we need dark matter">
The CMB temperature fluctuations are $\Delta T/T \sim 10^{-5}$, and the baryons at recombination had $\delta_b \sim 10^{-4}$ or so on galaxy scales. Between recombination ($a \approx 1/1100$) and today, linear theory grows perturbations by a factor of at most $\sim 1100$, and by less (about $0.78\times$) once Λ is included:

$$\delta_\text{today} \sim 10^{-4} \times 1100 \times 0.8 \approx 0.1.$$

That is not $\gg 1$. Without help, no galaxies would have formed yet. The help is dark matter: it does not interact with photons, so it started growing at matter–radiation equality ($z \approx 3400$), three times earlier, and it was not wiped out by photon pressure on small scales. At recombination the baryons simply fell into dark-matter wells that were already $\sim 10^{-2}$ deep. The fact that we exist is some of the strongest evidence for dark matter (see Chapter 21, [The Milky Way & Dark Matter](/ch/milky-way/)).
</Fermi>

### The Mészáros effect

In the radiation era the right-hand side of the growth equation contains only the dark-matter density, but $H$ is set by the much larger radiation density. Gravity is weak compared with Hubble drag, and the solution is $\delta \propto 1 + \tfrac32 a/a_\text{eq}$: from horizon entry until equality, a dark-matter perturbation grows by at most a factor 2.5, logarithmically. Péter Mészáros found this in 1974. It is the physical origin of the turnover in $P(k)$ at $k_\text{eq}$: all modes that entered the horizon during the radiation era were frozen for a while, and the earlier they entered, the longer they were frozen.

### Λ stops the growth

Add a cosmological constant. As Λ comes to dominate, $H$ tends to a constant and $\bar\rho$ keeps falling as $a^{-3}$. Hubble drag wins, and $\delta$ freezes. For ΛCDM the growth factor today is about 78% of what it would be in a pure-matter universe, and it will grow by only about 40% more, ever. The growth rate $f = d\ln D/d\ln a$ is well approximated by $f \approx \Omega_m(a)^{0.55}$ in general relativity. That exponent, 0.55, is a prediction of GR: modified-gravity theories that mimic dark energy usually change it. DESI, Euclid and Rubin measure $f\sigma_8$ through redshift-space distortions and weak lensing precisely to test it.

## When linear theory breaks: collapse

Linear theory fails when $\delta \sim 1$. Two classic analytic models tell us what happens next, and they bracket the truth nicely: one follows a single spherical lump, the other follows the flows of a whole random field.

### Spherical top-hat collapse

Take a uniform sphere with a slight overdensity in an otherwise uniform Einstein–de Sitter universe. By Newton's shell theorem (and Birkhoff's theorem in GR), the sphere evolves exactly like a small closed universe. Its radius obeys $\ddot R = -GM/R^2$, which is the Kepler problem in one dimension (Chapter 2, [Gravity & Orbits](/ch/orbits/)), with the parametric *cycloid* solution

$$
R = A(1 - \cos\theta), \qquad t = B(\theta - \sin\theta), \qquad A^3 = GMB^2.
$$

The sphere expands, slows, turns around at $\theta = \pi$, and collapses to a point at $\theta = 2\pi$. The background sphere of the same mass keeps growing as $t^{2/3}$, and the ratio of their volumes gives the nonlinear density contrast. Expand everything for small $\theta$ and you get linear theory, which lets us translate between the two. Three numbers come out of this and are used everywhere:

- **Turnaround** at $\delta = 9\pi^2/16 - 1 \approx 4.55$, when linear theory says $\delta_\text{lin} \approx 1.06$.
- **Collapse** when linear theory says $\delta_\text{lin} = \tfrac{3}{20}(12\pi)^{2/3} \approx 1.686$. This is $\delta_c$, the **critical overdensity**.
- **Virialisation.** Real lumps are not perfect spheres and do not collapse to a point. Particles cross, exchange energy and settle into equilibrium with $2K + W = 0$. Energy conservation then gives a final radius of half the turnaround radius. Compared with the background at the moment of collapse, the halo is $\Delta_\text{vir} = 18\pi^2 \approx 178$ times denser than average.

That is where the ubiquitous "200" comes from: a halo is conventionally defined as the sphere within which the mean density is 200 times the critical (or mean) density. In ΛCDM the exact virial overdensity depends a little on $\Omega_m$ and is about 100 relative to critical today, but 200 has stuck.

The powerful idea here is that we can predict *nonlinear* collapse using *linear* theory: a region collapses into a halo when its linearly extrapolated overdensity reaches $\delta_c = 1.686$. It does not matter that linear theory is badly wrong by then; it is used only as a clock.

<Aside>**The same number in ΛCDM.** With Λ, $\delta_c$ changes only from 1.686 to about 1.675. The threshold is remarkably insensitive to cosmology, because the collapse happens mostly while matter dominates the local dynamics.</Aside>

### The Zel'dovich approximation

Real overdensities are not spherical. Yakov Zel'dovich (1970) found an approximation that captures their shape. Instead of tracking density, track particles. Every particle starts at a Lagrangian position $\vec q$ and moves along a straight line:

<KeyEq title="The Zel'dovich approximation">
$$
\vec x(\vec q, t) = \vec q + D(t)\,\htmlData{term=psi}{\vec\psi}(\vec q), \qquad \nabla_q\cdot\vec\psi = -\delta_0(\vec q)
$$
<Fragment slot="where"><span data-term="psi">$\vec\psi$</span> is the displacement field, fixed at the start; $D(t)$ is the linear growth factor. In Fourier space $\vec\psi_{\vec k} = i\vec k\,\delta_{\vec k}/k^2$. The density follows from mass conservation, $1+\delta = 1/\det(\mathbb 1 + D\,\partial\vec\psi/\partial\vec q)$.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

To first order this is exactly linear theory. But particles keep moving along their straight lines even after $\delta$ becomes large, and the determinant tells you what happens. The deformation tensor $\partial\psi_i/\partial q_j$ is a symmetric $3\times3$ matrix with eigenvalues $-\lambda_1 \le -\lambda_2 \le -\lambda_3$. The density is

$$
1 + \delta = \frac{1}{(1 - D\lambda_1)(1 - D\lambda_2)(1 - D\lambda_3)}.
$$

The largest eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ reaches $1/D$ first, and the density becomes infinite along a surface: a **pancake** (a wall, or sheet). Collapse is generically one-dimensional first. Where two eigenvalues are large, you get a **filament**. Where all three, a **node**, the site of a massive halo. For a Gaussian field, the chance that all three eigenvalues of a random point are positive is only 8%; 42% have exactly one and another 42% exactly two. That is why the cosmic web is dominated by walls and filaments, with rare dense knots at their intersections, and why most of the volume ends up as voids.

When particles cross (*shell crossing*), the single-valued velocity field breaks down. In phase space the particle sheet *folds*. Three streams overlap in the same place, and the edges of the fold are caustics with formally infinite density. The 1D figure shows this.

<Sim name="structure-zeldovich" minHeight={440}>

Each particle's velocity is proportional to $\psi$, so the sheet is initially a wiggly line. Press play or drag $D$. Beyond that the sheet folds into an S, the region turns red (three streams), and caustics bound it. In 3D these folds are the walls and filaments of the cosmic web.
</Sim>

Zel'dovich keeps moving particles in straight lines after shell crossing, so pancakes overshoot and dissolve instead of staying bound. Real gravity pulls the streams back and they wind up into halos. But the approximation is excellent up to shell crossing, which is why simulations (including ours) use it to set up initial conditions at high redshift. Modern codes use second-order Lagrangian perturbation theory (2LPT) or higher to start later with smaller transients.

## Halos: the building blocks

After collapse and virialisation, dark matter settles into gravitationally bound, roughly spherical **halos**. Galaxies form at their centres, as gas cools and falls in (Chapter 23, [Galaxy Formation & AGN](/ch/galaxy-formation/)). So counting halos is the first step to counting galaxies.

### How many halos? The Press–Schechter idea

Press and Schechter (1974) combined the two results above into a formula for the abundance of halos. Smooth the linear density field on a sphere of mass $M$ (radius $R$ with $M = \tfrac43\pi R^3\bar\rho$). The smoothed field is Gaussian with variance $\sigma^2(M)$. Every region where it exceeds $\delta_c/D(t)$ has collapsed into a halo of mass at least $M$. So the fraction of mass in halos above $M$ is a Gaussian tail probability:

$$
F(>M) = 2\times\tfrac12\,\text{erfc}\!\left(\frac{\nu}{\sqrt2}\right), \qquad \nu = \frac{\delta_c}{D(t)\,\sigma(M)}.
$$

The factor of 2 was a famous fudge that was later justified by the *excursion set* argument (Bond et al. 1991). Differentiating gives the **halo mass function** $dn/dM$. Its shape is a power law at low mass, where $\sigma(M)$ is large and nearly everything has collapsed, and an exponential cutoff $e^{-\nu^2/2}$ at high mass. The cutoff mass $M_*$, where $\nu = 1$, is about $10^{13}\,M_\odot$ today: groups of galaxies. It was far smaller in the past, which is **hierarchical structure formation**: small halos form first and merge into bigger ones. Rich clusters of $10^{15}\,M_\odot$ are $\nu \approx 3$ peaks, and there are only a few thousand of them in the observable universe. Their exponentially sensitive abundance makes them a sharp probe of $\sigma_8$.

<Aside>**Refinements.** Sheth & Tormen (1999) improved the formula by allowing ellipsoidal collapse. Modern analyses use mass functions calibrated on large simulations (Tinker et al. 2008; emulators such as Aemulus) to about 1% accuracy.</Aside>

### The NFW profile

Simulated halos have a nearly universal density profile, found by Navarro, Frenk and White (1996):

$$
\rho(r) = \frac{\rho_s}{(r/r_s)(1 + r/r_s)^2}.
$$

It falls as $r^{-1}$ in the inner parts (a *cusp*) and as $r^{-3}$ in the outskirts. The ratio $c = R_{200}/r_s$ is the **concentration**, about 10 for a Milky Way–sized halo and about 4 for a cluster. Smaller halos formed earlier, when the universe was denser, and are more concentrated. Why the profile is universal is still not fully understood; it is an empirical result of simulations, confirmed by lensing (Chapter 24) and X-ray observations of clusters.

The size of a halo follows from its definition. A halo of <Var name="Mh" value={1.3e12} min={1e8} max={3e15} log /> M☉ has $R_{200} = $ <Out name="r200" /> and a circular velocity at that radius of <Out name="v200" />. That is a Milky Way–like halo at $10^{12}$, a dwarf galaxy at $10^{9}$–$10^{10}$, and a Coma-like cluster at $10^{15}$. Since $M \propto R^3$ at fixed density, $V_{200} \propto M^{1/3}$: a cluster is a thousand times more massive than the Milky Way but only ten times faster.

### Cold versus warm dark matter

"Cold" means the dark-matter particles moved slowly when structure began to form. If instead they were **warm**, for example a thermal relic with a mass of a few keV, their random motions would have let them stream out of small overdensities (*free streaming*), erasing structure below a scale of

$$
\lambda_\text{fs} \sim 0.1\,\text{Mpc}\,\left(\frac{m}{1\,\text{keV}}\right)^{-4/3}.
$$

The power spectrum is cut off exponentially above a wavenumber $\sim 1/\lambda_\text{fs}$. Halos smaller than the corresponding mass never form by gravitational collapse at all, and filaments become smooth rather than beaded with small halos. WDM was proposed partly to fix apparent small-scale problems of CDM (too many satellite galaxies, cusps rather than cores). Today the Lyman-α forest, the counts of Milky Way satellites, and strong-lensing anomalies all require $m \gtrsim 5$–$6$ keV for a thermal relic, which is almost indistinguishable from CDM on scales above a dwarf galaxy. The flagship simulation offers two extreme "toy" WDM masses, 0.1 and 0.05 keV, long ruled out, so that the cutoff falls where the simulation can resolve it. Try them with the same seed as CDM.

## Simulating the cosmic web

The linear and spherical calculations take us to the edge of collapse. Beyond it, only simulation will do. The equations are simple: $N$ particles, each representing a huge number of dark-matter particles, moving under their mutual gravity in an expanding box with periodic boundaries.

### Equations in comoving coordinates

With comoving position $\vec x$ and the *canonical momentum* $\vec p = a^2\,d\vec x/dt$, Newtonian gravity in an expanding background becomes

<KeyEq title="Comoving equations of motion">
$$
\frac{d\vec x}{da} = \frac{\vec p}{a^3 H(a)}, \qquad \frac{d\vec p}{da} = -\frac{\nabla\htmlData{term=phi}{\phi}}{a\,H(a)}, \qquad \nabla^2\phi = \frac{3}{2}\frac{\Omega_m H_0^2}{a}\,\delta
$$
<Fragment slot="where">$\vec x$ is the comoving position, $\vec p$ the canonical momentum (peculiar velocity is $\vec p/a$), $H(a) = H_0\sqrt{\Omega_m a^{-3} + \Omega_\Lambda}$ the Hubble rate, and <span data-term="phi">$\phi$</span> the peculiar gravitational potential sourced only by the density *contrast*: the uniform background drives the expansion and is already accounted for in $a(t)$. Using $a$ as the clock avoids solving for $t$ at all.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

We integrate these with the kick–drift–kick leapfrog from Chapter 2, with $a$ as the time variable: half a kick, a full drift, a new force, half a kick. The kick and drift factors are the integrals $\int da/(aH)$ and $\int da/(a^3H)$ over the step, computed exactly on the CPU. Steps are uniform in $\sqrt a$: large at early times when nothing much happens, small near today when halos are dense.

### The particle-mesh method

Direct summation costs $O(N^2)$: for two million particles that is $4\times10^{12}$ interactions per step. The **particle-mesh (PM)** method replaces it with a density grid and a Fourier transform. Each step:

1. **Assign mass to the mesh.** Spread each particle over the 8 nearest grid points with cloud-in-cell (CIC) weights.
2. **Forward FFT** the density contrast.
3. **Solve Poisson in Fourier space**: $\phi_{\vec k} = -\tfrac32\Omega_m H_0^2\,\delta_{\vec k}/(a k^2)$. Differentiation becomes multiplication.
4. **Inverse FFT** to get $\phi$ on the mesh, and difference it to get the acceleration.
5. **Interpolate** the acceleration back to each particle with the same CIC weights, and kick.

Periodic boundaries come for free, because the discrete Fourier transform is periodic.

Around $z \sim 5$ to 3 for this box, with filaments sharpening down to $z \approx 1$. Between $z = 50$ and $z = 10$ the growth factor increases fivefold, but $\delta$ on the scales the grid resolves is still much less than 1, so nothing visibly moves. The action is back-loaded: half of all the growth in $D$ happens after $z \approx 1$. Then Λ takes over and the web nearly freezes, so the last few gigayears look calm. Watch the redshift counter.

<Hood title="a particle-mesh code in compute shaders">
Every stage of the simulation is a WGSL compute kernel, and the particle data never leaves the GPU. Per step there are 13 dispatches in two command buffers: kick and drift; clear, deposit and convert the density; three FFT passes; Poisson; three inverse FFT passes; gradient; kick.

**Cloud-in-cell deposit with integer atomics.** Thousands of particles can hit the same cell, so the scatter needs atomic addition. WGSL only has integer atomics, so the mass is stored in fixed point: each particle has $2^{15}$ units, split between its 8 neighbouring cells with trilinear weights. Integer addition is associative, so the result is also bit-for-bit deterministic, which float atomics would not be.

```wgsl
let f0 = floor(x);  let f = x - f0;  let b = vec3u(f0);
for (var o = 0u; o < 8u; o++) {
  let d  = vec3u(o & 1u, (o >> 1u) & 1u, o >> 2u);
  let w3 = select(1.0 - f, f, d == vec3u(1u));
  atomicAdd(&rho[idx(b + d)], u32(w3.x * w3.y * w3.z * P.mscale + 0.5));
}
```

**The FFT.** A 3D FFT is three passes of 1D FFTs, one along each axis. Each workgroup loads one line of 64 or 128 complex numbers into workgroup memory and runs a radix-2 *Stockham* FFT there: $\log_2 N$ butterfly stages, ping-ponging between two halves of a shared array. Stockham writes its output in natural order, so no bit-reversal pass is needed.

```wgsl
for (var s = 0u; s < LOG; s++) {
  let dst = N - src;  let k = j % ns;
  let ang = U.sign * PI * f32(k) / f32(ns);
  let v0 = S[src + j];
  let v1 = cmul(S[src + j + H], vec2f(cos(ang), sin(ang)));
  let d = (j / ns) * ns * 2u + k;
  S[dst + d] = v0 + v1;  S[dst + d + ns] = v0 - v1;
  workgroupBarrier();  src = dst;  ns *= 2u;
}
```

Each pass reads and writes the whole grid once, so a 3D FFT of $128^3$ complex values moves about 100 MB, around 1–2 ms on an M1-class GPU.

**Green's function and deconvolution.** In Fourier space the Poisson solve is one multiply per mode. CIC assignment smooths the density with a window $W(\vec k) = \prod_i \text{sinc}^2(k_i\Delta/2)$, and CIC interpolation applies it again, so we divide by $W^2$ to undo both. That amplifies noise near the Nyquist frequency, so a small Gaussian filter $e^{-k^2 r_s^2}$ with $r_s = 0.5$ cells tames it. The gradient is then taken with a 4-point finite difference in real space, which needs one inverse FFT instead of three.

```wgsl
let s = vec3f(sinc(0.5 * k.x), sinc(0.5 * k.y), sinc(0.5 * k.z));
let w = s.x * s.x * s.y * s.y * s.z * s.z;          // CIC window
let green = -P.pois / (k2 * w * w) * exp(-k2 * P.rsm * P.rsm);
cx[i] = cx[i] * green;
```

**Force resolution.** The PM force is accurate for separations of more than about 2–3 cells and softened below that: $2.5$ $h^{-1}$Mpc at $64^3$ and 1.25 at $128^3$ in this box. That is enough for the web but not for the inside of halos, which appear as fuzzy blobs a couple of cells wide. Production codes add a short-range correction: *P³M* sums nearby pairs directly, and *TreePM* (Gadget, used for Millennium and IllustrisTNG) uses a Barnes–Hut tree for the short-range part. Using the same CIC kernel for deposit and interpolation guarantees that a particle exerts no net force on itself, and momentum is conserved to rounding error.

**Complexity.** Deposit and interpolation are $O(N)$ in the number of particles, and the FFTs are $O(M\log M)$ in the number of mesh cells, so a step costs $O(N + M\log M)$ instead of $O(N^2)$. At $N = M = 128^3$ that is about $4\times10^7$ operations versus $4\times10^{12}$.

**Precision.** Everything is f32. Positions are stored in mesh-cell units, between 0 and 128, so the rounding error is about $10^{-5}$ cells, far below the force resolution. Time is in units of $1/H_0$ and momenta are $O(1)$–$O(10)$ in cells times $H_0$, so no quantity strays far from unity. The initial conditions are generated on the GPU from hashed Gaussian noise, coloured by $\sqrt{P(k)}$, and turned into Zel'dovich displacements $\vec\psi_{\vec k} = i\vec k\delta_{\vec k}/k^2$ with three more inverse FFTs. Leapfrog in $a$ is symplectic only approximately, since the equations have explicit time dependence, but it is second order and time-reversible, and energy is not conserved in an expanding universe anyway (the Layzer–Irvine equation replaces it).
</Hood>

### The real thing

The largest simulations are the same algorithm scaled up by a factor of a million, with tree or fast-multipole short-range forces and many thousands of GPUs:

- **Millennium** (2005): $2160^3 \approx 10^{10}$ particles in a 500 $h^{-1}$Mpc box. It made semi-analytic galaxy catalogues mainstream.
- **IllustrisTNG** (2018): boxes of 35 to 205 $h^{-1}$Mpc with gas, star formation, black holes and magnetic fields, following baryons as well as dark matter (Chapter 23).
- **Uchuu** (2021): $12800^3 = 2.1$ trillion particles in a 2 $h^{-1}$Gpc box.
- **Euclid Flagship 2** (2024): about 4.1 trillion particles in a 3.6 $h^{-1}$Gpc box, run with the PKDGRAV3 code to make mock galaxy catalogues for the Euclid mission.

<Aside>**Particles are not dark-matter particles.** A simulation particle of $10^9\,M_\odot$ is a tracer of the phase-space distribution of perhaps $10^{76}$ real particles. Two-body encounters between simulation particles are an artefact, suppressed by softening.</Aside>

## The cosmic web observed

We can compare simulations with the real web because galaxy redshift surveys map it in 3D. The 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey and the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS, 2000 onwards) measured about a million redshifts and revealed the web directly: the Sloan Great Wall (about 400 Mpc long), large voids, and the "fingers of God" produced by cluster velocity dispersions. SDSS's BOSS and eBOSS programs made the first precise BAO measurements.

**DESI** (on the 4-m Mayall telescope since 2021) takes 5,000 spectra at a time with robotic fibre positioners. By 2025 it had tens of millions of redshifts, making the largest 3D map of the universe to date. **Euclid** (ESA, launched 2023) is imaging a third of the sky to measure weak lensing (Chapter 24) and tens of millions of spectroscopic redshifts; its first quick data release came in 2025. The **Vera C. Rubin Observatory** began its ten-year LSST survey in 2025 and will measure the shapes of billions of galaxies.

These surveys measure the same statistics we have used in this chapter: the galaxy power spectrum and its BAO wiggles, the growth rate $f\sigma_8$ from redshift-space distortions, and the lensing amplitude.

### σ8 and the S8 tension

Weak-lensing surveys are most sensitive to the combination $S_8 = \sigma_8\sqrt{\Omega_m/0.3}$. Planck's CMB, extrapolated forward with ΛCDM, predicts $S_8 \approx 0.83$. Lensing surveys (KiDS, DES, HSC) measured lower values, around 0.76 to 0.78, a 2–3σ discrepancy called the **S8 tension**. If real, it would mean structure grew less than ΛCDM predicts: new physics in dark matter or dark energy, or massive neutrinos, or simply stronger baryonic feedback than assumed, since AGN winds push matter out of halos and suppress small-scale power. Recent analyses (KiDS-Legacy in 2025, and combined DES+KiDS) have moved closer to Planck, and the tension now looks more like a mild $\lesssim 2\sigma$ difference. Euclid and Rubin should settle it.

## Where we go from here

The picture in this chapter is the standard model of structure formation. A nearly scale-invariant spectrum of Gaussian ripples from inflation, processed by the transfer function, grows linearly as $D(a)$, collapses into walls, filaments and halos when $\delta$ reaches order unity, and builds up hierarchically from small halos to clusters. Each piece has been tested: the CMB fixes the initial conditions, BAO and redshift surveys measure the growth, lensing weighs the halos, and simulations with trillions of particles connect them all. The same web appears on your screen from a few hundred lines of WGSL.

Some open questions:

- **What is dark matter?** Its small-scale behaviour (cold, warm, fuzzy, self-interacting) shows up in the smallest halos and in the inner profiles of dwarf galaxies.
- **What is dark energy?** DESI's hint of evolving dark energy will be tested by DESI's full survey, Euclid and Rubin within a few years.
- **Baryons.** Gas physics and feedback reshape the matter distribution on scales below a few Mpc, and modelling them is now the main systematic uncertainty in lensing cosmology.

This is the last chapter of the course. We started with Newton's inverse-square law and a leapfrog integrator for one planet. We end with the same law and the same integrator, applied to two million particles in an expanding universe. The physics of the cosmos is, to a remarkable extent, the same few ideas used over and over at different scales.
