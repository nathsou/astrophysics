## When pressure loses

<Photo src="/img/black-holes/m87-eht.jpg" alt="A glowing orange ring, brighter at the bottom, around a dark centre: the first image of a black hole's shadow, in M87" credit="EHT Collaboration" license="CC BY 4.0" href="https://www.eso.org/public/images/eso1907a/" size="wide" aspect="1280 / 746">
**The first picture of a black hole.** The shadow of M87\*, 6.5 billion solar masses at the heart of the galaxy M87, 55 million light-years away. The Event Horizon Telescope made it by combining radio dishes across the planet at 1.3 mm wavelength. The ring is 42 microarcseconds across: the size of an orange on the Moon. The dark centre is the shadow we derive in this chapter, and the brighter bottom is hot gas moving towards us.
</Photo>

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
Stars hold themselves up with pressure, the push of hot, crowded matter. [Chapter 16](/ch/stellar-death/) followed that push to its limits. The dead core of a star can be held up by crowded electrons (a white dwarf) or, if it is heavier, by crowded neutrons (a neutron star). But above roughly two and a half Suns' worth of mass, nothing known can hold it.

Worse, in Einstein's gravity pressure itself has weight. Squeeze harder to resist collapse, and the extra pressure pulls inward too. Past a certain point the fight is lost for good, and the core collapses into a region from which nothing, not even light, can get out: a **black hole**.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">

[Chapter 16](/ch/stellar-death/) ended with a stellar core that no pressure could hold up. The story goes like this. A white dwarf is supported by electron degeneracy pressure, which fails above the Chandrasekhar mass of about 1.4 M☉. A neutron star is supported by neutron degeneracy plus the repulsive core of the strong force. That fails too, somewhere around 2.2–2.5 M☉. This is the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit, and its exact value depends on the poorly known equation of state of nuclear matter ([Chapter 18](/ch/neutron-stars/)).

The deeper reason for this failure is worth understanding, because it is general relativistic. Hydrostatic equilibrium needs a pressure gradient to balance gravity. In Newtonian physics, only mass density $\rho$ gravitates. In general relativity (GR), **pressure gravitates too**: the source of gravity is roughly $\rho + 3P/c^2$. As a star is compressed, the extra pressure it needs adds to its weight, and so it needs still more pressure. Beyond a critical compactness no equation of state can win, even the stiffest one that causality allows (sound speed = $c$). Buchdahl proved in 1959 that no static fluid ball can be smaller than $\tfrac98$ of the radius we are about to derive. So a core heavier than about 2.5 M☉ **has to** collapse, and nothing known can stop it.

</Fragment>
</Tiers>

<Aside>**Failed supernovae.** Not every black hole needs an explosion. Some massive stars seem simply to wink out. A 25 M☉ supergiant in NGC 6946 brightened slightly in 2009 and then vanished in the optical. JWST now sees only a faint infrared glow at its position. This is the best candidate so far for a direct collapse to a black hole.</Aside>

<History title="Dark stars, twice forgotten" year="1783">
<Portrait src="/img/people/laplace.jpg" name="Pierre-Simon Laplace" years="1749–1827" credit="Paulin Guérin" license="Public domain" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pierre-Simon_Laplace.jpg" />

In November 1783 the English clergyman John Michell read a paper to the Royal Society. If light were made of particles slowed by gravity, he argued, a star 500 times the Sun's radius with the Sun's density would hold its light in. It would be invisible, detectable only by the orbits of companions around it. That is exactly how Cygnus X-1 and Sgr A\* were found two centuries later. Pierre-Simon Laplace reached the same idea independently and put it in the first two editions of his *Exposition du système du monde* (1796). Then he dropped it: once Thomas Young showed around 1801 that light behaves as a wave, a "corpuscle" slowed by gravity no longer made sense. The idea slept for more than a century, until general relativity gave it a new and much stranger meaning.
</History>

### An escape-velocity heuristic

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
Throw a ball upward hard enough and it never comes back: that speed is the **escape speed** (a refresher on energy: [Appendix A6](/ch/primer-mechanics/)). It is 11 km/s for the Earth and 618 km/s from the Sun's surface. Squash a body smaller while keeping its mass, and its escape speed grows. At a certain size it reaches the speed of light. In 1783 John Michell guessed that such a "dark star" would be invisible. His reasoning was off, but his size was exactly right. It is called the **Schwarzschild radius**, and it is about 3 km for every solar mass:
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">

Here is a surprisingly good Newtonian guess. In Chapter 2's vis-viva equation, the escape speed from the surface of a mass $M$ of radius $R$ is $v_\text{esc}=\sqrt{2GM/R}$. Set $v_\text{esc}=c$ and solve for $R$:

$$
\term{R}{R} = \frac{2\term{G}{G}\term{M}{M}}{\term{c}{c}^2}.
$$

John Michell (1783) and Pierre-Simon Laplace (1796) did exactly this and imagined "dark stars". Their reasoning is wrong in two ways. Light has no rest mass for Newtonian gravity to act on, and in their picture light could still climb partway up and fall back. Yet the answer is exactly right. It is the **Schwarzschild radius**:

</Fragment>
</Tiers>

<KeyEq title="The Schwarzschild radius">
$$
\term{rs}{r_s} = \frac{2\term{G}{G}\term{M}{M}}{\term{c}{c}^2} \approx 2.95\ \text{km}\,\left(\frac{\term{M}{M}}{\term{Msun}{\text{M}_\odot}}\right)
$$
<Fragment slot="where"><span data-term="rs">$r_s$</span> is the radius of the event horizon of a non-rotating black hole of mass <span data-term="M">$M$</span>. $G$ is <C k="G" /> and $c$ is <C k="c" />. It scales **linearly** with mass, so the mean density inside the horizon scales as $M^{-2}$.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

<Aside>**Scale check.** Squeezed to its Schwarzschild radius, the Earth would be a black hole 9 mm across, about the size of a marble. The Sun would be 5.9 km across.</Aside>

A black hole of <Var name="Mbh" value={10} min={1e-9} max={1e11} log /> M☉ has $r_s =$ <Out name="rs" />, and a mean density inside the horizon of <Out name="rho" />. For a 10 M☉ stellar remnant, that density is far above nuclear density ($2\times10^{17}$ kg/m³). Drag the mass up to the $6.5\times10^9$ M☉ of M87\*. Its "density" is then less than that of air. Supermassive black holes do not need extreme densities to form, only a lot of mass in one place.

<Aside>**The name.** Physicists called them "frozen stars" or "collapsed stars" until John Wheeler popularised "black hole" in a 1967 lecture, after someone in the audience suggested it.</Aside>



## The Schwarzschild metric

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
Einstein replaced Newton's force of gravity with *geometry*. Mass bends space and time, and objects that feel no force simply follow the straightest paths through that bent spacetime. Around a single ball of mass the bending was worked out in 1915 by Karl Schwarzschild. His answer says three things in plain words:

- **Clocks run slow deep down.** A clock close to the hole ticks slower than one far away. At twice the Schwarzschild radius it runs at 71% of the far-away rate; at the horizon it would appear to stop altogether.
- **Space is stretched.** Walking from one circle around the hole to the next takes more steps than their circumferences would suggest, like the steep inside of a funnel.
- **The horizon is a one-way door.** A traveller falling in feels nothing special when crossing it. But once inside, every possible future leads inward, as unavoidably as tomorrow follows today.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">
Einstein replaced Newton's force of gravity with geometry: mass curves space and time, and free objects follow the straightest possible paths. Outside a non-rotating mass the answer, found by Karl Schwarzschild in 1915, boils down to one number at each radius $r$:

$$
\sqrt{1-\frac{\term{rs}{r_s}}{\term{r}{r}}} .
$$

- **Clocks:** a clock hovering at radius $r$ ticks at $\sqrt{1-r_s/r}$ times the rate of a far-away clock. At $r = 2r_s$ that is $\sqrt{0.5}=0.71$.
- **Rulers:** the true distance between two nearby circles is *larger* than the difference of their radii, by $1/\sqrt{1-r_s/r}$. Space is stretched like a funnel.
- **Far away** ($r \gg r_s$) the factor is 1, and ordinary flat space returns.

At $r = r_s$ the clock factor is zero: the **event horizon**. Someone falling in crosses it without noticing anything local, but nothing inside can ever come back out. Every path inside leads to the centre.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l3">

Newtonian gravity is a force. General relativity replaces it with geometry. Spacetime is curved, freely falling bodies follow the straightest possible paths (**geodesics**), and the curvature is set by the mass-energy present through Einstein's field equations. We won't solve those here. We only need their answer for the simplest case.

In 1915, only weeks after Einstein published the field equations, Karl Schwarzschild found the exact solution outside any spherically symmetric, non-rotating mass. He did it while serving on the Russian front. The solution is summarised by the **line element**, which gives the spacetime interval $ds$ between two nearby events:

<KeyEq title="The Schwarzschild metric">
$$
\term{ds}{ds}^2 = -\left(1-\frac{\term{rs}{r_s}}{\term{r}{r}}\right)\term{c}{c}^2\,d\term{t}{t}^2 + \left(1-\frac{\term{rs}{r_s}}{\term{r}{r}}\right)^{-1} d\term{r}{r}^2 + \term{r}{r}^2\left(d\term{theta}{\theta}^2 + \sin^2\term{theta}{\theta}\,d\term{phi}{\phi}^2\right)
$$
<Fragment slot="where">$t$ is the time on a clock at rest very far away. $r$ is the *areal* radius: a sphere at $r$ has area $4\pi r^2$. $\theta, \phi$ are the usual angles. The proper time along a timelike path is $d\tau = \sqrt{-ds^2}/c$. Light travels along paths with $ds = 0$.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

<History title="A solution from the Russian front" year="1915">
<Portrait src="/img/people/schwarzschild.jpg" name="Karl Schwarzschild" years="1873–1916" credit="Unknown photographer, c. 1900" license="Public domain" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Karl.Schwarzschild.png" />

Karl Schwarzschild was director of the Potsdam observatory, and at 40 one of Germany's leading astronomers, when he volunteered for the army in 1914. In late 1915 he was computing artillery trajectories on the Russian front when he read Einstein's newly published field equations. Within weeks he had found their exact solution outside a spherical mass, and mailed it to Einstein. Einstein replied that he "would not have expected that one could formulate the exact solution of the problem in such a simple way," and presented it to the Prussian Academy in January 1916. By then Schwarzschild had contracted pemphigus, a painful autoimmune skin disease. He was invalided home and died in May 1916, aged 42. Neither he nor Einstein thought the radius $r_s$ described anything real. It took until 1939 (Oppenheimer and Snyder's collapsing dust cloud) and really the 1960s for physicists to accept that stars could fall through it.
</History>

Read it term by term, as you would read a piece of code:

- **The $dt^2$ term is gravity's clock.** A clock hovering at fixed $r$ has $dr = d\theta = d\phi = 0$, so $d\tau = \sqrt{1-r_s/r}\,dt$. Clocks deep in the well tick slowly compared with clocks far away. For weak fields, $\sqrt{1-r_s/r}\approx 1 + \Phi/c^2$ with $\Phi=-GM/r$. So this one coefficient contains Newtonian gravity: a slower clock lower down is what makes free particles "fall".
- **The $dr^2$ term stretches space radially.** The proper distance between shells at $r$ and $r + dr$ is $dr/\sqrt{1-r_s/r}$, which is *more* than $dr$. Space near the hole is deeper than its circumference suggests. Draw the equatorial plane as a surface in 3D and you get the familiar funnel (Flamm's paraboloid). This spatial part is what doubles the Newtonian deflection of starlight.
- **The angular part is ordinary.** Spheres are round and have area $4\pi r^2$. This is what *defines* $r$. It is not "the distance to the centre", which is not even well defined inside the horizon.
- **Far away, $r\gg r_s$**, both coefficients go to 1 and we recover the flat Minkowski spacetime of [Chapter 17](/ch/relativity/).

At $r = r_s$ the $dt^2$ coefficient vanishes and the $dr^2$ coefficient blows up. This is a *coordinate* singularity. A falling observer passes through it in finite proper time and notices nothing locally special there. (Curvature invariants such as the Kretschmann scalar $\propto r_s^2/r^6$ are finite at the horizon.) It is still a real boundary: inside, $r$ becomes a timelike coordinate. Decreasing $r$ is then as unavoidable as tomorrow, and every future-directed path ends at $r = 0$, where the curvature really is infinite.

</Fragment>
</Tiers>


<Aside>**Coordinates can lie.** In Eddington–Finkelstein or Kruskal coordinates the horizon is a perfectly smooth null surface. The $1/(1 - r_s/r)$ blow-up is an artefact of using clocks at infinity to label events near the hole, much as longitude is undefined at the North Pole.</Aside>

### Clocks and colours near the hole

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
Slow clocks deep down have a colour (for waves and wavelengths, see [Appendix A8](/ch/primer-waves/)). Light is a clock too: every wave crest is a tick. Light sent up from near the hole arrives with its ticks spread out, so it is shifted to longer, redder wavelengths. This **gravitational redshift** has been measured on Earth over a height of just 22 metres, and GPS satellites have to correct for it every day. Near a black hole it becomes enormous. Light from the horizon itself would be stretched infinitely, which is one way to see why a horizon is black.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">

The clock-rate factor has a direct observable consequence. Suppose an atom hovering at radius $r$ emits light at frequency $\nu_\text{em}$. The wave crests are emitted at a fixed rate of the local proper time, but they are counted by a distant observer in coordinate time $t$. The spacetime is static, so it takes each crest the same coordinate time to climb out. The distant observer therefore receives

$$
\frac{\term{nuinf}{\nu_\infty}}{\term{nuem}{\nu_\text{em}}} = \sqrt{1-\frac{\term{rs}{r_s}}{\term{r}{r}}}, \qquad 1+\term{z}{z} = \left(1-\frac{\term{rs}{r_s}}{\term{r}{r}}\right)^{-1/2}.
$$

At the surface of the Sun, $r_s/r = 4\times10^{-6}$, so $z = 2\times10^{-6}$. This shift was measured in the solar spectrum and in the lab (Pound and Rebka, 1959). GPS satellites correct for the Earth's version of it, about +45 μs per day. Near a black hole the effect becomes enormous.

</Fragment>
</Tiers>

A clock hovering at <Var name="rr" value={1.5} min={1.0001} max={100} log digits={4} /> $r_s$ runs at <Out name="rate" /> of the rate of a distant clock. One hour there is <Out name="hours" /> far away. The redshift is $z =$ <Out name="zz" />, so hydrogen's red H-alpha line (656.3 nm) arrives at <Out name="lam" />. Drag $r$ towards 1: the redshift diverges, which is why a horizon is black.

## Geodesics: orbits in curved spacetime

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
In [Chapter 2](/ch/orbits/) we pictured an orbit as a marble rolling in a bowl. The bowl's slope is gravity pulling in; its steep rim near the centre is the **centrifugal barrier**. Anything circling sideways is flung outward and bounces off that rim, so in Newton's gravity a planet with sideways motion can never fall straight in.

Einstein's gravity changes the bowl in one way: it adds an extra pull that grows very fast close in. So the rim has a **top**. Come in with too much energy, or too little sideways motion, and you go over it and fall into the hole. No amount of sideways speed can save you if you get too close.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">
Two quantities are conserved for anything moving freely around the hole, just as in [Chapter 2](/ch/orbits/): its **energy** and its **angular momentum** $L$ (per unit mass, $L = r\,v_\perp$ for sideways speed $v_\perp$). Working through the Schwarzschild geometry (the calculus is in the engineering version), the radial motion obeys exactly the Newtonian energy equation with one extra term:

<KeyEq title="Effective potential near a black hole">
$$
\term{Veff}{V_\text{eff}}(\term{r}{r}) = -\frac{\term{G}{G}\term{M}{M}}{\term{r}{r}} + \frac{\term{L}{L}^2}{2\term{r}{r}^2} - \frac{\term{gr}{GML^2}}{\term{gr}{c^2r^3}}
$$
<Fragment slot="where">$\tfrac12 v_r^2 + V_\text{eff}(r)$ is constant, where $v_r$ is the radial speed. The first two terms are Newton's gravity and the centrifugal barrier from Chapter 2. The <span data-term="gr">last term</span> is Einstein's correction. It is tiny far away but grows like $1/r^3$, faster than the barrier's $1/r^2$.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l3">

How do things move in this geometry? A free particle extremises its proper time (new to derivatives? see [Appendix A4](/ch/primer-calculus/)). The equations of motion follow from the Euler–Lagrange equations, but the metric's symmetries let us skip nearly all of that algebra. The same thing happened in [Chapter 2](/ch/orbits/): **each symmetry gives a conserved quantity**.

- The metric does not depend on $t$, so there is a conserved **energy** per unit rest mass: $\ \mathcal E = \left(1-\frac{r_s}{r}\right)c^2\,\frac{dt}{d\tau}$.
- The metric does not depend on $\phi$, so there is a conserved **angular momentum** per unit rest mass: $\ L = r^2\,\frac{d\phi}{d\tau}$. The motion stays in a plane, which we can take to be $\theta = \pi/2$.

The third relation is the normalisation of the four-velocity, $g_{\mu\nu}\,\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu = -c^2$ (a dot means $d/d\tau$). Substitute $dt/d\tau$ and $d\phi/d\tau$ in terms of $\mathcal E$ and $L$:

$$
-\frac{\term{E}{\mathcal E}^2}{\term{c}{c}^2}\left(1-\frac{\term{rs}{r_s}}{\term{r}{r}}\right)^{-1} + \left(1-\frac{\term{rs}{r_s}}{\term{r}{r}}\right)^{-1}\term{rdot}{\dot r}^2 + \frac{\term{L}{L}^2}{\term{r}{r}^2} = -\term{c}{c}^2 .
$$

Multiply through by $(1 - r_s/r)/2$ and use $r_s = 2GM/c^2$. After a line of algebra, you get something very familiar:

<KeyEq title="Radial motion in Schwarzschild spacetime">
$$
\frac12\term{rdot}{\dot r}^2 + \term{Veff}{V_\text{eff}}(\term{r}{r}) = \frac{\term{E}{\mathcal E}^2 - \term{c}{c}^4}{2\term{c}{c}^2}, \qquad \term{Veff}{V_\text{eff}}(\term{r}{r}) = -\frac{\term{G}{G}\term{M}{M}}{\term{r}{r}} + \frac{\term{L}{L}^2}{2\term{r}{r}^2} - \frac{\term{gr}{GML^2}}{\term{gr}{c^2r^3}}
$$
<Fragment slot="where">Here $\dot r = dr/d\tau$ uses the particle's proper time, $\mathcal E$ is its conserved energy per unit mass (including rest energy, so $\mathcal E = c^2$ means "released from rest at infinity"), and $L$ is its angular momentum per unit mass. The first two terms of $V_\text{eff}$ are **exactly** Newton's (Chapter 2). The <span data-term="gr">new term, $-GML^2/c^2r^3$</span>, is all of general relativity's correction for orbits.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

This is the Chapter 2 picture again, with one addition. The radial motion is a ball rolling in a one-dimensional potential. The new term is small at large $r$, but it falls off as $1/r^3$, faster than the centrifugal barrier's $1/r^2$. So at small $r$ it always wins: **the centrifugal barrier has a top**, and past it the particle falls in, however much angular momentum it has. In Newtonian gravity, a particle with any $L \neq 0$ bounces off the barrier. Here, the barrier has a finite height.

</Fragment>
</Tiers>

### Circular orbits and the ISCO

Circular orbits sit where the curve is flat: at the bottom of a valley (stable) or the top of a hill (unstable). For a lot of angular momentum there are two: the familiar Newtonian one far out, and a new, unstable one close in, at about $1.5\,r_s$. As $L$ drops, the valley and the hill move together, and at a critical value they merge and vanish. That happens at $3\,r_s$. **Inside three Schwarzschild radii there is no stable circular orbit.** Try it in the figure below.

<Level min={3} title="Finding the circular orbits">

Circular orbits sit where $dV_\text{eff}/dr = 0$. Differentiate and multiply by $r^4/GM$:

$$
\term{r}{r}^2 - \frac{\term{L}{L}^2}{\term{G}{G}\term{M}{M}}\,\term{r}{r} + \frac{3\term{L}{L}^2}{\term{c}{c}^2} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \term{rpm}{r_\pm} = \frac{\term{L}{L}^2}{2\term{G}{G}\term{M}{M}}\left[1 \pm \sqrt{1 - \frac{12\,\term{G}{G}^2\term{M}{M}^2}{\term{c}{c}^2\term{L}{L}^2}}\right].
$$

For large $L$ there are two roots. $r_+ \approx L^2/GM$ is the Newtonian stable orbit at the bottom of the well. $r_-\approx 3GM/c^2$ is a new, *unstable* circular orbit on top of the barrier. As $L$ decreases, the two approach each other. They merge when the square root vanishes, at $L^2 = 12\,G^2M^2/c^2$, where $r = 6GM/c^2 = 3r_s$. Below that, there are no circular orbits at all.

</Level>

<KeyEq title="The innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO)">
$$
\term{risco}{r_\text{ISCO}} = \frac{6\term{G}{G}\term{M}{M}}{\term{c}{c}^2} = 3\,\term{rs}{r_s}, \qquad \frac{\term{Eisco}{\mathcal E_\text{ISCO}}}{\term{c}{c}^2} = \sqrt{\frac{8}{9}} \approx 0.943

<Fragment slot="where">Inside $3r_s$, no circular orbit is stable: the smallest nudge sends the particle spiralling in. The new term also makes bound orbits precess. The period of radial oscillation no longer matches the orbital period, so the ellipse rotates by $\Delta\phi \approx 6\pi GM/[c^2 a(1-e^2)]$ per orbit. For Mercury that is 43 arcseconds per century, the anomaly that Einstein explained in November 1915.

### Light: the photon sphere and the shadow

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
Light feels the pull too. Far away it is bent only slightly. At **1.5 Schwarzschild radii** gravity is strong enough to bend light into a full circle: the **photon sphere**. A photon there could orbit forever, but the orbit is as unstable as a pencil balanced on its tip.

A ray aimed a bit closer than about **2.6 Schwarzschild radii** from the centre is swallowed. Turn that around: looking at a black hole, every direction within that distance shows nothing but darkness. The hole casts a **shadow** about 2.6 times the size of its horizon. Rays that just miss whirl around the photon sphere a few times before escaping and form a thin, bright **photon ring** at the shadow's edge.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">
For light the same bookkeeping works, with one change: what matters is the **impact parameter** $b$, the distance by which the ray would miss the centre if it went straight. Light can circle the hole at

$$
\term{rph}{r_\text{ph}} = \tfrac32\,\term{rs}{r_s} ,
$$

the **photon sphere**, an unstable orbit. Every ray with

$$
\term{b}{b} < \term{bc}{b_c} = \frac{\sqrt{27}}{2}\,\term{rs}{r_s} \approx 2.60\,\term{rs}{r_s}
$$

is captured. So the hole casts a **shadow** of radius $2.6\,r_s$, bigger than the horizon because the hole lenses its own silhouette. Rays with $b$ just above $b_c$ wind around the photon sphere before escaping, making a thin bright **photon ring**.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l3">

For light, $ds^2 = 0$ replaces the $-c^2$ on the right-hand side with 0, and $\tau$ is replaced by an affine parameter $\lambda$. The same steps give

$$
\frac{1}{\term{L}{L}^2}\left(\frac{d\term{r}{r}}{d\term{lam}{\lambda}}\right)^2 + \underbrace{\frac{1}{\term{r}{r}^2}\left(1 - \frac{\term{rs}{r_s}}{\term{r}{r}}\right)}_{\term{W}{W(r)}} = \frac{1}{\term{b}{b}^2}, \qquad \term{b}{b} \equiv \frac{\term{c}{c}\term{L}{L}}{\term{E}{\mathcal E}}.
$$

Here $b$ is the **impact parameter**, the perpendicular distance at which the ray would pass the hole if spacetime were flat. The ray's path depends on $b$ alone. The "potential" $W(r)$ has no well at all, only a single peak. Setting $W' = 0$ gives

$$
\term{rph}{r_\text{ph}} = \tfrac32\,\term{rs}{r_s} = \frac{3\term{G}{G}\term{M}{M}}{\term{c}{c}^2}, \qquad \term{W}{W}_\text{max} = \frac{4}{27\,\term{rs}{r_s}^2}.
$$

At $r = 1.5\,r_s$ light can orbit in a circle. This is the **photon sphere**. The orbit is unstable, like a pencil balanced on its tip. A ray with $1/b^2 > W_\text{max}$ goes over the top and falls in. So every ray with

$$
\term{b}{b} < \term{bc}{b_c} = \frac{\sqrt{27}}{2}\,\term{rs}{r_s} = \sqrt{27}\,\frac{\term{G}{G}\term{M}{M}}{\term{c}{c}^2} \approx 2.60\,\term{rs}{r_s}
$$

is captured. Run this backwards from a telescope. Every pixel whose backwards-traced ray has $b < b_c$ ends on the horizon, and no light can come from that direction. The black hole casts a **shadow** of radius $\sqrt{27}\,GM/c^2$. That is 2.6 times the horizon radius, magnified by the hole's own lensing. Rays with $b$ just above $b_c$ wind round the photon sphere several times before escaping. Each extra half-turn makes an exponentially thinner copy of the whole sky, stacked at the shadow's edge: the **photon ring**.

</Fragment>
</Tiers>

<Level min={3} title="The orbit equation for light">

The shape of a light ray also follows from $u = 1/r$ as a function of $\phi$, exactly as in Chapter 2. With $u' = du/d\phi$:

$$
\frac{d^2\term{u}{u}}{d\term{phi}{\phi}^2} + \term{u}{u} = \frac{3}{2}\,\term{rs}{r_s}\,\term{u}{u}^2 .
$$

Without the right-hand side this is a straight line, $u = \sin\phi/b$. With it, a ray grazing the Sun is bent by $2r_s/b = 1.75''$. Eddington's 1919 eclipse expedition measured this deflection and made Einstein famous.

</Level>

<Fragment slot="l1">
The picture is built from four effects, and you can switch each one off:

- **Lensing.** Light bends around the hole. That is why you see the far side of the disk arched over the top, and why stars near the shadow are smeared into arcs and rings.
- **A hot disk.** Gas spiralling in heats up, hottest a little outside the innermost stable orbit, and glows like a blackbody: redder far out, whiter close in.
- **Doppler beaming.** The inner disk orbits at about half the speed of light. Gas coming towards us looks bluer and *much* brighter than gas moving away, which is why one side of the ring outshines the other.
- **Gravitational redshift.** Light climbing out of the well loses energy, dimming and reddening the innermost gas.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">

The picture is built up from four pieces of physics, and you can turn each one on and off.

**Lensing.** Rays are bent strongly near the hole. The shadow is a dark disk of angular radius $\sin\alpha_\text{sh} = (b_c/r)\sqrt{1 - r_s/r}$ for a camera at radius $r$, which is about $b_c/r$ far away. Just outside the shadow, the sky is squeezed into a thin ring containing infinitely many ever-thinner images of the whole celestial sphere. Put a galaxy exactly behind the hole and its light arrives from every side at once as an **Einstein ring** at $b\approx\sqrt{2r_s D}$ for a distant source (see [Chapter 24](/ch/lensing/)).

**Disk temperature.** Gas in a thin disk spirals inward slowly, through a sequence of nearly circular orbits. Viscosity transfers angular momentum outward and turns orbital energy into heat. Each ring radiates locally as a blackbody. Energy and angular-momentum conservation then give the flux emitted per unit area of each face:

$$
\term{sigma}{\sigma} \term{T}{T}^4(\term{r}{r}) = \frac{3\term{G}{G}\term{M}{M}\term{Mdot}{\dot M}}{8\pi \term{r}{r}^3}\left(1 - \sqrt{\frac{\term{rin}{r_\text{in}}}{\term{r}{r}}}\right).
$$

Here $\dot M$ is the accretion rate (kg/s). The bracket comes from the zero-torque condition at the inner edge, where gas stops being held up by rotation and plunges. The profile peaks at $r = \tfrac{49}{36}r_\text{in}\approx 4.1\,r_s$, and $T \propto r^{-3/4}$ far out. For a 10 M☉ hole eating $10^{-8}$ M☉/yr, the peak temperature is around $10^7$ K, so the disk shines in X-rays. For a $10^8$ M☉ quasar it is a few $\times10^5$ K, in the ultraviolet ($T\propto M^{-1/4}$ at a fixed Eddington ratio). The sim lets you choose $T_\text{peak}$ so that the colours land in the visible.

**Doppler beaming.** The inner disk orbits at up to half the speed of light (at the ISCO, a local observer measures $v = c/2$). Photons from the side approaching us are blueshifted, and those from the receding side are redshifted. Brightness changes far more than frequency. The quantity $I_\nu/\nu^3$ is invariant along a ray (it counts photons in phase space), so the bolometric intensity scales as $I = g^4 I_\text{em}$, where $g = \nu_\text{obs}/\nu_\text{em}$. At $g = 1.3$ that is a factor of 2.9, and at $g = 0.7$ a factor of 0.24. The approaching side outshines the receding side by more than ten to one.

**Gravitational redshift.** The factor $\sqrt{1-r_s/r}$ from the previous section also applies to the disk. It dims the inner disk and reddens it. Combined with the orbital motion, the full shift for gas on a circular orbit at angular velocity $\Omega = \sqrt{GM/r^3}$ is

$$
\term{g}{g} = \frac{\sqrt{1 - \tfrac32 \term{rs}{r_s}/\term{r}{r}}}{1 - \term{Omega}{\Omega}\,\term{ell}{\ell}/\term{c}{c}^2},
$$

where $\ell$ is the photon's conserved angular momentum per unit energy about the disk's axis ($\ell\,c$ plays the role of $L/\mathcal E$). The numerator combines gravitational redshift with the moving clock's time dilation. The denominator is the ordinary first-order Doppler shift.

</Fragment>
</Tiers>

<Hood title="a WebGPU geodesic ray tracer">
The whole renderer is one full-screen fragment shader. Each pixel is independent, which makes this an embarrassingly parallel problem and a natural fit for a GPU. There are no meshes and no textures except a 256-entry blackbody colour look-up table.

**What to integrate.** The full geodesic equation has eight coupled first-order ODEs, with Christoffel symbols. We need much less. Every Schwarzschild geodesic lies in a plane through the centre, and a photon's path shape depends only on $b$. So we integrate the photon's 3D *spatial* path in plain Cartesian coordinates with a fictitious central force chosen to reproduce the Binet equation. For any central acceleration $f(r)\hat r$ with conserved $h = |\vec x\times\dot{\vec x}|$, Binet gives $u'' + u = -f/(h^2u^2)$. Setting $f = -\tfrac32 h^2/r^4$ (in units $r_s = c = 1$) gives exactly $u'' + u = \tfrac32 u^2$:

$$
\ddot{\term{x}{\vec x}} = -\frac32\,\term{h2}{h^2}\,\frac{\term{x}{\vec x}}{\term{r}{r}^5}.
$$

This avoids polar-coordinate singularities, gives the out-of-plane direction for free, and costs one `rsqrt`-ish operation per force evaluation. The parameter is not affine, but we never need arrival times.

**Initial conditions.** A camera hovering at radius $r_c$ is a static observer. Its local sky is related to coordinate directions by the $\sqrt{1-r_s/r}$ radial stretch, so a local direction $\hat n = n_r\hat r + \vec n_t$ becomes coordinate velocity $\propto n_r\sqrt{1 - 1/r_c}\,\hat r + \vec n_t$. The equation is homogeneous in the velocity scale, so only the direction matters. With this correction the rendered shadow has exactly the angular size quoted in the readout. The disk's redshift also includes the camera's own blueshift, $1/\sqrt{1-1/r_c}$.

**The integrator** is classical RK4 ([Appendix A5](/ch/primer-odes/) covers integrators). It is not symplectic, but each ray lives for a few hundred steps, not millions, so secular drift doesn't matter (compare Chapter 2). What does matter is the step size. Near the hole the curvature scale is $\sim r$, so we take $\Delta\lambda = k\,r$ with $k = 0.04$–$0.12$ (quality setting). This gives many steps per orbit near the photon sphere and a logarithmic march out to the escape sphere, about 30 steps from $r = 2$ to $r = 60$.

```wgsl
fn accel(p: vec3f, k: f32) -> vec3f {
  let r2 = dot(p, p);
  return -k * p / (r2 * r2 * sqrt(r2));        // k = (3/2) h²
}
// … per pixel:
for (var i = 0; i < maxSteps; i++) {
  let r = length(p);
  if (r < 1.0) { break; }                                   // horizon
  if (r > u.prm.w && dot(p, v) > 0.0) { escaped = true; break; }
  let dt = u.prm.x * r;                                      // step ∝ r
  let p0 = p;
  rk4(&p, &v, dt, k);
  if (p0.z * p.z < 0.0) {                                    // crossed the disk plane
    let pc = mix(p0, p, p0.z / (p0.z - p.z));
    let e = diskEmit(pc, lam);
    col += trans * e.rgb;  trans *= 1.0 - e.a;
    if (trans < 0.02) { break; }
  }
}
```

**Termination.** A ray stops when (i) $r < r_s$, and it is black; (ii) $r$ exceeds the escape radius (60 $r_s$) while moving outward, and it samples the procedural sky in its *final* direction; (iii) the disk has made it nearly opaque; or (iv) it runs out of steps. Case (iv) happens only for rays within a hair of the photon sphere, and they are painted black. That is the right limit, since such rays hug the orbit for ever. Each crossing of the plane $z = 0$ is located by linear interpolation between steps, and it is composited front to back. A ray can cross the disk up to three times, which is how the secondary and tertiary images appear.

**Precision.** Everything is f32, which is safe because the units keep every number of order 1: $r\in[1, 60]$, $h\in[0, 60]$. The dangerous term is $1/r^5$, but it is only evaluated for $r \ge 1$, so it never overflows. The main f32 error is in the photon ring: rays within $\sim10^{-7}$ of $b_c$ cannot be distinguished, but they would need about $\ln(10^7)/2\pi\approx 2.6$ turns to separate anyway, far thinner than a pixel.

**Cost.** The cost is $O(\text{pixels}\times\text{steps})$ with 4 force evaluations per step. At "Medium" (80% resolution, at most 300 steps) most rays finish in 40–80 steps. Rays that graze the photon sphere use the full budget, and they are a thin ring of pixels. Resolution is the main knob. The canvas is rendered at reduced size and upscaled by the browser's bilinear filter. The accretion flow is textured with two cross-faded layers of fBm noise, advected at the local Kepler rate and reset in turn, so the shearing pattern never winds up.
</Hood>

## Accretion: the most efficient engine in nature

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
A black hole does not shine, but gas falling into one does, and very efficiently. Gas can only spiral in slowly, orbit by orbit, rubbing against itself and glowing hot as it goes. By the time it reaches the innermost stable orbit and drops in, it has given off **about 6%** of its mass as energy (Einstein's $E = mc^2$). Nuclear fusion in the Sun manages only 0.7%. Around a fast-spinning hole the figure can reach about 30–40%. Pound for pound, falling into a black hole is the most powerful energy source in nature short of matter meeting antimatter. That is how quasars outshine whole galaxies.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">

A black hole does not shine, but gas falling into one does, very efficiently. Consider a parcel of gas that spirals inward through a thin disk, radiating its orbital energy as it goes. It reaches the ISCO with specific energy $\mathcal E_\text{ISCO}$ and then plunges, taking that energy across the horizon. What it radiated along the way is the **radiative efficiency**:

$$
\term{eta}{\eta} = 1 - \frac{\term{Eisco}{\mathcal E_\text{ISCO}}}{\term{c}{c}^2} = 1 - \sqrt{\tfrac89} = 5.7\% \quad\text{(Schwarzschild)}.
$$

Hydrogen fusion into helium releases 0.7% of the rest mass ([Chapter 12](/ch/fusion/)). A non-rotating black hole is **eight times better** than the best nuclear fuel. A maximally spinning one (next section) has $\eta = 1 - 1/\sqrt3 = 42\%$. In practice, disks torque the hole up to about $a = 0.998$, where $\eta \approx 32\%$. This is why quasars are the most luminous steady objects in the universe, and why most of the energy released by baryons since the Big Bang came out of accretion onto black holes as well as from stars.

</Fragment>
</Tiers>

<Fermi title="How fast does a quasar eat?">
A bright quasar has $L \sim 10^{40}$ W, about $2.6\times10^{13}\,L_\odot$. With $\eta \approx 0.1$ and $L = \eta\dot Mc^2$:

$$\dot M = \frac{L}{\eta c^2} = \frac{10^{40}}{0.1\times 9\times10^{16}} \approx 1.1\times10^{24}\ \text{kg/s} \approx 18\ \text{M}_\odot/\text{yr}.$$

That is a few Suns per year. The upper limit is the **Eddington luminosity** (Chapter 23). There, radiation pressure on electrons balances gravity on protons: $L_\text{Edd} = 4\pi GMm_pc/\sigma_T \approx 1.3\times10^{31}\,(M/\text{M}_\odot)$ W. So $10^{40}$ W needs $M \gtrsim 10^9$ M☉. The e-folding time for growth at the Eddington limit (the Salpeter time) is $t = \eta\,\sigma_T c/(4\pi G m_p (1 - \eta)) \approx 50$ Myr. Growing a $10^9$ M☉ hole from a 100 M☉ seed takes $\ln(10^7)\approx 16$ e-foldings, about 800 Myr. That is uncomfortably close to the ages of the most distant quasars JWST sees, which is why "heavy seeds" are a hot topic.
</Fermi>

Real accretion flows are messier than the thin disk in the simulation. At low accretion rates (Sgr A\* today, M87\*) the gas cannot cool. It stays hot, puffed up and optically thin, and much of its energy is swallowed or blown out in winds and jets instead of being radiated. At rates near Eddington, radiation pressure puffs the disk up instead. The thin-disk model describes the middle ground, which includes most bright X-ray binaries and quasars.

<Aside>**Jets.** Magnetised gas around a spinning hole can tap its rotational energy directly (the Blandford–Znajek mechanism, 1977). The jets of M87 reach thousands of light-years and are powered at up to $\sim10^{37}$ W, with efficiencies that can exceed 100% of the accreted rest-mass energy because the energy comes from the hole's spin.</Aside>

## Spinning black holes

Real black holes spin. Collapsing stars rotate, and accreted gas brings angular momentum. The spinning solution was found by Roy Kerr in 1963, 48 years after Schwarzschild's. It is described by the mass $M$ and a dimensionless spin $a = cJ/GM^2$ between 0 and 1. (Charge could be a third number, but astrophysical holes neutralise quickly.) That is all. The "no-hair" theorems say that a stationary black hole in GR is fully described by $M$, $J$ and $Q$. Every other detail of the collapsing star is radiated away.

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
Spin changes things in four ways:

- **Space is dragged around.** Near a spinning hole, space itself swirls with it, like honey around a turning spoon.
- **The ergosphere.** Close in, the swirl is so strong that nothing can stay still, even moving at the speed of light. Energy can be stolen from the hole's spin there, which is probably what powers the great jets.
- **Orbits can go closer.** Gas orbiting with the spin can circle stably much closer in, and so radiates more of its energy before falling: up to 42% of its mass, in theory.
- **The shadow is squashed** slightly on one side, but its size barely changes. That makes it a good scale for weighing the hole.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">

Rotation changes the picture qualitatively:

- **Frame dragging.** Spacetime near the hole is dragged around with it. A zero-angular-momentum observer orbits at angular velocity $\omega \approx 2GJ/c^2r^3$ far out, and faster closer in. The same effect was measured around the Earth by Gravity Probe B (2011) and by the LAGEOS satellites, where Earth's dragging amounts to about 39 milliarcseconds per year.
- **The ergosphere.** Inside the surface $r_\text{E}(\theta) = \frac{GM}{c^2}\bigl(1 + \sqrt{1 - a^2\cos^2\theta}\bigr)$, dragging is so strong that nothing can stay still relative to distant stars, even moving at $c$ against the rotation. At the equator this surface is at $2GM/c^2$, outside the horizon at $r_+ = \frac{GM}{c^2}(1 + \sqrt{1-a^2})$. Particles in the ergosphere can have *negative* energy as seen from infinity. That makes it possible to extract rotational energy (the Penrose process), up to 29% of the mass for $a = 1$.
- **A spin-dependent ISCO.** Prograde orbits are helped by the dragging. The ISCO moves in from $6GM/c^2$ ($a = 0$) to $GM/c^2$ ($a\to1$), deep in the well, where orbiting gas has lost 42% of its rest energy. Retrograde orbits are pushed out to $9GM/c^2$. X-ray astronomers measure spins by fitting the inner edge of the disk: its thermal spectrum, and the relativistically smeared iron Kα line at 6.4 keV. Many X-ray binaries and AGN come out with $a \gtrsim 0.9$.
- **A squashed, shifted shadow.** Seen from the equator, the shadow of a rapidly spinning hole is flattened on one side and displaced by up to $2GM/c^2$. Its size changes by only about 4% across all spins, which is why the EHT ring measures mass so robustly.

</Fragment>
</Tiers>

## The evidence

Black holes are no longer theoretical. Four independent lines of evidence now each point the same way.

**Stars orbiting Sagittarius A\*.** For three decades, two teams (Reinhard Genzel's at MPE, with the GRAVITY instrument at the VLT, and Andrea Ghez's at Keck) have tracked stars in the central arcsecond of the Milky Way. The star S2 orbits in 16.05 years on an ellipse with $e = 0.88$. It comes within 120 AU of the centre, about 1,400 $r_s$, moving at 7,700 km/s (2.6% of $c$). Kepler III, applied exactly as in the Fermi estimate of Chapter 2, gives $M = 4.30\times10^6$ M☉ inside that pericentre. At the 2018 pericentre passage, GRAVITY measured S2's **gravitational redshift** (plus transverse Doppler) at the level GR predicts. In 2020 it measured the orbit's **Schwarzschild precession**: 12 arcminutes per orbit, prograde, exactly as predicted. Genzel and Ghez shared the 2020 Nobel Prize.

<Aside>**Why "must be a black hole"?** Four million solar masses inside 120 AU is compact enough. A cluster of dark stars (neutron stars, stellar black holes) that dense would evaporate or collapse in far less than the Galaxy's age. VLBI puts even tighter limits on the size of the radio source itself: less than 10 $r_s$. A boson star or other exotica cannot be excluded completely, but each needs new physics.</Aside>

**Images of the shadow.** The Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) links radio dishes from Greenland to the South Pole into an Earth-sized interferometer at 1.3 mm wavelength. Its resolution is about 20 microarcseconds, enough to read a newspaper in New York from Paris. In 2019 it published M87\*, a ring $42\pm3$ μas across. For a $6.5\times10^9$ M☉ hole at 16.8 Mpc, the shadow diameter $2\sqrt{27}\,GM/c^2$ subtends 40 μas. In 2022 came Sgr A\*, with a $51.8\pm2.3$ μas ring against a predicted 53 μas from the S2 mass. These two holes differ in mass by a factor of 1,500, and both rings match the GR shadow size to within about 10%. Later EHT campaigns have mapped the magnetic field polarisation around both holes and followed M87\*'s ring from year to year. The brighter side of the ring moves around, as expected for turbulent accretion around a steady shadow.

<History title="A bet on Cygnus X-1" year="1974">
Cygnus X-1 was found by rocket-borne X-ray counters in 1964. In 1971–72 Louise Webster and Paul Murdin in England, and independently Tom Bolton in Toronto, found that a blue supergiant there swings back and forth every 5.6 days around an invisible companion of several solar masses. That was too heavy for a neutron star. In 1974 Stephen Hawking made a bet with Kip Thorne that it was *not* a black hole. Hawking called it an insurance policy: he had invested so much in black holes that if they turned out not to exist, he would at least win a year's subscription to *Private Eye*. By 1990 the evidence was overwhelming. Hawking broke into Thorne's office and signed the framed bet in concession, and Thorne got a year of *Penthouse*.
</History>

**X-ray binaries.** Cygnus X-1, the first black-hole candidate (1971), is a hot X-ray source in a 5.6-day orbit with a blue supergiant 2.2 kpc away. The companion's radial-velocity curve ([Chapter 10](/ch/exoplanets/) uses the same technique for planets) together with a VLBI parallax distance gives a compact object of $21\pm2$ M☉. That is nearly ten times the maximum neutron-star mass. About twenty such "dynamically confirmed" stellar black holes are known, and Gaia DR3 has found some dormant ones. Gaia BH3 (2024), with 33 M☉, is the heaviest stellar black hole known in the Galaxy.

**Gravitational waves.** On 14 September 2015, LIGO heard two black holes of 36 and 29 M☉ merge 1.3 billion light-years away. The signal rose in frequency and amplitude over a fraction of a second, which is the chirp of [Chapter 20](/ch/gravitational-waves/), and then rang down at the frequency of the final 62 M☉ Kerr hole. About $3\,\text{M}_\odot c^2$ was radiated in 0.2 s, briefly outshining all the stars in the observable universe combined. By the end of the fourth observing run (O4, 2023–2025), LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA had around 300 merger candidates. They include GW231123, with a total mass of about 225 M☉ and both holes spinning near the Kerr limit. There is also the exceptionally loud GW250114, whose ringdown tests the no-hair theorem and confirms Hawking's area theorem: the horizon area grew in the merger, as it must.

<Aside>**The ringdown is the shadow, heard.** The frequency and damping time of the final ringing are set by the light ring, the unstable photon orbit. The oscillation frequency is roughly the orbital frequency at $1.5r_s$, and the damping rate is its Lyapunov exponent. The feature that makes the EHT's ring is the same one that sets LIGO's final note.</Aside>

## Not quite black: Hawking radiation

In 1974 Stephen Hawking (for the quantum background, see [Appendix A9](/ch/primer-quantum/)) combined quantum field theory with the Schwarzschild geometry and found that black holes are not quite black. A horizon emits thermal radiation at a temperature set by its **surface gravity** $\kappa = c^4/4GM$. That is the acceleration needed to hover just outside, redshifted to infinity.

<Level max={1}>
In plain words: quantum physics makes the horizon glow very faintly, as if it had a temperature. The bigger the hole, the colder it is. A black hole the mass of the Sun would be 60 billionths of a degree above absolute zero, far too cold to see and colder than the afterglow of the Big Bang. Only tiny black holes, the mass of a mountain, would be hot enough to glow brightly and eventually evaporate.
</Level>

<KeyEq title="Hawking temperature">
$$
\term{TH}{T_H} = \frac{\term{hbar}{\hbar} \term{c}{c}^3}{8\pi \term{G}{G} \term{M}{M} \term{kB}{k_B}} \approx 6.2\times10^{-8}\ \text{K}\left(\frac{\term{Msun}{\text{M}_\odot}}{\term{M}{M}}\right)
$$
<Fragment slot="where">$\hbar$ is <C k="hbar" /> and $k_B$ is <C k="kB" />. The formula contains the constants of quantum mechanics ($\hbar$), relativity ($c$), gravity ($G$) and thermodynamics ($k_B$), which makes it perhaps the most "unified" equation in physics. Bigger holes are **colder**.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

<Fermi title="Estimating Hawking's temperature without quantum gravity">
A thermal photon has a wavelength $\lambda \sim hc/k_BT$. A horizon can only "emit" wavelengths comparable to its own size, $\lambda \sim r_s$. Set them equal:

$$k_B T \sim \frac{hc}{r_s} = \frac{hc^3}{2GM}.$$

For 1 M☉, $r_s = 3$ km gives $T \sim 6.6\times10^{-34}\times 3\times10^8 / (3\times10^3 \times 1.4\times10^{-23}) \approx 5\ \mu$K. The exact answer is $8\pi^2\approx 80$ times smaller, because Hawking quanta have wavelengths many times the size of the hole. The estimate still captures the physics. **$T \propto 1/M$**, and a stellar black hole is tens of nanokelvin, far colder than the 2.7 K cosmic microwave background. Today every astrophysical black hole absorbs more CMB photons than it emits, and grows.

The luminosity follows from Stefan–Boltzmann: $L \sim \sigma T^4 \cdot 4\pi r_s^2 \propto M^{-2}$. Energy budget $Mc^2$ divided by $L$ gives a lifetime $t \propto M^3$. Plugging in numbers, $t \approx 2\times10^{67}$ yr for 1 M☉.
</Fermi>

A black hole of <Var name="Mh" value={1e12} min={1e5} max={1e32} log /> kg has $r_s =$ <Out name="rsh" />, a Hawking temperature of <Out name="TH" />, and would evaporate in about <Out name="tev" />. A primordial black hole born in the early universe with about $2\times10^{11}$ kg would be finishing its evaporation today, in a final burst of gamma rays. None has been seen, which limits how common such holes can be. Around $10^{17}$–$10^{22}$ kg (asteroid masses), primordial black holes are still allowed to make up all of the dark matter ([Chapter 21](/ch/milky-way/)).

<Aside>**The information paradox.** If a black hole evaporates into purely thermal radiation, what happened to the information about what fell in? Quantum mechanics says information cannot be destroyed. Recent "island" calculations (2019 onwards) reproduce the Page curve expected if information comes out, but how it gets out is still debated.</Aside>

## Where we go from here

The Schwarzschild metric took us from a Newtonian guess ($v_\text{esc} = c$) to shadows, photon rings and the 5.7% efficiency that powers quasars. The main tools were the same as in Chapter 2: conserved energy and angular momentum, an effective potential, and a carefully chosen integrator. General relativity changed the potential by a single $1/r^3$ term, and that term produced the ISCO, the photon sphere, capture and zoom-whirl orbits.

Two black holes in orbit around each other are not a static geometry. They shake spacetime, radiate gravitational waves, and spiral together. [Chapter 20](/ch/gravitational-waves/) derives the quadrupole formula and the chirp. [Chapter 23](/ch/galaxy-formation/) returns to supermassive black holes as engines that shape entire galaxies, and [Chapter 24](/ch/lensing/) develops the weak-field version of the lensing you just rendered.
