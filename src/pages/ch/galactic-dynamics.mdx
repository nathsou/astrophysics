## A gas of stars that never collides

<Photo src="/img/galactic-dynamics/antennae.jpg" alt="Two colliding spiral galaxies, the Antennae, their tangled cores laced with dark dust lanes and pink star-forming regions" credit="NASA, ESA, and the Hubble Heritage Team (STScI/AURA)-ESA/Hubble Collaboration" license="CC BY 4.0" href="https://esahubble.org/images/heic0615a/" size="wide" aspect="1 / 1">
**The Antennae (NGC 4038/4039), 20 Mpc away.** Two spiral galaxies in mid-collision, imaged by Hubble. The pink knots are huge clouds of hydrogen lit up by newborn stars, triggered as the galaxies' gas clouds crash together. The stars themselves pass by one another untouched. From the ground, two long tidal tails (off this frame) give the pair its name. By the end of this chapter you will make them on your own GPU.
</Photo>

In [Chapter 2](/ch/orbits/) we solved the two-body problem exactly, and in Chapter 3 we saw that three bodies are already chaotic. A galaxy has about $10^{11}$ bodies. That sounds hopeless. Surprisingly, it is *easier* than three bodies, because with enough bodies the individual pulls blur into a smooth average, and each star simply orbits in that average field.

Think of the Milky Way's disk as a gas (a refresher on gases and random motions: [Appendix A7](/ch/primer-thermo/)) whose "molecules" are stars. Two questions decide how the gas behaves: do the molecules collide, and if they do not collide physically, do they scatter each other gravitationally?

Physical collisions are out of the question. A star has a radius of about $R_\odot = 7\times10^{8}$ m. Near the Sun the stars are about 1 pc ($3\times10^{16}$ m) apart. Shrink the Sun to a grain of sand 1 mm across and its nearest neighbour is another grain of sand 40 km away. In the entire history of the disk, only a tiny fraction of stars have ever touched another star. (Dense cluster cores are the exception, and they are where stellar collisions and blue stragglers happen.)

Gravitational scattering is subtler. Every time star A passes star B, B nudges A's path a little. One nudge is tiny, but there are many. Do they add up?

<History title="Galaxies on a table of light bulbs" year="1941">
Long before computers could do it, the Swedish astronomer **Erik Holmberg** at Lund ran the first N-body simulation of colliding galaxies with light bulbs. Light intensity falls off as $1/r^2$, exactly like gravity, so he built each galaxy from 37 bulbs on a table, measured the total light arriving at each bulb with a photocell (from several directions, to get the force components), and moved the bulbs by hand, step by step. His 1941 paper showed two passing disks raising spiral-like tidal arms and losing orbital energy, so that they could capture each other: tidal tails and merging, 30 years before the Toomres. Almost nobody noticed. Holmberg's "analog computer" had the same core as the GPU kernel in this chapter: add up every pair's inverse-square pull, then take a timestep.
</History>

### Two-body relaxation

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
Picture a star crossing the galaxy. Every other star it passes gives it a tiny sideways tug, each in a random direction. Random tugs mostly cancel, like a drunkard's steps, but slowly they accumulate. The question is how many crossings it takes before the tugs have added up to something as big as the star's own speed, so that it has "forgotten" its original path. (For a refresher on speeds, energy and orbits, see [Appendix A6](/ch/primer-mechanics/).)

The answer turns out to be roughly *the number of stars divided by eight times its natural logarithm*. The more stars there are, the smoother the galaxy looks, because each one is a smaller fraction of the total. For a galaxy with a hundred billion stars it takes hundreds of millions of crossings.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">
Consider a test star crossing a galaxy of $N$ stars. Each encounter gives it a small velocity kick in a random direction. Random kicks add like a random walk (logarithms and powers rusty? see [Appendix A1](/ch/primer-numbers/)): after $n$ of them the total is about $\sqrt n$ times one kick, so it is the *squares* of the kicks that add up. Adding up the squared kicks from all the stars it passes in one crossing, and using the fact that $v^2 \approx GM/R$ for a galaxy in balance (the virial theorem, below), gives a remarkably simple result: each crossing changes $v^2$ by a fraction of about $8\ln N/N$. The star forgets its initial orbit after about $N/(8\ln N)$ crossings. The logarithm appears because every factor of two in encounter distance contributes equally: distant encounters are weaker but more numerous.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l3">
Consider a test star crossing a galaxy of $N$ stars of mass $m$, radius $R$, typical speed $v$. An encounter with impact parameter $b$ deflects it by a velocity kick $\delta v \approx 2Gm/(bv)$: the force $Gm/b^2$ acts for about the time $2b/v$ that the star spends near its partner. The kicks point in random directions, so they add like a random walk: their *squares* add.

Per crossing, the number of stars with impact parameter between $b$ and $b + db$ is the surface density $N/\pi R^2$ times the ring area $2\pi b\,db$. Summing the squared kicks,

$$
\term{dv2}{\Delta v^2} \approx \int_{\term{bmin}{b_\text{min}}}^{\term{Rsys}{R}} \left(\frac{2\term{G}{G}\term{mstar}{m}}{\term{bimp}{b}\,\term{vtyp}{v}}\right)^2 \frac{2\term{N}{N}}{\term{Rsys}{R}^2}\, \term{bimp}{b}\,d\term{bimp}{b} = 8\term{N}{N}\left(\frac{\term{G}{G}\term{mstar}{m}}{\term{Rsys}{R}\term{vtyp}{v}}\right)^2 \term{lnL}{\ln\Lambda},
\qquad \term{lnL}{\Lambda} = \frac{\term{Rsys}{R}}{\term{bmin}{b_\text{min}}}.
$$

Every factor of two in $b$ contributes equally, which is where the logarithm comes from: distant encounters are weaker but more numerous. Now use the virial theorem (below) to relate speed to mass, $v^2 \approx GNm/R$, and the fractional change per crossing becomes $\Delta v^2/v^2 \approx 8\ln\Lambda/N$. The star forgets its initial orbit after $n_\text{relax} = N/(8 \ln\Lambda)$ crossings. With $b_\text{min} \approx R/N$ (the impact parameter for a 90° deflection), $\Lambda \approx N$.

</Fragment>
</Tiers>

<KeyEq title="Two-body relaxation time">
$$
\term{trelax}{t_\text{relax}} \approx \frac{\term{N}{N}}{8\ln \term{N}{N}}\; \term{tc}{t_\text{cross}}, \qquad \term{tc}{t_\text{cross}} = \frac{\term{Rsys}{R}}{\term{vtyp}{v}}
$$
<Fragment slot="where"><span data-term="N">N</span> is the number of stars and <span data-term="tc">$t_\text{cross}$</span> the time to cross the system once. After $t_\text{relax}$, the accumulated star–star encounters have changed a typical star's velocity by an amount comparable to the velocity itself. A system much younger than $t_\text{relax}$ is **collisionless**.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

A system of <Var name="Nstars" value={1e11} min={100} max={1e12} log /> stars, <Var name="R" value={10} min={0.001} max={1000} log /> kpc across, with speeds of <Var name="v" value={200} min={1} max={2000} log /> km/s, has a crossing time of <Out name="tcross" /> and a relaxation time of <Out name="trelax" />. That is <Out name="relaxAge" />.

<Aside>Drag the numbers. A globular cluster: $N = 10^5$, $R = 0.005$ kpc, $v = 10$ km/s. A galaxy cluster, counting galaxies as the "stars": $N = 1000$, $R = 1000$ kpc, $v = 1000$ km/s.</Aside>

<Fermi title="Is the Milky Way collisionless?">
The Milky Way has $N \approx 10^{11}$ stars, $\ln N \approx 25$, so $N/(8\ln N) \approx 5\times10^{8}$. The disk is $R \approx 10$ kpc across, and stars move at $v \approx 200$ km/s, so

$$t_\text{cross} \approx \frac{3\times10^{20}\,\text{m}}{2\times10^{5}\,\text{m/s}} \approx 1.5\times10^{15}\,\text{s} \approx 50\,\text{Myr}.$$

Then $t_\text{relax} \approx 5\times10^{8}\times 50\,\text{Myr} \approx 2\times10^{16}$ yr, a million times the age of the Universe. Star–star encounters are irrelevant to the Galaxy as a whole. By contrast, a globular cluster ($N = 10^5$, $t_\text{cross} \approx 0.5$ Myr) relaxes in about 0.5 Gyr: clusters *are* collisional, which is why they have mass segregation and why they slowly evaporate.
</Fermi>

The conclusion is the organising principle of this chapter. In a galaxy, **each star moves in the smooth gravitational field of all the others**, as if their mass were spread out like a fluid. Graininess does not matter. The flagship simulation in this chapter uses this fact, and so does every galaxy simulation ever run: the "particles" in an N-body code are not stars but samples of a smooth distribution.

## The equations of a collisionless galaxy

### The collisionless Boltzmann equation

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
Describe a galaxy not star by star, but by a map that says how crowded stars are at every *place* and every *velocity*. Because stars never collide, this crowd flows smoothly, like an incompressible liquid: follow any star and the crowding around it never changes. That single rule, together with "the crowd makes the gravity that moves the crowd", is the whole physics of a galaxy. Computers can't store the map directly (it has six dimensions), so they sample it with particles and move each one along its orbit. That is exactly what the simulations in this chapter do.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l3">
Describe the galaxy by its **distribution function** $f(\vec x, \vec v, t)$: the density of stars in six-dimensional phase space. Stars are not created or destroyed, and they move smoothly (no collisions), so $f$ flows through phase space like an incompressible fluid. The density of stars around any given star, as it moves, stays constant:

<KeyEq title="Collisionless Boltzmann (Vlasov) equation">
$$
\frac{\partial \term{fdf}{f}}{\partial \term{time}{t}} + \term{vvec}{\vec v}\cdot\nabla_{\term{xvec}{\vec x}} \term{fdf}{f} - \nabla\term{Phi}{\Phi}\cdot\nabla_{\term{vvec}{\vec v}} \term{fdf}{f} = 0, \qquad \nabla^2\term{Phi}{\Phi} = 4\pi \term{G}{G} \term{rhom}{\rho}, \quad \term{rhom}{\rho} = \int \term{fdf}{f}\, d^3\term{vvec}{v}
$$
<Fragment slot="where">$f$ is the phase-space density of stars (mass per unit volume per unit velocity-volume), $\Phi$ the smooth gravitational potential, and $\rho$ the mass density it produces through Poisson's equation. The first equation is Liouville's theorem applied to stars; the pair is self-consistent: the stars make the potential that moves the stars.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

This is the same equation that governs collisionless plasmas (with the sign of the force flipped) and dark matter in cosmology (Chapter 28). It is a partial differential equation in seven dimensions, which is why nobody solves it on a grid. Instead we **sample** $f$ with particles and move each one along its characteristic, that is, integrate its orbit. That *is* an N-body simulation.

</Fragment>
</Tiers>

<Level min={3} title="Jeans equations: weighing galaxies from their stellar motions">

Taking velocity moments (integrals over velocity; see [Appendix A4](/ch/primer-calculus/)) of the Boltzmann equation gives fluid-like equations. Multiply by $v_j$ and integrate over velocities, and for a steady, spherical system you get the **Jeans equation**:

$$
\frac{d(\term{nu}{\nu}\term{sigr}{\sigma_r}^2)}{d\term{rr}{r}} + \frac{2\term{betaan}{\beta}}{\term{rr}{r}}\term{nu}{\nu}\term{sigr}{\sigma_r}^2 = -\term{nu}{\nu}\frac{d\term{Phi}{\Phi}}{d\term{rr}{r}},
$$

where $\nu$ is the stellar density, $\sigma_r$ the radial velocity dispersion and $\beta = 1 - \sigma_t^2/\sigma_r^2$ the anisotropy. It is hydrostatic equilibrium ([Chapter 11](/ch/stellar-structure/)) with "pressure" $\nu\sigma^2$ supplied by random stellar motions instead of thermal ones. It is how astronomers weigh elliptical galaxies and dwarf spheroidals from their measured dispersions, and it is a main source of evidence for dark matter in dwarf galaxies. The catch is the anisotropy $\beta$: radial and tangential motions give different masses, and they are hard to separate from line-of-sight velocities alone (the "mass–anisotropy degeneracy").

</Level>

### The virial theorem

For any bound self-gravitating system in a steady state, the time-average of the kinetic energy $K$ and potential energy $W$ satisfies

<KeyEq title="Virial theorem">
$$
2\langle \term{Kin}{K}\rangle + \langle \term{Wpot}{W}\rangle = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \term{Mtot}{M} \approx \frac{\term{sigv}{\sigma}^2 \term{Rsys}{R}}{\term{G}{G}}
$$
<Fragment slot="where">$K = \tfrac12\sum m v^2$ and $W$ is the total gravitational potential energy (negative). Up to a structure factor of order a few, the mass of a system is its velocity dispersion squared times its radius, over $G$. The total energy is $E = K + W = -K$: a bound system that loses energy gets *hotter*.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

It follows from differentiating $I = \sum m r^2$ twice and averaging (the "moment of inertia" trick; we skip the algebra). Fritz Zwicky applied it to the Coma cluster in 1933 and found that the galaxies moved far too fast for the visible mass: the first evidence for dark matter ([Chapter 21](/ch/milky-way/)).

<Aside>**Negative heat capacity.** $E = -K$ means removing energy speeds stars up. It is why a star that radiates contracts and heats (Chapter 11), and why the cores of star clusters collapse.</Aside>

## Orbits in a galaxy: rosettes and epicycles

A star in a galaxy does not feel a point mass. The mass inside its orbit grows with radius, so the rotation curve is nearly flat, $v_c(R) \approx 220$ km/s at the Sun, rather than Keplerian. The consequence is that orbits do not close. An eccentric orbit traces a **rosette**, its pericentre advancing from one pass to the next.

### Epicycles

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
For a nearly circular orbit there is a lovely picture. Imagine an invisible point, the **guiding centre**, going round the galaxy on a perfect circle. The real star wobbles in and out around that point, like a child on a swing carried along on a merry-go-round. Seen from the merry-go-round, the star traces a small loop, an **epicycle**, and it goes round that loop *backwards*. The in-and-out wobble has its own rhythm, the **epicyclic frequency**, and in a galaxy it is about 1.4 times faster than the orbital rhythm. Because the two rhythms don't match, the orbit never quite closes. For the Sun, the wobble carries it about half a kiloparsec in and out every 170 million years.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">
For a nearly circular orbit there is a beautiful approximation. Let the star's **guiding centre** move on a circle of radius $R_g$ with angular velocity $\Omega = v_c/R_g$. The star oscillates in and out around that radius at the **epicyclic frequency** $\kappa$, while its guiding centre goes round at $\Omega$. If the rotation curve rises or falls as a power of radius, $v_c \propto R^{\beta}$, then

<KeyEq title="Epicyclic frequency (power-law rotation curve)">
$$
\frac{\term{kappa}{\kappa}}{\term{Omega}{\Omega}} = \sqrt{2(1+\term{betarc}{\beta})}
$$
<Fragment slot="where">$\Omega$ is the orbital angular velocity and $\kappa$ the frequency of radial oscillation. For a point mass ($\beta = -\tfrac12$) $\kappa = \Omega$ and the orbit closes: an ellipse. For a flat rotation curve ($\beta = 0$) $\kappa = \sqrt2\,\Omega \approx 1.41\,\Omega$. For a uniform sphere ($\beta = 1$) $\kappa = 2\Omega$.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

In a frame rotating with the guiding centre, the star traces a small ellipse, the **epicycle**, backwards. Each radial oscillation, the pericentre advances by $360°\times\Omega/\kappa$: about 255° for a flat rotation curve instead of 360°, which is why the orbit is a rosette.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l3">
For a nearly circular orbit there is a beautiful approximation. Let the star's **guiding centre** move on a circle of radius $R_g$ with angular velocity $\Omega(R_g) = v_c/R_g$. Write the star's radius as $R = R_g + x$ with $x$ small. The radial equation of motion is $\ddot R = -\partial\Phi_\text{eff}/\partial R$, with the effective potential $\Phi_\text{eff} = \Phi + L^2/2R^2$ we met in Chapter 2. Expanding to first order in $x$ gives a harmonic oscillator (see [Appendix A5](/ch/primer-odes/)), $\ddot x = -\kappa^2 x$, with the **epicyclic frequency**

<KeyEq title="Epicyclic frequency">
$$
\term{kappa}{\kappa}^2 = \term{Rgal}{R}\frac{d\term{Omega}{\Omega}^2}{d\term{Rgal}{R}} + 4\term{Omega}{\Omega}^2
$$
<Fragment slot="where">$\Omega(R)$ is the circular angular velocity. A star on a slightly eccentric orbit oscillates radially at frequency $\kappa$ while its guiding centre goes round at $\Omega$. For a point mass $\kappa = \Omega$ (the orbit closes: an ellipse). For a uniform sphere $\kappa = 2\Omega$. For a flat rotation curve $\kappa = \sqrt2\,\Omega$.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

In a frame rotating with the guiding centre, the star traces a small ellipse, the **epicycle**, backwards (clockwise when the galaxy turns counter-clockwise). The ellipse's axes are in the ratio $2\Omega/\kappa$. Ptolemy's epicycles were a fitting device for planets; these are real.

</Fragment>
</Tiers>

<Level min={2} title="κ/Ω for any rotation curve">

For a power-law rotation curve $v_c \propto R^{\beta}$ you can check that $\kappa/\Omega = \sqrt{2(1+\beta)}$. At the Sun, the Oort constants measured with Gaia give $\kappa \approx 37$ km/s/kpc and $\Omega \approx 28$ km/s/kpc, so $\kappa/\Omega \approx 1.3$. The Sun's own epicycle carries it about 0.5 kpc in and out of its mean radius every $2\pi/\kappa \approx 170$ Myr, and it bobs through the disk plane vertically every ~70 Myr or so.

</Level>

<Predict question="In a flat rotation curve (κ = √2 Ω), you watch the orbit from a frame rotating at Ω − κ/2 instead of Ω. What does the orbit look like?" choices={['A wider rosette', 'An almost closed oval centred on the galaxy', 'A straight line through the centre']} answer={1}>
In a frame rotating at $\Omega_p$, the star completes one radial oscillation every $2\pi/\kappa$ while its angle advances by $2\pi(\Omega - \Omega_p)/\kappa$. With $\Omega_p = \Omega - \kappa/2$ that is exactly $\pi$: two radial oscillations per turn, so the orbit closes into an oval (an $m = 2$ shape) centred on the galaxy. Because $\Omega - \kappa/2$ is nearly constant over a wide range of radii in real galaxies, a whole family of such ovals can be nested and aligned. That is a bar, or a two-armed spiral. Switch the right-hand frame to "Ω − κ/2" and see.
</Predict>

At the guiding centre's angular velocity (cross), the orbit reduces to a small backwards ellipse, the epicycle. ### Resonances

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
A spiral or a bar is a *pattern* that turns rigidly, like a searchlight, while the stars go round at their own speeds. Where a star's in-and-out wobble keeps time with the pattern sweeping past, the pattern's small pushes add up, like pushing a swing at the right moment. These places are called **resonances**. At **corotation** the stars move with the pattern; at the inner and outer **Lindblad resonances** they meet it once per wobble.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">
A spiral or bar is a pattern that rotates rigidly at a **pattern speed** $\Omega_p$. A star at radius $R$ sees the pattern pass at frequency $m(\Omega - \Omega_p)$ for an $m$-armed pattern. Three radii are special:

- **Corotation** (CR): $\Omega = \Omega_p$. Stars ride along with the pattern.
- **Inner Lindblad resonance** (ILR): $\Omega - \kappa/m = \Omega_p$. The star meets an arm each time it reaches its epicyclic pericentre: pushes add up coherently.
- **Outer Lindblad resonance** (OLR): $\Omega + \kappa/m = \Omega_p$.

</Fragment>
</Tiers>

At resonances, a weak periodic force builds large responses, like pushing a swing in time. Between the ILR and OLR a spiral density wave can propagate; at the resonances it is absorbed. Bars end near their corotation radius, and the Sun sits close to the Milky Way bar's OLR. Gaia DR3's velocity maps of the solar neighbourhood show ridges and moving groups (the Hercules stream is the famous one) sculpted by just these resonances.

<Aside>**Lindblad.** Bertil Lindblad worked out epicyclic orbits and their resonances in the 1920s–60s, and suggested that spiral arms might be kinematic patterns rather than material ones, an idea Lin and Shu made quantitative.</Aside>

## Spiral structure

### The winding problem

Galaxies rotate differentially: at a flat rotation curve, a star at 4 kpc laps one at 16 kpc four times. Suppose a spiral arm were made of a fixed set of stars. After one rotation at the Sun ($2\pi \times 8\,\text{kpc}/220\,\text{km/s} \approx 220$ Myr), the inner parts would be ahead by a full turn relative to the outer parts. After 10 Gyr, about 40 turns: the arm would be wound as tightly as a watch spring. Real spirals have **pitch angles** of 10–30°, loosely wound. So arms cannot be material objects that persist. That is the **winding problem**.

Inside the dashed corotation circle stars overtake the pattern; outside, the pattern overtakes the stars. Change the pitch angle and the corotation radius.

### Density waves: the Lin–Shu hypothesis

<History title="A traffic jam in the sky" year="1964">
<Portrait src="/img/people/lin-chia-chiao.jpg" name="Chia-Chiao Lin" years="1916–2013" credit="Unknown photographer, 1940s–50s" license="Public domain" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chia-Chiao_Lin.jpg" />

**C. C. Lin** was an applied mathematician at MIT, famous for his work on hydrodynamic stability and turbulence, when he turned to the winding problem in the early 1960s. With his student **Frank Shu**, he recast Bertil Lindblad's ideas about kinematic spiral waves as the tools of fluid stability theory: a disk of stars could support a *wave* of density with its own pattern speed, and the arms were the crests. Their 1964 paper launched decades of work and some fierce debate. Toomre and others showed that such waves should propagate and die out in a few rotations unless something keeps regenerating them, a problem still not fully settled: in 2026, many astronomers think grand-design spirals are density waves driven by bars and companions, while most flocculent arms are transient, swing-amplified features.
</History>

In 1964, C. C. Lin and Frank Shu proposed that grand-design spiral arms are **density waves**: a quasi-stationary pattern of compression rotating at a single pattern speed, like a traffic jam on a motorway. Cars enter the jam, slow down, crowd together and leave; the jam itself moves slowly and persists while its members change. Stars' epicyclic motions are organised so they crowd where the arms are. The arms contain only perhaps 10–20% more stars than the gaps.

Arms look far more dramatic than that because of the **gas**. Gas, being collisional and cold, is shocked as it enters the arm's potential well, compresses and forms stars. Massive O and B stars live only ~10 Myr, not long enough to drift far out of the arm, so the arms are lit up by young blue stars and pink HII regions ([Chapter 6](/ch/ism/)). An offset between dust lanes (on the inner, upstream edge inside corotation), young clusters and older stars is exactly what the theory predicts, and it is observed.

Density-wave theory also gives a dispersion relation linking the arm spacing to $\kappa$, the surface density and velocity dispersion. It works well for the tightly-wound limit, and "grand-design" two-armed spirals like M51 and M81 (often driven by a companion or a bar) are the best examples. Many galaxies, though, are **flocculent**: patchy, short arm fragments. Modern simulations show that many arms are transient, continually forming, shearing and re-forming. That is where the next idea comes in.

### Toomre's Q

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
Why does a disk form any structure at all? A patch of disk wants to collapse under its own gravity. Two things fight back. The stars' random jiggling puffs small patches apart, and the disk's rotation spins big patches so they can't fall in. Gravity only wins if there is a range of sizes in between that neither defence covers. Alar Toomre boiled this down to a single number, **Q**: roughly "jiggling times spin, divided by how much mass the disk has". If Q is below 1 the disk shatters into clumps. Between about 1 and 2 it holds together but ripples into spiral arms. Well above 2 it stays smooth. Real galaxy disks sit near Q ≈ 1.5: just stable, and full of structure.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">
Why does a disk form any structure at all? A patch of disk of size $\lambda$ collapses under its own gravity unless something resists it. Two things do: random stellar motions ("pressure", which disperses small patches) and rotation (the Coriolis force, which makes large patches spin and stops them collapsing). Gravity wins only if there is a window between the two scales. Working it out properly for a thin disk (Toomre 1964) gives the condition for local stability against axisymmetric collapse:

<KeyEq title="Toomre stability criterion">
$$
\term{Q}{Q} \equiv \frac{\term{sigR}{\sigma_R}\,\term{kappa}{\kappa}}{3.36\,\term{G}{G}\,\term{Sigma}{\Sigma}} > 1
$$
<Fragment slot="where">$\sigma_R$ is the radial velocity dispersion of the stars, $\kappa$ the epicyclic frequency and $\Sigma$ the surface density of the disk. For a gas disk, replace 3.36 by $\pi$ and $\sigma_R$ by the sound speed. $Q \lt 1$: the disk fragments into clumps. $Q \approx 1$–2: it is stable to collapse but responds strongly to perturbations, making spiral arms. $Q \gg 1$: it is smooth and featureless.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

A rough derivation: pressure stabilises scales smaller than the Jeans length $\sigma^2/G\Sigma$; rotation stabilises scales larger than $G\Sigma/\kappa^2$. Collapse is possible only if the first is smaller than the second, $\sigma^2/G\Sigma \lt G\Sigma/\kappa^2$, which is $\sigma\kappa/G\Sigma \lt 1$. The 3.36 comes from doing the calculation carefully.

</Fragment>
</Tiers>

In the solar neighbourhood $\Sigma_\star \approx 35$ M☉/pc², $\sigma_R \approx 35$ km/s and $\kappa \approx 37$ km/s/kpc, giving $Q \approx 1.5$–2. The Milky Way's disk is just stable enough to avoid fragmenting and just unstable enough to be full of structure. That is not a coincidence: a disk with lower $Q$ grows spirals, which scatter stars, raising $\sigma$ and $Q$, until it settles near marginal stability. Galaxy disks self-regulate.

<Predict question="A disk with Q = 1.2 is stable according to Toomre's criterion. What will you see when you run it?" choices={['A perfectly smooth disk', 'Spiral arms that come and go', 'Immediate fragmentation into clumps']} answer={1}>
Toomre's $Q > 1$ guarantees stability only against **axisymmetric** (ring-like) collapse. Non-axisymmetric perturbations get a boost the criterion ignores: as differential rotation shears a leading wavelet into a trailing one, it spends a little time nearly radial, where self-gravity, shear and epicyclic motion conspire to amplify it by a large factor. This **swing amplification** (Goldreich & Lynden-Bell 1965; Julian & Toomre 1966; Toomre 1981) turns the particle noise of the disk into a pattern of multi-armed, trailing, transient spirals. Only above $Q \approx 2$ does it fade.
</Predict>

### Bars

About two-thirds of disk galaxies, the Milky Way included, have a **bar**: a rigidly rotating elongated structure made of stars on elongated orbits (the $x_1$ family) aligned with it. In the late 1960s and early 1970s, the first N-body disk simulations had an embarrassing habit: they all formed bars within a rotation or two. Ostriker and Peebles (1973) argued that real disks must be stabilised by a massive, hot, **dark halo**, one of the earliest theoretical arguments for dark matter. The modern view is subtler: halos exchange angular momentum with bars through resonances, and can even make them grow (Athanassoula 2002). The Milky Way's bar is about 4.5–5 kpc long and turns with $\Omega_p \approx 35$–40 km/s/kpc (from Gaia and APOGEE). Bars funnel gas to the centre, fuelling starbursts and black holes ([Chapter 23](/ch/galaxy-formation/)), and thicken over time into the "boxy/peanut" bulge we see in the Milky Way's infrared images.

<Aside>**JWST bars.** JWST has found barred disks at redshift $z \approx 3$ and beyond, less than 2 Gyr after the Big Bang, earlier than many simulations predicted. Cold, massive disks must have settled surprisingly early.</Aside>

## When galaxies collide

Stars in a galaxy never collide, but galaxies do, often. Galaxies are separated by only ~20–100 times their own diameters (compare stars: $10^7$ diameters), and they are pulled together by their dark halos. The Local Group is falling together right now.

### Dynamical friction

<History title="Chandrasekhar's drag" year="1943">
<Portrait src="/img/people/chandrasekhar.jpg" name="Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar" years="1910–1995" credit="NASA" license="Public domain" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Subrahmanyan_Chandrasekhar.gif" />

**Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar** had a habit of mastering a field completely, writing the definitive book, and moving on. After white dwarfs ([Chapter 16](/ch/stellar-death/)), he spent the early 1940s at Yerkes Observatory on stellar dynamics, treating a star in a cluster like a particle in Brownian motion. In 1943 he showed that the random pulls from the other stars have a systematic part: a drag opposite to the star's motion, proportional to its mass. He meant it for stars in clusters. Its greatest application came decades later, when it explained why satellite galaxies sink, why galaxies merge and why the LMC's days are numbered. The same formula runs inside the flagship simulation below, pulling the two dark halos together.
</History>

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
A heavy object moving through a sea of lighter ones pulls them towards its path as it passes. They bunch up in a **wake** behind it, like the wake behind a boat, and the wake's gravity pulls the object back. It slows down and sinks towards the centre. The heavier the object, the bigger its wake and the faster it sinks. A satellite galaxy far out in a big halo can take many billions of years; a heavy one close in, only a few hundred million.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">
A massive body $M$ moving through a sea of lighter bodies pulls them towards its path. They pile up in a gravitational **wake** behind it, and the wake pulls back on $M$. Chandrasekhar (1943) worked out the drag; its most useful consequence is how long a satellite on a circular orbit takes to spiral into the centre of a dark halo with a flat rotation curve $v_c$:

$$
\term{tfric}{t_\text{fric}} \approx \frac{1.17}{\term{lnL}{\ln\Lambda}}\,\frac{\term{r0}{r_0}^2\, \term{vc}{v_c}}{\term{G}{G}\,\term{Msat}{M}}
$$
<Fragment slot="where">$r_0$ is the starting radius, <span data-term="Msat">$M$</span> the satellite's mass, $v_c$ the halo's circular speed and $\ln\Lambda$ the "Coulomb logarithm", a slowly varying number of 2–10. Heavy satellites sink fast; double the distance and it takes four times longer.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>
</Fragment>

The body slows down and sinks. Chandrasekhar (1943) computed the drag for a uniform background of density $\rho$ with a Maxwellian velocity distribution of dispersion $\sigma$:

<KeyEq title="Chandrasekhar dynamical friction">
$$
\frac{d\term{vM}{\vec v_M}}{d\term{time}{t}} = -\frac{4\pi \term{G}{G}^2 \term{Msat}{M} \term{rhobg}{\rho} \term{lnL}{\ln\Lambda}}{\term{vM}{v_M}^3}\left[\operatorname{erf}(\term{Xr}{X}) - \frac{2\term{Xr}{X}}{\sqrt\pi}e^{-\term{Xr}{X}^2}\right]\term{vM}{\vec v_M}, \qquad \term{Xr}{X} = \frac{\term{vM}{v_M}}{\sqrt2\,\term{sigv}{\sigma}}
$$
<Fragment slot="where"><span data-term="Msat">$M$</span> is the mass of the sinking body, $\vec v_M$ its velocity relative to the background, $\rho$ the background density and $\ln\Lambda \approx \ln(b_\text{max}v^2/GM)$ the Coulomb logarithm, typically 2–10. The drag is proportional to $M$ (not to the mass of the background particles): heavy objects sink faster. For fast objects it falls as $1/v^2$.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

The friction force scales as $M^2$, so the acceleration scales as $M$. In an isothermal halo, a satellite on a circular orbit at radius $r_0$ spirals in on the timescale

$$
\term{tfric}{t_\text{fric}} = \frac{1.17}{\term{lnL}{\ln\Lambda}}\frac{\term{r0}{r_0}^2 \term{vc}{v_c}}{\term{G}{G}\term{Msat}{M}}.
$$

</Fragment>
</Tiers>

The Large Magellanic Cloud ($M \approx 1$–$2\times10^{11}$ M☉ including its own dark halo, at 50 kpc) gives $t_\text{fric} \approx 1$–2 Gyr: it will merge with the Milky Way in about 2.5 Gyr (Cautun et al. 2019). A globular cluster of $10^5$ M☉ at 5 kpc takes much longer than the age of the Universe. Dynamical friction is also what makes galaxies merge at all: two halos passing through each other lose orbital energy to each other's dark matter.

### Tidal tails and bridges

In 1972, Alar and Juri Toomre ran a remarkably simple computer experiment. They modelled each galaxy as a point mass surrounded by rings of massless test particles, and sent two such galaxies past each other on parabolic orbits. The long, thin tails and bridges that came out matched the strange "peculiar galaxies" in Arp's atlas: the Antennae (NGC 4038/9), the Mice (NGC 4676), the Whirlpool with its companion (M51). Tidal features, they showed, are not ejected by explosions but are purely gravitational.

<History title="Two brothers and a CDC 6600" year="1972">
**Alar Toomre** (MIT) and his brother **Juri Toomre** (then at NYU) wanted to know whether the bizarre filaments in Halton Arp's *Atlas of Peculiar Galaxies* were the relics of explosions or magnetic fields, as many believed, or just gravity. Their "restricted" model was deliberately minimal: two point masses on parabolic orbits, each surrounded by a few rings of massless test particles, integrated on a mainframe with output drawn by plotter. The pictures that came out matched the Antennae and the Mice, down to the curvature of the tails. Their paper also made a bold suggestion: that such mergers, far more frequent in the past, turn spirals into ellipticals. It took two decades of better simulations to confirm it.
</History>

The mechanism is tidal. The companion's pull differs across the disk: stronger on the near side, weaker on the far side. Stars on the near side are pulled into a **bridge** towards the companion; stars on the far side are left behind and flung into a **tail**. The effect is dramatic when the encounter is **prograde**, that is, the disk spins in the same sense as the companion's orbit. Then, for a while, the disk's stars move alongside the companion and feel its pull in resonance, for much longer than a passing glance. Retrograde disks barely notice. Tails are long because stars at the edge of the disk have the least binding energy and the largest lever arm.

<Hood title="a GPU N-body code for galaxy mergers">
**What is simulated.** Each galaxy has a *live* exponential stellar disk ($M_d = 5\times10^{10}$ M☉, scale length 3 kpc, sampled with 32k–512k particles), a Hernquist bulge and a Hernquist dark halo ($1.5\times10^{12}$ M☉, $a = 36$ kpc). The halos and bulges are *rigid* analytic potentials attached to two centres, whose orbit is integrated on the CPU in double precision, with the Chandrasekhar formula supplying the friction between the halos.

**Why rigid halos?** A live halo is the "right" answer, but to keep the halo's graininess from heating the disk it needs roughly 5–10 times more particles than the disk, and its self-gravity costs $O(N^2)$ (or a tree). A rigid halo is free, perfectly smooth and quiet, and dynamical friction, the one important thing a live halo does in a merger, is put back analytically. The price: the halos do not deform, no momentum flows between disk and halo, and the final remnant's halo is an unrealistic superposition. For showing tails, bridges, rings and the merger sequence, which are set by the disk stars' response to the tide, that is an excellent trade (the same one Toomre & Toomre made, with far more fidelity).

**Force calculation.** A contiguous prefix of the disk particles (1k–8k "sources") carries the disk mass; every particle feels every source through direct summation, plus the analytic potentials. The work is $N \times N_\text{src}$: at 128k particles and 4k sources that is $5\times10^8$ interactions per step. The kernel is **tiled**: a workgroup of 256 threads loads 256 sources into fast on-chip shared memory with one coalesced read each, synchronises, and then every thread accumulates 256 interactions from that tile before loading the next. Each source is fetched from main memory once per workgroup instead of once per thread, so the kernel is limited by arithmetic, not bandwidth.

```wgsl
var<workgroup> tile : array<vec4f, WG>;

@compute @workgroup_size(WG)
fn force(@builtin(global_invocation_id) gid : vec3u, @builtin(local_invocation_index) li : u32) {
  let i = min(gid.x, P.n - 1u);           // idle threads still join the barriers
  let p = pos[i].xyz;
  var a = vec3f(0.0);
  for (var base = 0u; base < P.nSrc; base += WG) {
    tile[li] = pos[base + li];            // xyz + mass
    workgroupBarrier();
    for (var j = 0u; j < WG; j++) {
      let q = tile[j];
      let d = q.xyz - p;
      let inv = inverseSqrt(dot(d, d) + P.eps2);   // Plummer softening
      a += d * (q.w * inv * inv * inv);
    }
    workgroupBarrier();
  }
  a += rigid(p);                          // two Hernquist halos + bulges
  ...
}
```

**Softening and noise.** Forces are Plummer-softened, $\vec a = Gm\,\vec d/(d^2 + \epsilon^2)^{3/2}$ with $\epsilon = 360$ pc, which removes the $1/d^2$ singularity so close pairs cannot produce huge kicks. The source subset is a Monte Carlo estimate of the disk's field, and its noise causes exactly the two-body relaxation of the first section: with $N_\text{src} = 4096$ and $\epsilon$ this large, $t_\text{relax}$ is several Gyr, comparable to the run. That is the real limitation of this design, and why production codes use $10^6$–$10^{10}$ particles with tree or particle-mesh gravity.

**Integration.** Kick–drift–kick leapfrog (Chapter 2) with a fixed step $\Delta t = 0.55$ Myr, split into two dispatches per step (half-kick and drift; then force and half-kick). An orbit at 3 kpc has a period of about 90 Myr, so ~160 steps per orbit; the step is also small compared to $\epsilon/v \approx 1.3$ Myr, the time to cross a softening length. Several steps are encoded per frame in one command buffer, each reading its own halo positions through a dynamic uniform offset.

**Precision.** Everything on the GPU is `f32`. Units are chosen so the numbers are $O(1)$: $G = 1$, length 3 kpc, mass $5\times10^{10}$ M☉, so velocity 268 km/s and time 11 Myr. With a 7-digit mantissa, positions up to ~100 units keep ~$10^{-5}$ relative resolution, far below the softening. Leapfrog's energy error stays bounded rather than drifting; the round-off of $10^{3}$-term f32 sums is at the $10^{-6}$ level and is random, so it is swamped by the particle noise above. The halo centres, whose orbit decides *when* things happen, are integrated in f64 on the CPU.

**Why not Barnes–Hut?** A tree reduces the cost to $O(N\log N)$ and is the right tool for $10^6$+ gravitating particles, but on a GPU it means rebuilding an octree every step (sorting by Morton code, building nodes, computing multipoles) and then walking it with divergent, pointer-chasing threads. For $N_\text{src} \lesssim 10^4$ the brute-force tile loop is simpler, branch-free, and on an M1-class GPU it runs a 128k-particle step in about 3 ms. Rendering is instanced quads with additive blending into a 16-bit float target, followed by a tonemapping pass ($1 - e^{-x}$), so dense cores saturate gracefully to white instead of clipping.
</Hood>

### Mergers make ellipticals

Watch a merger to the end. The second and third passages come quickly, and the two disks dissolve into one smooth, roundish, featureless blob. That is how giant **elliptical galaxies** form: Toomre (1977) proposed it, and simulations since (Barnes & Hernquist 1992, and today's cosmological runs) confirm it.

How can a collisionless system "forget" its initial ordered rotation? During the merger the potential itself fluctuates wildly on the crossing timescale. A star's energy is conserved only in a *static* potential; in a time-varying one<Level min={3} inline> ($dE/dt = m\,\partial\Phi/\partial t$)</Level> it is not, and stars gain or lose energy essentially at random. Lynden-Bell (1967) called this **violent relaxation**. It mixes stars in phase space within a few crossing times, far faster than two-body relaxation, and independent of stellar mass (unlike two-body relaxation, which makes heavy stars sink). The result has a characteristic de Vaucouleurs $R^{1/4}$ surface-brightness profile, like observed ellipticals.

<Aside>**The gas.** Real mergers also have gas, which, being collisional, loses angular momentum in shocks and falls to the centre. That triggers starbursts (the Antennae are forming stars at ~20 M☉ per year) and feeds the central black holes, which may merge (the gravitational waves of Chapter 20 in the supermassive regime, detected by pulsar timing arrays in 2023).</Aside>

Tidal tails carry stars far from the remnant, some falling back over billions of years as shells and streams. Deep images from surveys like Euclid and Rubin/LSST reveal faint shells and streams around a large fraction of nearby massive galaxies, fossils of past mergers. The Milky Way itself swallowed the Gaia–Sausage–Enceladus dwarf about 8–10 Gyr ago; Gaia found its debris in 2018 as a population of stars on strongly radial orbits.

### The Milky Way meets Andromeda

Andromeda (M31) is 780 kpc away and approaching at 110 km/s. Whether it will hit us depends on its *sideways* velocity, which requires measuring its proper motion of a few tens of micro-arcseconds per year. HST (van der Marel et al. 2012) found a small transverse velocity and predicted a head-on-ish collision in ~4 Gyr, with the merger complete in ~6 Gyr. Gaia (van der Marel et al. 2019) found a somewhat larger transverse velocity (~57 km/s), and a 2025 study (Sawala et al.) that included the LMC and M33 found only about a 50% chance of a merger within the next 10 Gyr. The masses of the two halos are uncertain by a factor of about two, and the outcome is sensitive to them.

In the "Milky Way – Andromeda" scenario, the simulation starts today from 780 kpc with a closing speed of 110 km/s; the halo centres are fast-forwarded until they are 150 kpc apart, and then the disks are built and the collision is followed. With the default transverse velocity, the first close passage happens a little over 4 Gyr from now and the merger completes 2–3 Gyr later. Increase the transverse velocity to ~100 km/s and the galaxies miss wide the first time. The Sun, for what it is worth, will be fine: no star will collide with another, although the Solar System may be thrown into the merger remnant's outskirts. The remnant, a giant elliptical sometimes nicknamed "Milkomeda", will be the Local Group's dominant galaxy.

## Where we go from here

Galaxies turn out to be collisionless fluids in six-dimensional phase space. Stars move in a smooth potential; their orbits are rosettes, their small oscillations epicycles; resonances between those oscillations and rotating patterns make bars and spiral density waves; a disk's $Q$ decides whether it fragments, spirals or stays calm. When galaxies meet, tides pull out tails and bridges, dynamical friction drags the halos together, and violent relaxation scrambles two ordered disks into an elliptical.

All of this rested on a silent assumption: that we know where the mass is. The rotation curves, Toomre's bar instability, the timing of mergers and Zwicky's virial masses all point to the same conclusion: most of it is not in stars. The next chapters follow that thread: the dark matter that makes up the halos we have treated as rigid spheres, and how halos and galaxies grew together from tiny fluctuations in the early Universe.
