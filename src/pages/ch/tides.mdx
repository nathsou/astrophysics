## Gravity has a gradient

<Photo src="/img/tides/saturn-rings.jpg" alt="Saturn and its full ring system in natural colour, seen nearly edge-on to broadside, mosaicked from over a hundred Cassini images" credit="NASA/JPL/Space Science Institute" license="Public domain" href="https://images.nasa.gov/details/PIA06193" size="wide" aspect="16 / 9">
**Saturn, October 2004.** This 126-frame Cassini mosaic is the picture this whole chapter is trying to explain: a ring system, not one more moon, sitting almost entirely inside Saturn's Roche limit — and shepherded, gapped and warped by resonances with Saturn's moons.
</Photo>

[Chapter 2](/ch/orbits/) treated planets and moons as points: gravity pulls the *centre of mass* toward another *centre of mass*, full stop. Real bodies have size, and gravity is stronger on the near side of a body than on the far side. That difference — the **tidal force** — is what this chapter is about. It is not a new force; it's ordinary Newtonian gravity, but *differenced* across an extended body rather than evaluated at a point.

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
Picture a body — an ocean, a moon, a star — sitting near something massive. Gravity pulls harder on the near side than on the far side, simply because the near side is closer. That mismatch *stretches* the body along the line to the perturber. It's not a new force, just the same gravity pulling unevenly across something that has size.

Squeeze a body from the sides while stretching it end-to-end and it puffs out into two bulges: one facing the perturber, one facing directly away. Both bulges are real, and both are pulled *up*, not "left behind" — the far one just gets pulled toward the perturber a little *less* than the body's centre does, so relative to the centre it lags outward too.

The strength of this stretch falls off very fast with distance — faster than ordinary gravity — so it is almost always the *closest* massive neighbour that matters, not the most massive one.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">
Put a perturber of mass $M$ a distance $d$ from the centre of an extended body of radius $R \ll d$. Gravity is slightly stronger on the near side and slightly weaker on the far side than at the centre; subtracting off the centre's own acceleration leaves a small leftover field, the **tidal field**, that stretches the body along the line to the perturber and squeezes it side-on:

<KeyEq title="The tidal acceleration (leading order, scalar form)">
$$
a_\text{tidal,axis} = \frac{2\term{G}{G}\term{M}{M}\term{rpar}{r}}{\term{d}{d}^3}, \qquad a_\text{tidal,transverse} = -\frac{G\term{M}{M}\term{rperp}{r}}{d^3}
$$
<Fragment slot="where"><span data-term="rpar">r</span> is the distance from the body's own centre, along (<span data-term="rpar">$r_\parallel$</span>) or across (<span data-term="rperp">$r_\perp$</span>) the line to the perturber, <span data-term="M">M</span> is the perturber's mass and <span data-term="d">d</span> the separation. Both scale as $1/d^3$ — one power steeper than ordinary gravity's $1/d^2$ — which is why tides fall off so fast with distance and why a close neighbour usually beats a far, more massive one.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

Because the field stretches along the axis and compresses across it **on both sides**, a deformable body grows **two** bulges — one toward the perturber, one away from it — not one. The far bulge is not "left behind"; it is pulled up by the same physics as the near bulge, just pulled toward the perturber a little less than the centre is.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l3">
Put a perturber of mass $M$ a distance $d$ from the centre of an extended body of radius $R \ll d$. A point on the body's surface, displaced by $\vec r$ from the centre (with $|\vec r| = R$), feels

$$
\vec g(\vec r) = -\frac{GM(\vec d - \vec r)}{|\vec d - \vec r|^3} \, ,
$$

where $\vec d$ points from the body's centre to the perturber. Expand this in powers of $r/d$ — the first term is the mean field that produces ordinary orbital acceleration of the centre of mass (it does no *differential* work); the next term, first order in $r/d$, is the tidal field:

<KeyEq title="The tidal acceleration field (leading order)">
$$
\vec a_\text{tidal}(\vec r) = \frac{G\term{M}{M}}{\term{d}{d}^3}\big(2\,\term{rpar}{r_\parallel}\,\term{dhat}{\hat d} \;-\; \term{rperp}{r_\perp}\,\hat\perp\big)
$$
<Fragment slot="where"><span data-term="rpar">$r_\parallel$</span> is the component of $\vec r$ along the body–perturber line <span data-term="dhat">$\hat d$</span>; <span data-term="rperp">$r_\perp$</span> is the component transverse to it. The along-axis term stretches (coefficient $+2GMr/d^3$); the transverse term compresses (coefficient $-GMr/d^3$). Both scale as $1/d^3$, one power steeper than the $1/d^2$ monopole force — this is why tides fall off so fast with distance and why close encounters matter so much more than distant ones. New to vectors or gradients? See [Appendix A3](/ch/primer-vectors/) and [A4](/ch/primer-calculus/).</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

The two coefficients aren't independent: the tidal field is (minus) the gradient of a potential, $\vec a_\text{tidal} = -\nabla \Phi_\text{tidal}$, with $\Phi_\text{tidal}(\vec r) = -\frac{GM}{d^3}\left(r_\parallel^2 - \tfrac12 r_\perp^2\right)$, and $\nabla^2\Phi_\text{tidal}=0$ away from any mass. A traceless, harmonic quadrupole is the generic leading-order shape of *any* differential force field — the same mathematics reappears for gravitational-wave strain in [Chapter 20](/ch/gravitational-waves/).

Because the field stretches along the axis and compresses transverse to it **on both sides**, a deformable body raised by tides grows **two** bulges — one facing the perturber, one facing away — not one. This is the single most common tidal misconception to clear up early: the far bulge is not "left behind" or "flung out"; it is pulled *up* by the same physics as the near bulge, just because the far side is pulled toward the perturber *less* than the centre is.
</Fragment>
</Tiers>

<Level min={3} title="Deriving the factor of 2">
Along the axis, $g(d-r) - g(d) \approx \frac{d}{dr}\!\left[-\frac{GM}{(d-r)^2}\right]_{r=0} \cdot (-r)$… work through the sign and you get $+2GMr/d^3$ pointing *away* from the centre on both the near and far side. Perpendicular to the axis, geometry (the two lines from the perturber to $\pm r_\perp$ converge slightly) gives the compressive $-GMr/d^3$. The two are related by $\nabla\!\cdot\!\vec a_\text{tidal}=0$ in vacuum (Laplace's equation for the potential), so stretching one way must compress the other by exactly half.
</Level>

<Sim name="tides-field" minHeight={420} params={{ distance: '3' }}>
**The tidal field around a body.** Arrows show the differential acceleration relative to the body's centre — note the two bulge directions (toward and away from the perturber) and the compression at 90°. Drag the perturber, or use the sliders, and watch the arrows shrink as $1/d^3$: pull the perturber twice as far away and the field drops by a factor of eight.
</Sim>

<Predict question="A perturber's distance doubles. By what factor does the tidal acceleration on a fixed body drop?" choices={['2×', '4×', '8×']} answer={2}>
Ordinary gravity drops by $2^2=4$. But the *tidal* term is one derivative steeper, $\propto 1/d^3$, so doubling the distance drops it by $2^3=8$. This single power of $d$ is why the Moon (much less massive than the Sun, but 400× closer) dominates Earth's tides, and why a spacecraft can orbit safely just outside a planet's Roche limit while one a little closer is torn apart.
</Predict>

## Ocean tides: the Moon versus the Sun

Earth's oceans respond almost in real time to this field. As Earth rotates under the (roughly fixed, over a day) tidal bulge raised by the Moon, a given point on the coast passes through high tide roughly twice per lunar day (24 h 50 min) — once under the near bulge, once under the far one.

The Sun raises tides too. Compare their strengths using $a_\text{tidal} \propto M/d^3$:

$$
\frac{a_\odot}{a_\text{Moon}} = \frac{M_\odot}{M_\text{Moon}}\left(\frac{d_\text{Moon}}{d_\odot}\right)^3 = (2.7\times10^{7})\times\left(\frac{3.84\times10^5}{1.496\times10^{8}}\right)^3 \approx 0.46.
$$

<Aside>**Numbers.** $M_\odot/M_\text{Moon}\approx 2.7\times10^7$; the Sun is about 390× farther than the Moon, and $390^3 \approx 5.9\times10^7$. The ratio of these two huge numbers lands close to $1/2$ — a coincidence of the current era, since the Moon is slowly receding (more below).</Aside>

So solar tides are about **46% as strong** as lunar tides, despite the Sun's overwhelming mass. When the Sun and Moon pull along the same line (new and full Moon), their bulges add: **spring tides**, roughly $1+0.46\approx 1.46\times$ the lunar-only range. When they pull at right angles (quarter Moon), the Sun's stretch partially fills in the Moon's compression: **neap tides**, roughly $1-0.46\approx 0.54\times$. The spring/neap ratio of about **2.7 : 1**, better known to sailors than to astronomers is a direct, checkable consequence of the $1/d^3$ law and the specific numbers above.

<Fermi title="Why is the ratio close to 1/2, not 1/1000?">
Naively the Sun's gravity on Earth is $\sim(M_\odot/M_\text{Moon})/(d_\odot/d_\text{Moon})^2 \approx 2.7\times10^7/1.5\times10^5 \approx 180\times$ stronger than the Moon's — which is true, and is why Earth orbits the Sun and not the Moon. But *tides* care about the gradient, which trades one more power of distance for the mass ratio: $180 \times (d_\text{Moon}/d_\odot) \approx 180 \times 0.0026 \approx 0.46$. Every extra power of $1/d$ you add to a force law suppresses distant sources relative to near ones — which is exactly why the tidal field, and not the orbital field, is dominated by whatever is *close*, regardless of mass.
</Fermi>

## Tidal locking

Ocean tides dissipate energy through friction (turbulence, seafloor drag) — and a *solid* body being flexed by a changing tidal field dissipates energy too, through internal friction. Dissipation always acts to reduce free energy, and for a spinning, orbiting pair the available free energy (for fixed angular momentum — new to angular momentum and conservation laws? see [Appendix A6](/ch/primer-mechanics/)) is minimised when spin and orbit are **synchronised**: the same face permanently toward the perturber, zero relative angular velocity between the tidal bulge and the line to the perturber, hence zero flexing, hence zero further dissipation. That end state is **tidal locking**.

The Moon is tidally locked to Earth: it rotates once per orbit, so the same hemisphere always faces us (the famous "far side" is simply the side we never see from Earth, not a permanently dark side — it gets just as much sunlight). The timescale for locking scales steeply with distance:

<KeyEq title="Tidal synchronisation timescale (order of magnitude)">
$$
\term{tausync}{\tau_\text{sync}} \sim \frac{\term{omega0}{\omega_0}\, m\, \term{d}{d}^6}{3\, \term{k2Q}{k_2 Q^{-1}}\, G\, \term{M}{M}^2\, \term{R}{R}^5}
$$
<Fragment slot="where"><span data-term="omega0">$\omega_0$</span> is the initial spin rate, $m$ and <span data-term="M">M</span> the synchronising body's and perturber's mass, <span data-term="R">R</span> the synchronising body's radius, <span data-term="d">d</span> the separation, and <span data-term="k2Q">$k_2/Q$</span> a dimensionless combination of the body's *Love number* $k_2$ (how easily it deforms) and *tidal quality factor* $Q$ (how lossy that deformation is — small $Q$ means strong dissipation, fast locking). The steep $d^6$ and $R^{-5}$ dependence is why small, close moons lock almost instantly (in astronomical terms) while distant, small ones may never lock at all.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

Nearly every large moon in the solar system is tidally locked to its planet — our Moon, all the large moons of Jupiter and Saturn. Mercury is the interesting exception that proves the rule works: it is **not** in a 1:1 lock with the Sun, but a **3:2 spin–orbit resonance** — it rotates three times for every two orbits. This happens because Mercury's orbit is fairly eccentric ($e \approx 0.206$); the tidal torque, which depends on the (rapidly varying) instantaneous distance, is strongest near perihelion, and for an eccentric orbit the time-averaged torque can lock the spin at a half-integer multiple of the mean motion rather than exactly 1:1. The 3:2 state is a stable equilibrium for Mercury's eccentricity; the exact-synchronous state also exists mathematically but Mercury's early spin history landed it in the 3:2 basin instead.

<Sim name="tides-spinlock" minHeight={420} params={{ e: '0.206' }}>
**Spin–orbit locking.** An elongated body (the white dot marks one end of its long axis) spins on an eccentric orbit. Two torques act on it: a dissipative tidal torque, $\propto (\omega - \dot\theta)/r^6$ and so strongest at periapsis, that drags the spin toward the orbital angular rate; and the star's pull on the body's permanent bulge, $\propto \sin 2(\phi-\theta)/r^3$, which can trap it in a resonance. It starts at Mercury's eccentricity (0.206), spinning 1.7 times per orbit, and spins down until it locks into 3:2, Mercury's actual state. Drop the eccentricity below about 0.1 and it settles into 1:1 instead, facing the star like the Moon. Start it spinning fast (3× or more) and capture becomes a matter of luck: it may be caught at 2:1 or 3:2, or sail through into 1:1, just as Mercury's own capture was a roll of the dice. Both torques are exaggerated enormously, so locking takes tens of orbits rather than millions of years.
</Sim>

## Tidal dissipation, dissected: the Earth–Moon system

Locking one side (the Moon) is not the end of the story, because Earth itself is *not* locked to the Moon — Earth's day (24 h) is far shorter than the Moon's orbital period (27.3 d). Earth's spin drags its tidal bulge slightly *ahead* of the Earth–Moon line (friction takes time to relax the bulge back into alignment). That misaligned bulge pulls on the Moon with a small forward component of force — the same mechanism that would eventually lock Earth's spin to the Moon's orbit too, if the Sun doesn't disrupt things first (it will, in a few billion years, as Earth's oceans evaporate under a brightening Sun).

<Sim name="tides-recession" minHeight={420}>
**The lag angle and its torque.** Because Earth spins faster than the Moon orbits, friction drags the ocean bulge ahead of the Earth–Moon line by a lag angle δ (about 3° today, the default). The near bulge's pull on the Moon then has a small forward component, drawn magnified: it speeds the Moon up so it spirals outward, while the Moon's equal and opposite pull brakes Earth's spin. The readouts scale today's measured rates by sin 2δ. Drag δ to zero and the torque vanishes; drag it up and both rates climb.
</Sim>

That forward tug does two things simultaneously, both required by conservation of angular momentum for the Earth–Moon system as a whole:

1. **The Moon gains orbital angular momentum** and spirals *outward*, currently at **3.8 cm/yr** (measured directly, to millimetre precision, by timing the round-trip of laser pulses bounced off retroreflectors the Apollo astronauts left on the surface).
2. **Earth loses spin angular momentum** and its rotation slows, lengthening the day by about **2 ms per century**.

<Fermi title="How far was the Moon, 400 million years ago?">
Extrapolating linearly (a real approximation — the true history involves resonances in ocean-basin geometry that changed as continents drifted, so the rate wasn't always 3.8 cm/yr) back $4\times10^8$ years at 3.8 cm/yr moves the Moon inward by $\sim 1.5\times10^7$ m, a few percent of its current $3.84\times10^8$ m orbit. The matching day-length change, $\sim 20\,\text{s}$ per million years $\times 400 = 8000\,\text{s} \approx 2.2$ hours shorter, means a Devonian day was about **21.8 hours**, and a Devonian *year* held about **402 days** — since the year (Earth's orbit around the Sun) barely changes, but the day does. This isn't just theory: fossil coral growth rings (which track daily and annual banding, like tree rings) and Devonian tidal rhythmites independently indicate almost exactly this — around 400 days per year 380–400 million years ago.
</Fermi>

A day 400 million years ago was <Out name="dayLength" />, and a year held <Out name="yearLength" />, when the Moon was <Out name="moonDistance" /> away, extrapolating <Var name="myrAgo" value={400} min={0} max={4000} log={false} /> million years back at today's rates.

<History title="Darwin's son and the shrinking Moon" year="1879">
Charles Darwin's second son, **George Howard Darwin**, trained as a mathematician and turned his attention to a question his father's theory of evolution needed answered indirectly: had the Earth–Moon system always looked as it does now? In 1879 he showed mathematically that tidal friction must be transferring angular momentum from Earth's spin to the Moon's orbit, meaning the Moon has been receding and Earth's day lengthening ever since the two formed — and, extrapolated far enough back, that the Moon could once have been much closer, which fed into (now-superseded) ideas about the Moon splitting from a fast-spinning proto-Earth. The core mechanism — tidal torque transferring angular momentum outward — is exactly the one confirmed a century later by laser ranging and eclipse records.
</History>

<Aside>**Ancient eclipse records.** Babylonian and Chinese astronomers recorded solar eclipses precisely enough that modern astronomers can back-compute where the eclipse path *should* have fallen given a constant day length, compare it to where records place it, and directly measure the accumulated day-length drift over 2,700 years — one of the longest-baseline measurements in all of science, and one of the most direct confirmations of tidal dissipation.</Aside>

## Io: a moon kept molten by resonance

Tidal dissipation doesn't only slow spins — inside a body, it generates real heat, proportional (very roughly) to $\dot E \sim \dfrac{GM^2 R^5}{Q\,d^6}\,e^2\,n$, where $n$ is the orbital mean motion. Jupiter's innermost large moon, **Io**, is the most volcanically active body in the solar system, resurfaced continuously by molten silicate (not water-ice) volcanism, entirely powered by this mechanism.

<History title="A resonance found before the moons were understood" year="1784">
<Portrait src="/img/people/laplace.jpg" name="Pierre-Simon Laplace" years="1749–1827" credit="Paulin Guérin" license="Public domain" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pierre-Simon_Laplace.jpg" />

**Pierre-Simon Laplace** proved in 1784 that the near-integer period ratios of Jupiter's moons Io, Europa and Ganymede were not a coincidence but a stable *resonance*: a mutual gravitational lock that keeps $n_\text{Io} - 3n_\text{Europa} + 2n_\text{Ganymede}$ pinned to zero. He worked this out from Newtonian celestial mechanics alone, two centuries before anyone could explain *why* it mattered physically — that a resonance which pumps eccentricity, combined with tidal dissipation that damps it, would keep a moon's interior perpetually flexed and hot. That connection had to wait for Voyager 1's 1979 discovery, days before it arrived at Jupiter, that Io is covered in active volcanoes — the first ever seen beyond Earth.
</History>

The catch: pure tidal circularisation (below) should drive $e\to 0$ quickly, which would shut the heating off. Io stays hot because its eccentricity is *forced* to stay nonzero by the **Laplace resonance**: Io, Europa and Ganymede orbit in a chain where their periods stand in almost exactly the ratio 1:2:4 (equivalently, their mean motions satisfy $n_\text{Io} - 3n_\text{Europa} + 2n_\text{Ganymede} = 0$ to high precision). Each moon periodically lines up with its neighbours at the same orbital phase, and the repeated tug pumps Io's eccentricity back up as fast as tidal dissipation damps it — a steady-state balance between resonant excitation and tidal damping, not a coincidence of initial conditions.

<Photo src="/img/tides/io-volcano.jpg" alt="Io's limb during a Galileo flyby, showing a blue volcanic plume rising over Pillan Patera and, inset, the plume itself and the older Prometheus plume in close-up" credit="NASA/JPL" license="Public domain" href="https://images.nasa.gov/details/PIA00703" aspect="4 / 3">
**A moon caught mid-eruption.** This 1997 Galileo image shows a 140-km plume erupting from Pillan Patera on Io's bright limb (top inset), next to the smaller, long-lived Prometheus plume (bottom inset) — both powered entirely by the tidal flexing the Laplace resonance keeps pumped up, with no help from radioactive decay or a young interior the way Earth's volcanism gets its heat.
</Photo>

The same mechanism, gentler, keeps **Europa's** and possibly **Enceladus's** subsurface oceans liquid: Europa's ice shell floats on a global salt-water ocean sustained partly by tidal flexing, and Enceladus (in a resonance with Dione) vents observed plumes of water ice from its south pole, discovered by Cassini, that are direct evidence of a tidally heated subsurface ocean venting to space. Both moons are prime astrobiology targets for exactly this reason (see [Chapter 10](/ch/exoplanets/) on habitability more broadly).

<Photo src="/img/tides/enceladus-plume.jpg" alt="Enceladus in silhouette against Saturn's dim glow, backlit by the Sun, with a tall fountain of water-ice jets erupting from the south-polar terrain" credit="NASA/JPL/Space Science Institute" license="Public domain" href="https://images.nasa.gov/details/PIA07758" aspect="4 / 3">
**Fountains of Enceladus, 2005.** Backlit by the Sun, Cassini's cameras caught individual jets of water-ice fanning out of the south-polar "tiger stripe" fractures, hundreds of kilometres above a moon barely 500 km across. The plume material feeds Saturn's diffuse E ring — direct, visible proof that a tidally flexed interior is venting a subsurface ocean straight into space.
</Photo>

<Aside>**Circularisation in binary stars.** The same $e\to 0$ damping operates in close binary stars: tidal friction during periastron passage bleeds orbital eccentricity into heat until the orbit is (nearly) circular, on a timescale that again falls steeply with separation. Nearly all binary stars with periods under about a week are observed to be circular for exactly this reason; wider binaries retain their original, often substantial, eccentricities because the tidal torque there is negligible.</Aside>

## The Roche limit

If tides can raise oceans and melt moons from the inside, they can also simply pull a body apart — once the *stretching* tidal force at the surface exceeds the body's own *self-gravity* holding it together.

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
Every self-gravitating body is in a tug-of-war between its own gravity, which holds it together, and any nearby tide, which tries to pull it apart. Move it closer to a big planet and the tide grows (as $1/d^3$) while its own self-gravity doesn't change at all. There is therefore a critical distance — the **Roche limit** — inside which the tide always wins, no matter how the body is held together, so long as it isn't glued by something stronger than gravity itself.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">
Set the tidal stretch at the satellite's surface equal to its own self-gravity there, for a small body of mass $m$, radius $R_m$, density $\rho_m$, orbiting a planet of mass $M$, radius $R_M$, density $\rho_M$, at separation $d$:

$$
\underbrace{\frac{2GM R_m}{d^3}}_{\text{tidal stretch at the moon's surface}} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{Gm}{R_m^2}}_{\text{moon's self-gravity at its surface}}.
$$

Writing both masses in terms of density and radius and solving for $d$ gives the Roche limit below — the satellite's own radius cancels out, so only the *density ratio* matters.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l3">
Set them equal for a small body of mass $m$, radius $R_m$, density $\rho_m$, orbiting a planet of mass $M$, radius $R_M$, density $\rho_M$, at separation $d$:

$$
\underbrace{\frac{2GM R_m}{d^3}}_{\text{tidal stretch at the moon's surface}} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{Gm}{R_m^2}}_{\text{moon's self-gravity at its surface}}.
$$
</Fragment>
</Tiers>

<Level min={3} title="Substituting densities">
Write $M = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi R_M^3\rho_M$ and $m=\tfrac{4}{3}\pi R_m^3\rho_m$, substitute, and the $R_m$ and $G$ cancel cleanly.
</Level>

<KeyEq title="The Roche limit (rigid body)">
$$
\term{dRoche}{d_\text{Roche}} = 1.26\, \term{RM}{R_M}\left(\frac{\term{rhoM}{\rho_M}}{\term{rhom}{\rho_m}}\right)^{1/3}
$$
<Fragment slot="where"><span data-term="RM">$R_M$</span> and <span data-term="rhoM">$\rho_M$</span> are the planet's radius and density; <span data-term="rhom">$\rho_m$</span> is the (rigid) satellite's own density. Notice the satellite's *size* dropped out entirely — only the density ratio matters, because both self-gravity and tidal stretch scale the same way with the body's own radius.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

The factor 1.26 comes from the exact balance of a genuinely rigid sphere. A real fluid or rubble-pile body isn't rigid: as it approaches the planet, it *itself* deforms into a prolate shape (elongated toward the planet), which increases its effective $R_m$ along the critical axis and moves the self-gravity/tide balance outward. Solving the (harder) self-consistent problem for a fluid, self-gravitating ellipsoid gives the commonly quoted:

<KeyEq title="The Roche limit (fluid body)">
$$
\term{dRoche}{d_\text{Roche}} = 2.44\, \term{RM}{R_M}\left(\frac{\term{rhoM}{\rho_M}}{\term{rhom}{\rho_m}}\right)^{1/3}
$$
<Fragment slot="where">Nearly double the rigid-body radius. Real small bodies — comets, rubble-pile asteroids, icy ring particles with no tensile strength — sit closer to this fluid limit than the rigid one, which is why it's the more commonly cited number, and the one used for ring systems below.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

A planet of density $\rho$ (in units of the satellite's own density) has a rigid Roche limit of <Out name="dRigid" /> and a fluid one of <Out name="dFluid" />, for a density ratio <Var name="rho" value={2.2} min={0.3} max={10} log /> — try Saturn/ice ($\rho_M/\rho_m\approx 1.3$) or a rubble-pile comet against a rocky planet ($\rho_M/\rho_m\approx 3$–5).

<History title="A limit derived a century before it was seen" year="1848">
The French astronomer **Édouard Roche** worked out the fluid-body disruption distance in 1848, purely from Newtonian mechanics and a hypothesis about how Saturn's rings might have formed — not from any observation of a body actually being torn apart, since no telescope of the era could have shown one. For well over a century the "Roche limit" was mostly a footnote about why Saturn has rings rather than one more moon.

That changed abruptly in July 1994, when fragments of Comet Shoemaker–Levy 9 — already stretched into Roche's predicted "string of pearls" during a 1992 pass inside Jupiter's Roche limit — struck the planet one after another, live on the world's telescopes. A 146-year-old back-of-the-envelope calculation had just been confirmed on television.
</History>

### Watching disruption happen

<Predict question="A loosely-bound rubble pile (no tensile strength, held together only by its own gravity) crosses just inside the fluid Roche limit on a close flyby. A single close pass inside the Roche limit doesn't have time to fully disperse a body — tidal stretching competes with the body's own (weak) self-gravity over the finite time of the encounter, producing exactly the elongated "string of pearls" that Comet Shoemaker–Levy 9 became after a 1992 pass within Jupiter's Roche limit, before its fragments hit Jupiter in 1994. A body kept permanently at that distance (like ring particles) never gets the chance to re-cohere and is smeared into a ring instead.
</Predict>

<Hood title="the rubble-pile N-body sim">
The flagship runs entirely on the GPU. Each step is a kick-drift-kick leapfrog (exactly the scheme from [Chapter 2](/ch/orbits/)) built from three tiny compute passes plus one heavy one that evaluates direct $O(N^2)$ self-gravity between every pair of the few thousand grains, plus the planet's exact (not linearised) monopole pull — using the real force law rather than the tidal approximation is what lets the same code show both a gentle far-field stretch and a violent close disruption without switching models.

```wgsl
// planet's exact gravity, then softened self-gravity + soft-sphere contacts, 64 grains at a time
var a = -pr.GMp * ri / (rp * rp * rp + 1e-6);
for (var t = 0u; t < n; t = t + WG) {
  tile[lid.x] = pIn[min(t + lid.x, n - 1u)];   // each thread loads one grain into shared memory
  workgroupBarrier();
  for (var k = 0u; k < min(WG, n - t); k = k + 1u) {
    if (t + k == i) { continue; }
    let d = tile[k].pos - ri;
    let r2 = dot(d, d);
    let s2 = r2 + pr.eps2;                      // softened: caps the force at close range
    a = a + pr.Gm * d / (s2 * sqrt(s2));
    let overlap = radius(i) + radius(t + k) - sqrt(r2);
    if (overlap > 0.0 && r2 > 1e-12) {          // spring + dashpot along the contact normal
      let nrm = d * inverseSqrt(r2);
      a = a - (pr.kSpring * overlap - pr.kDamp * dot(tile[k].vel - me.vel, nrm)) * nrm;
    }
  }
  workgroupBarrier();
}
```

**Softening.** Two point-mass grains passing arbitrarily close would blow up the $1/r^2$ force; `eps2` caps it, standing in for the grains' finite size. Too much softening and grains pass through each other with no resistance (the pile never resists compression); too little and a close pass produces an unphysical slingshot that can eject grains at nonsense speeds and destabilise the leapfrog. The soft-sphere contact term is a second, complementary fix: once two grains' softening radii actually overlap, an explicit spring pushes them apart, giving the pile something closer to real granular cohesion rather than a smooth self-gravitating gas.

**Choosing the frame.** The sim integrates in the planet's frame, treated as *inertial* (the planet doesn't move) rather than a frame *rotating* with the pile's orbit. A rotating frame would need explicit centrifugal and Coriolis terms and is the natural choice if you want a body sitting still at a fixed distance; the inertial planet frame is simpler and is exactly what real spacecraft trajectory codes use, since the planet's own barycentric motion is usually negligible over the encounter.

**Timestep: the springs, not the orbit.** The orbit is slow; the stiffest thing in the problem is the contact spring between touching grains, which must be stiff enough that self-gravity squeezes grains by only about a tenth of their radius. Its oscillation period is only a few thousandths of an orbit, and a leapfrog step must resolve it (about 14 steps per spring period here), or energy leaks *into* the pile and it boils apart on its own. So the step is the smaller of 1/800 of an orbit and 1/14 of a spring period — roughly 3,000 steps per orbit — and all of a frame's steps are recorded into one GPU command buffer. A production granular code would use sub-cycling or implicit contacts to escape this limit.

**A flat pile is not a sphere.** The grains live in a plane, and a flat disk pulls on its own rim about 2.2 times harder than a sphere of the same mass and radius would. Left alone, that makes the pile artificially tough (it survives down to about three quarters of the fluid Roche distance), so each grain's mass is scaled down by that factor. The grains also come in a spread of sizes: identical discs lock into a hexagonal crystal that resists shear like a solid, while mixed sizes flow like loose rubble. With both fixes the pile holds together at the fluid Roche limit and comes apart about 10% inside it.

Complexity is $O(N^2)$ per step; at a few thousand grains that's a few million pairwise force evaluations per step and tens of millions per frame, comfortably inside a 60 fps budget on the GPU (each thread reads its partners from fast workgroup memory, 64 at a time, and the inner loop is simple arithmetic with one branch for the contact check) but it would not scale to planetary-ring particle counts (millions) without a tree code or grid-based method — the Barnes–Hut and particle-mesh approaches used for galaxy-scale N-body work in [Chapter 22](/ch/galactic-dynamics/).
</Hood>

## Rings, shepherds and gaps

Saturn's main rings sit almost entirely *inside* Saturn's fluid Roche limit for ice ($\rho_m\approx 0.9\,\text{g/cm}^3$), which is why they're rings and not a moon: any icy body that formed or wandered there would be (or was) tidally shredded into a disk of particles, each too small for its own gravity to matter, orbiting independently on Keplerian orbits that happen to be nearly coplanar and nearly circular.

<Aside>**How old are the rings?** For decades the textbook answer was "as old as Saturn" (4.5 Gyr). Cassini's final (2017) measurements of the rings' mass — smaller than expected — reopened the debate: a low-mass ring erodes and darkens (from micrometeorite pollution) faster than a massive one, so a low mass is easier to reconcile with a *young* ring, perhaps only 100–200 Myr old, maybe from a relatively recent shredded moon or comet. The question remains actively debated as of 2026, and is a clean example of how a single, precise dynamical measurement (ring mass, extracted from Cassini's gravity science during its final, closest orbits) can overturn an assumption an entire field held for decades.</Aside>

Rings aren't structureless. **Shepherd moons** — small moons orbiting just inside or outside a ring edge — confine ring particles via resonant torques: Saturn's Prometheus and Pandora bracket the narrow F ring; Encke and Keeler gaps in the A ring are kept open by Pan and Daphnis, respectively. Saturn's rings also show mysterious radial **spokes**, transient radial markings first captured by Voyager, caused by fine dust particles electrostatically levitated above the ring plane, that appear and fade with Saturn's rotation and, plausibly, its magnetosphere and seasons.

The same resonant logic that confines a shepherded ring edge can instead **clear a gap**, depending on geometry. In the asteroid belt, Jupiter's gravity has swept clean the semi-major axes where an asteroid's orbital period is a simple ratio of Jupiter's (a **mean-motion resonance**): repeated conjunctions at the same orbital phase pump up eccentricity until the asteroid's orbit crosses a planet's or is otherwise perturbed away, over millions of years. These are the **Kirkwood gaps** — most prominently at the 3:1, 5:2 and 2:1 resonances with Jupiter. Saturn's rings show the analogous effect turned up to eleven: the sharp inner edge of the Cassini division is maintained by the 2:1 resonance with the moon Mimas.

Resonances aren't only destabilising, though — the very same mechanism can **lock** a system into long-term stability if the geometry avoids close approaches. Neptune and Pluto orbit in a stable 3:2 mean-motion resonance: Pluto's orbit actually crosses inside Neptune's, but the resonance guarantees they are never simultaneously near the crossing point, so they've never had a close encounter in over 4 billion years despite the orbits geometrically overlapping. The Laplace resonance that keeps Io hot (above) is the same phenomenon: stabilising for the system's long-term architecture, while pumping individual eccentricities that then get dissipated as heat.

Resonant chains turn out to be common, not exceptional, in tightly packed planetary systems. **TRAPPIST-1**, seven Earth-sized planets around an M-dwarf, sit in a remarkable chain of near-resonances (approximately 24:15:9:6:4:3:2 in orbital period ratios, i.e. each adjacent pair close to an 8:5, 5:3, 3:2, 3:2, 4:3 or 3:2 ratio) that is almost certainly a fossil of the system's formation: such tightly packed chains are the natural outcome of planets migrating inward through a gas disk and locking into resonance as they converge, rather than a coincidence assembled after the fact. Exoplanet surveys (Kepler, TESS, and increasingly precise radial-velocity and transit-timing follow-up) have found enough of these resonant chains that they're now a standard test of migration theories.

## Beyond the solar system: tidal disruption of stars

Everything above generalises: swap "rubble-pile moon" for "star" and "planet" for "supermassive black hole," and the Roche-limit physics is identical in spirit, just with relativistic factors and radii that make the encounter almost unrecognisably more violent. A star that wanders within the **tidal disruption radius** of a supermassive black hole is stretched past its self-gravity's ability to hold it together — precisely the same $2GM r/d^3$ versus $Gm/r^2$ balance, now with $M\sim 10^6$–$10^9\,M_\odot$. Roughly half the star's debris falls back onto the black hole over weeks to months, producing a **tidal disruption event (TDE)**: a luminous flare, now routinely found by wide-field time-domain surveys (ZTF, and soon LSST at scale), that briefly lights up an otherwise quiescent galactic nucleus and gives a rare, direct measurement of a black hole's mass and spin. [Chapter 19](/ch/black-holes/) covers the relativistic version of this calculation, including why sufficiently massive black holes swallow stars whole (past the horizon) before they can be tidally disrupted at all.

Zoom out even further and the same stretching operates on galaxies: a close galaxy passage or merger raises **tidal tails** — long streams of stars and gas pulled out by the differential force between a galaxy's near and far side, visible in systems like the Antennae and the Mice, and studied in detail in [Chapter 22](/ch/galactic-dynamics/).

## Where we go from here

One idea — gravity's gradient, not its raw strength — turned out to explain ocean tides and their spring/neap cycle, why the Moon shows one face, why Mercury spins 3:2, why Io is the most volcanic body in the solar system, why some moons stay liquid inside, why Saturn has rings instead of one more moon, why the asteroid belt has gaps, why some resonances stabilise a planetary system for billions of years while others quietly destabilise it — and why the most energetic transients found in modern time-domain surveys are, in the end, just tides taken to their most dramatic conclusion. The next chapter turns to what happens when gravity has *no* small parameter to expand in at all: genuinely chaotic few-body dynamics, where tidal-strength perturbations accumulate until prediction itself breaks down.
