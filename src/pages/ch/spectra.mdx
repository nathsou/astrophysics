## Three laws of light

In 1859, Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen pointed a prism at a gas-discharge lamp and at the Sun, and noticed that the pattern of bright lines from a heated element lined up exactly with the pattern of *dark* lines missing from sunlight at the same wavelengths. That coincidence — codified as **Kirchhoff's three laws of spectroscopy** — is still the operating manual for every spectrograph pointed at the sky:

1. **A hot, dense object emits a continuous spectrum.** A blackbody (Chapter 4) radiates at every wavelength, set only by its temperature.
2. **A hot, low-density gas emits bright lines at wavelengths specific to its atoms.** Electrons cascade down from excited states, each transition emitting a photon of one exact energy.
3. **A cool, low-density gas in front of a hotter continuum source absorbs at those same wavelengths**, carving dark lines into the continuum.

<Aside>**Fraunhofer lines.** Joseph von Fraunhofer catalogued hundreds of dark lines in sunlight in 1814, decades before anyone knew what caused them. He labelled the strongest ones with letters — the Ca II H & K lines and Na D lines in this chapter's flagship figure still carry his labels today.</Aside>

<History title="A salt flame and a rooftop telescope" year="1859">
<Portrait src="/img/people/kirchhoff.jpg" name="Gustav Kirchhoff" years="1824–1887" credit="Unknown author, Smithsonian Libraries" license="Public domain" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gustav_Robert_Kirchhoff.jpg" />

Robert Bunsen (inventor of the burner that bears his name) and Gustav Kirchhoff, both at Heidelberg, built the first spectroscope worth the name by pointing a prism and a small telescope at a Bunsen-burner flame salted with different chemicals. Each element burned with its own unmistakable set of coloured lines. Kirchhoff then aimed the same instrument at sunlight projected through a slit and noticed the dark lines Fraunhofer had already catalogued lined up exactly with the bright emission lines of sodium and iron from the lab. Within months he had the three laws that open this chapter, and Bunsen is said to have joked that they had discovered a way to identify the chemical make-up of the Sun — 150 million kilometres away, with no way of ever bringing back a sample. Bunsen and Kirchhoff went on to discover the elements caesium and rubidium spectroscopically, the first elements ever found by their light rather than in a test tube.
</History>

The lamp strips below show law 2 directly: each element's characteristic set of bright lines, the same lines that would appear dark if you put that gas in front of a hotter source (law 3). No two elements share a pattern — that is precisely why line spectra work as fingerprints.

<Sim name="spectra-emission" size="wide" minHeight={340}>
**Emission spectra of gas-discharge lamps.** Hover a line for its wavelength. Every element's pattern is unique and reproducible: this is literally how spectroscopy identifies elements, in a lab or 10,000 light-years away.
</Sim>

Why does a star, which is mostly hot gas, give an *absorption* spectrum rather than a pure emission spectrum? Because a star is both things at once, stacked in radius. Deep in the photosphere the gas is dense enough to emit a smooth, near-blackbody continuum (law 1). The outer photosphere is cooler and thinner. Photons on their way out pass through it and get preferentially absorbed at each atom's characteristic wavelengths (law 3) before escaping — so what reaches your telescope is a continuum with dark lines punched into it. If you could somehow view *only* the cooler outer gas against a dark background, you would see the same wavelengths in emission. The direction of the temperature gradient, not anything special about "absorption" as a process, is what makes stellar spectra look the way they do.

## The Bohr atom and hydrogen's ladder

Kirchhoff's laws describe *what* happens; explaining the *exact* wavelengths took until 1913, when Niels Bohr proposed that an electron orbiting a nucleus is only allowed at certain radii — those where its angular momentum is an integer multiple of $\hbar$: $L = n\hbar$, $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ (new to quantised angular momentum? see [Appendix A9](/ch/primer-quantum/)).

<History title="Three postulates and a spectral line" year="1913">
<Portrait src="/img/people/bohr.jpg" name="Niels Bohr" years="1885–1962" credit="Bain News Service, Library of Congress" license="Public domain" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Niels_Bohr_-_LOC_-_ggbain_-_35303.jpg" />

Ernest Rutherford's 1911 nuclear atom had a fatal flaw: classical electromagnetism says an orbiting (accelerating) electron should radiate energy continuously and spiral into the nucleus in about $10^{-11}$ seconds. Matter should not exist. A 27-year-old Niels Bohr, working in Rutherford's lab, cut through the problem by simply *declaring* certain orbits stable by fiat — quantising angular momentum — and deriving the hydrogen spectrum from that one postulate. It reproduced the Balmer formula, already known empirically for decades, to the accuracy of the day, and predicted the ultraviolet Lyman series and infrared Paschen series before either had been observed. Bohr himself never claimed to know *why* only those orbits were allowed; that answer had to wait until 1926, when Schrödinger's wave equation replaced Bohr's orbits with probability clouds that happened to give the same energies.
</History>

Set the Coulomb attraction equal to the centripetal force required for a circular orbit of radius $r$ and speed $v$:

$$\frac{k e^2}{r^2} = \frac{m_e v^2}{r}, \qquad k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}.$$

Quantise the angular momentum, $L = m_e v r = n\hbar$, and eliminate $v$ between the two equations to get the allowed radii, $r_n = n^2\hbar^2/(m_e k e^2) \equiv n^2 a_0$. The total energy is kinetic plus potential, $E = \tfrac12 m_e v^2 - ke^2/r$; using the force balance to write $\tfrac12 m_e v^2 = \tfrac12 ke^2/r$ (the virial relation for a $1/r$ potential) gives $E_n = -\tfrac12 ke^2/r_n = -m_e e^4/(2(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2\hbar^2 n^2) = -13.6\,\text{eV}/n^2$. This is quantitatively right for hydrogen — Bohr's model gets the energies exactly correct, though its picture of literal circular orbits is wrong. The real story needs the Schrödinger equation, which reproduces the same $E_n$ but replaces sharp orbits with probability clouds.
</Level>

<Aside>**Beyond one electron.** Multi-electron atoms don't have a clean $1/n^2$ ladder: electron-electron repulsion and different orbital shapes ($s,p,d,f\dots$) split each $n$ into sub-levels with slightly different energies (fine structure splits them further via spin-orbit coupling). The result is thousands of possible lines per element instead of one series — which is exactly why iron alone contributes tens of thousands of lines to the Sun's spectrum, and why real spectral synthesis needs huge atomic-line databases rather than a formula.</Aside>

## Beyond hydrogen: forbidden lines and radio whispers

Heavier atoms and ions produce far richer spectra, but two special cases are worth knowing by name because they show up constantly in astronomy.

**Forbidden lines.** Quantum mechanics' selection rules say most transitions must change angular momentum by exactly one unit and involve an electric-dipole-allowed jump; transitions that violate these rules are not literally impossible, just enormously less probable (lifetimes of seconds to hours instead of nanoseconds). In the dense lab or a stellar photosphere, collisions de-excite an atom in a forbidden state long before it can radiate. But in the exceedingly thin gas of a planetary or supernova-remnant nebula — densities of 10²–10⁶ cm⁻³, collision times of days — there's nothing to stop it, and forbidden lines dominate the spectrum. The green light of many nebulae is **[O III] 500.7 nm** (and its partner 495.9 nm), a transition inside doubly-ionised oxygen so improbable it was originally attributed to a hypothetical element, "nebulium," before quantum mechanics explained it in 1927.

<Photo src="/img/spectra/catseye.jpg" alt="The Cat's Eye Nebula: a bullseye pattern of concentric blue-green shells around a bright white central star, on a dark background" credit="NASA, ESA, HEIC, and The Hubble Heritage Team (STScI/AURA)" license="Public domain" href="https://images.nasa.gov/details/GSFC_20171208_Archive_e002155" size="wide" aspect="1 / 1">
**The Cat's Eye Nebula (NGC 6543), Hubble Space Telescope.** The green glow filling the shells is overwhelmingly the [O III] forbidden line: gas this thin (far less dense than the best laboratory vacuum) lets an otherwise "impossible" transition happen anyway, because nothing collides with the ion fast enough to de-excite it first.
</Photo>

<Aside>**Notation.** Square brackets, as in [O III], mark a forbidden line. The roman numeral is the ionisation stage: O I is neutral oxygen, O II is O⁺, O III is O²⁺. The same convention appears for every element in this chapter (Ca II, Fe I, He II, …).</Aside>

**The 21-cm line.** Neutral hydrogen's ground state has two nearly degenerate sub-levels, split by the tiny magnetic interaction between the electron's and proton's spins (hyperfine structure). Flipping between them releases just $5.9\times10^{-6}\,\text{eV}$ — a photon at 1420.4 MHz, or 21.1 cm, deep in the radio. The transition is so forbidden (spontaneous lifetime ≈ 11 million years) that it only matters at all because interstellar space has so much hydrogen and so much time. It is one of the most important tools in astronomy: it maps neutral gas throughout the Milky Way and other galaxies even where dust blocks visible light entirely, and it traces galaxy rotation curves out to radii where no stars remain to serve as tracers (Chapter 21 and Chapter 6 build on it directly).

## Line widths: broadening and the curve of growth

Real spectral lines are never mathematically thin. Three effects set their shape and width:

**Natural broadening.** The Heisenberg energy-time uncertainty relation, $\Delta E\, \Delta t \gtrsim \hbar$, means a level with a finite lifetime $\tau$ has an energy spread $\Delta E \sim \hbar/\tau$, giving every line an intrinsic **Lorentzian** profile. It is almost always the narrowest contribution and often negligible next to the other two.

**Thermal Doppler broadening.** Atoms in a gas at temperature $T$ have a Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution along the line of sight, and each atom's line is Doppler-shifted by its own velocity. Averaging over the distribution gives a **Gaussian** profile (new to this kind of averaging over a distribution? see [Appendix A7](/ch/primer-thermo/)):

<KeyEq title="Doppler width">
$$
\frac{\term{dlam}{\Delta\lambda}}{\lambda} = \sqrt{\frac{2 k_B \term{Tg}{T}}{\term{mmass}{m}\, c^2}}
$$
<Fragment slot="where"><span data-term="mmass">m</span> is the absorbing atom's mass and <span data-term="Tg">T</span> its temperature. Lighter atoms and hotter gas both broaden lines. This is the *same* physics as the redshift/blueshift of bulk motion (below) — here the "motion" is thermal, and it broadens rather than shifts.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

A hydrogen atom at <Var name="Tdop" value={5800} min={1000} max={40000} log /> K (mass <Var name="massDop" value={1} min={1} max={56} log /> u) gives Hα a Doppler width of <Out name="dopplerWidth" />.

**Pressure (collisional) broadening.** Collisions with neighbouring particles interrupt the emitting/absorbing atom's phase, which — by the same uncertainty argument as natural broadening, but with the collision rate standing in for $1/\tau$ — adds another **Lorentzian** contribution that grows with density (and hence usually with depth in a stellar atmosphere, where deeper is denser).

<Fermi title="How wide is the Sun's Hα line from temperature alone?">
The Sun's photosphere is around 5,800 K. Hydrogen's mass is $1.66\times10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$. Plugging into the Doppler formula:

$$\frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda} = \sqrt{\frac{2(1.38\times10^{-23})(5800)}{(1.66\times10^{-27})(3\times10^8)^2}} \approx 1.5\times10^{-5}$$

At $\lambda = 656\,\text{nm}$ that's $\Delta\lambda \approx 10\,\text{pm}$, or about 5 km/s in Doppler-velocity units. The Sun's *observed* Hα width is several times larger, because pressure broadening and unresolved convective motions ("microturbulence") both add in. Width alone, without a model, only ever gives a lower bound on temperature.
</Fermi>

Since a Gaussian core and Lorentzian wings both contribute, the true line shape is their convolution, the **Voigt profile** — Doppler-dominated near line centre, with Lorentzian power-law wings that dominate far out. Real codes evaluate it via the Faddeeva function (a scaled complex error function); this chapter's sims use the standard **pseudo-Voigt** approximation, a weighted sum of a Gaussian and a Lorentzian of matched width, accurate to a fraction of a percent and far cheaper to evaluate per pixel per line.

<Sim name="spectra-broadening" size="wide" minHeight={380}>
**Natural + Doppler + pressure broadening → Voigt.** Raise the temperature to widen the Gaussian core; raise the electron density to widen the Lorentzian wings. Switch atoms: heavier atoms have narrower thermal cores at fixed $T$.
</Sim>

One more piece completes the picture of a real line: as you add more absorbers, the line first deepens (optically thin), then the core saturates and it mainly grows in the Doppler core (flat part of the **curve of growth**, equivalent width vs. column density), and only once the far Lorentzian wings become optically thick does the equivalent width grow again, slowly, as $\sqrt{N}$. Curve-of-growth analysis is how classical spectroscopists measured chemical abundances before computers could fit whole spectra at once; this chapter's synthetic-spectrum model sidesteps it with a simpler saturating formula (see the flagship's "under the hood" section below).

## Boltzmann, Saha, and the peak of the Balmer lines

To predict how strong a given line should be, you need to know what *fraction* of a given element's atoms are sitting in the right lower energy level of the right ionisation stage to absorb that photon. Two equations, both from statistical mechanics, answer that.

**Excitation: the Boltzmann equation.** Within one ionisation stage, level populations in thermal equilibrium follow (new to the Boltzmann factor? see [Appendix A7](/ch/primer-thermo/)):

<KeyEq title="Boltzmann excitation equation">
$$
\frac{\term{Nb}{N_b}}{\term{Na}{N_a}} = \frac{\term{gb}{g_b}}{\term{ga}{g_a}}\, e^{-\term{dEab}{\Delta E_{ab}}/k_B T}
$$
<Fragment slot="where"><span data-term="Na">$N_a$</span>, <span data-term="Nb">$N_b$</span> are the number of atoms in levels $a$ (lower) and $b$ (upper); <span data-term="ga">$g_a$</span>, <span data-term="gb">$g_b$</span> are their statistical weights (degeneracies, $g_n = 2n^2$ for hydrogen); <span data-term="dEab">$\Delta E_{ab}$</span>$= E_b - E_a > 0$.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

For Hα and Hβ, the relevant lower level is $n=2$, at $\Delta E = 10.2\,\text{eV}$ above the ground state. Even at a scorching 10,000 K, $k_BT \approx 0.86\,\text{eV}$ — thirty times smaller than $\Delta E$ — so only a tiny Boltzmann-suppressed fraction of neutral hydrogen ever sits in $n=2$. That fraction rises steeply with $T$, which is why Balmer lines strengthen as you go from cool to hot stars.

**Ionisation: the Saha equation.** But it also depends on how much of the hydrogen is neutral in the first place. Treat ionisation as a chemical reaction, H $\rightleftharpoons$ H$^+$ + e$^-$, and statistical mechanics (a partition-function ratio, with the free electron's translational states contributing a factor from the ideal-gas partition function) gives:

<KeyEq title="Saha ionisation equation">
$$
\frac{\term{Nr1}{N_{r+1}}\,\term{ner}{n_e}}{\term{Nr}{N_r}} = \frac{2\,\term{Ur1}{U_{r+1}}}{\term{Ur}{U_r}}\left(\frac{2\pi m_e k_B T}{h^2}\right)^{3/2} e^{-\term{chir}{\chi_r}/k_B T}
$$
<Fragment slot="where"><span data-term="Nr">$N_r$</span>, <span data-term="Nr1">$N_{r+1}$</span> are populations in successive ionisation stages; <span data-term="ner">$n_e$</span> is the electron density; <span data-term="chir">$\chi_r$</span> is the energy needed to ionise stage $r$; <span data-term="Ur">$U_r$</span>, <span data-term="Ur1">$U_{r+1}$</span> are partition functions (sums over all bound levels' Boltzmann factors — this chapter's sims approximate them by the ground-state degeneracy alone, an honest simplification — see the flagship's "under the hood" section). The factor of 2 counts the electron's two spin states.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

<History title="A thesis on the ionisation of atoms" year="1920">
<Portrait src="/img/people/saha.jpg" name="Meghnad Saha" years="1893–1956" credit="Aliva Sahoo" license="CC BY-SA 4.0" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Meghnad_Saha.jpg" />

Meghnad Saha, an Indian physicist working at the University of Calcutta, was the first to realise that a star's spectrum is a thermometer *and* a barometer at once, if you apply the right piece of chemistry: treat ionisation as a reversible reaction and apply the same law of mass action that a chemist would use for any equilibrium. His 1920 equation let astronomers, for the first time, read off both temperature and pressure/density directly from which ionisation stages' lines were present — turning stellar classification from a taxonomy of shapes into physics. Saha later became a member of the Indian parliament and championed large scientific institutions and river-management projects; the crater Saha on the Moon and India's Meghnad Saha Institute of Nuclear Physics are named for him.
</History>

<Aside>**Why $n_e$ appears.** Saha's equation has electron density, not just temperature, on the ionisation side. More free electrons around make recombination (the reverse reaction) more likely, pushing the balance back toward the neutral stage — exactly like adding a product shifts a chemical equilibrium. Stellar atmospheres are dense enough, and cool stars' free electrons scarce enough, that this matters: a red giant's puffed-up, low-density outer layers are *more* ionised at fixed $T$ than a dwarf's compact photosphere.</Aside>

As $T$ climbs past a few times $\chi_r/k_B$, hydrogen ionises almost completely — and once it is H$^+$, there is no bound $n=2$ level left to populate at all. So Balmer-line strength is a *product* of two competing effects: a Boltzmann factor rising with $T$, and a Saha "neutral fraction" falling with $T$. Multiply them and the strength rises, peaks, and falls. At <Var name="Texc" value={9800} min={4000} max={20000} log /> K, the neutral-hydrogen population fraction in $n=2$ (the excited-*times*-still-neutral product above) is <Out name="n2frac" /> — drag the temperature and watch it rise, peak, then fall, tracking the observed Balmer-strength maximum near 9,500–10,000 K.

It peaks and falls. Below ~9,500 K, hydrogen is almost entirely neutral, but the Boltzmann factor keeps $n=2$ nearly empty, so Balmer lines are weak (this is why the Sun's Balmer lines are only moderate — it's on the cool side of the peak). </Predict>

```ts
  for (let it = 0; it < 24; it++) {
    const fH = stageFractions([13.598], [2, 1], T, ne);
    const fHe = stageFractions([24.587, 54.418], [1, 2, 1], T, ne);
    const neNew = nH * fH[1] + nHe * (fHe[1] + 2 * fHe[2]) + metalFloor;
    ne = 0.5 * ne + 0.5 * Math.max(neNew, 1e6);   // damped fixed-point iteration
  }
  return ne;
}
```

For each curated line, the model multiplies a Saha ionisation-stage fraction by a two-level Boltzmann excitation fraction to get a **line strength**, then turns that into a line-centre optical depth $\tau$ with a curated opacity scale per line, and maps it to line **depth** with a crude curve of growth, `maxDepth * tau / (1 + tau)`: linear while the line is weak, saturating once its core is opaque. Saturated lines keep strengthening through their wings, so the drawn width also grows as $\sqrt{\tau}$, which is why A-star Balmer lines look so fat. Depths are then rendered with the pseudo-Voigt profile from the broadening figure, combining thermal-Doppler and pressure widths, and the whole line list is Doppler-shifted by the velocity slider before compositing onto the Planck continuum.

**What's real:** the Planck continuum, the Rydberg/Bohr level structure, the Boltzmann and Saha equations themselves, and the pseudo-Voigt line shape are all the genuine physics. **What's simplified, and why:** partition functions are truncated to ground-state degeneracies (full $U(T)$ sums would need tabulated energy levels for every ion); TiO and CH molecular-band strengths are empirical logistic fits to temperature rather than a molecular-equilibrium (Saha-like, but for molecules) solve; and line depth uses a one-line curve of growth rather than integrating the equation of radiative transfer through a model atmosphere. Real spectral-synthesis codes — MOOG, Turbospectrum, SME, or full model-atmosphere codes like PHOENIX — solve the transfer equation layer by layer through a 1D (or 3D, for the state of the art) atmosphere model, with millions of tabulated atomic and molecular transitions and non-LTE corrections where collisions are too rare to keep level populations thermal. This sim is tuned to get the qualitative *and* rough quantitative story right — which lines appear when, and roughly how strong — in a form cheap enough to recompute at 60 fps.
</Hood>

## The spectral sequence: OBAFGKM(LTY)

Stack spectra like the flagship's across the full temperature range and a natural ordering falls out: **O B A F G K M**, running hot to cool, historically remembered by generations of astronomy students as "Oh Be A Fine Girl/Guy, Kiss Me" — and now extended to the brown dwarfs with **L, T, Y** below the hydrogen-fusion limit (Chapter 14).

This looks alphabetically scrambled because it *is*: the sequence was first ordered A through (roughly) P purely by the strength of hydrogen lines, before anyone knew what set that strength. Once astronomers understood that hydrogen-line strength is not monotonic in temperature (the peak you found in the figure above), that ordering had to be scrambled and re-sorted by temperature — and O, B, A, F, G, K, M is what survived the reshuffling.

Applying the still-new Saha equation to stellar spectra in her Harvard PhD thesis, Cecilia Payne showed that the enormous strength of hydrogen and helium lines across the spectral sequence implied that stars are made almost entirely of hydrogen and helium — a result so at odds with the accepted view (that stars had roughly Earth-like composition) that her thesis advisor, Henry Norris Russell, persuaded her to call it merely "spectroscopically indicated" rather than physically real. Russell, who had held the conventional view, confirmed her result independently four years later and for decades was wrongly credited with it alone. Astronomer Otto Struve later called her thesis "the most brilliant PhD thesis ever written in astronomy." Stars — and hence the ordinary matter of the universe — are mostly hydrogen, not rock.

Within a class, decimal sub-types (G2 for the Sun, M1 for Betelgeuse, M5.5 for Proxima Centauri) interpolate temperature further; luminosity classes (Ia, Ib, II…V) added by the Morgan-Keenan system separate giants and supergiants from main-sequence dwarfs of the same temperature by subtle differences in line *widths and ratios* — lower-density giant atmospheres show narrower pressure-broadened lines and different Saha balances at fixed $T$, exactly the density-dependence the Saha equation predicts.

## Spectroscopy across the universe

Everything so far has been about *which* wavelengths appear. Their *exact* wavelengths carry one more piece of information: motion.

**Radial velocities.** Bulk motion toward or away from the observer Doppler-shifts every line by $\Delta\lambda/\lambda = v_r/c$ (non-relativistic; Chapter 17 gives the relativistic version). A star's whole line spectrum sliding back and forth in lockstep, with a period of days, is the signature of an orbiting planet tugging on it — the radial-velocity method, one of the two workhorse exoplanet-detection techniques (Chapter 10).

**Cosmological redshift.** On the largest scales, the systematic recession of distant galaxies stretches every line by the same factor $1+z$, not because galaxies are "moving through" space in the ordinary sense but because space itself is expanding while the light is in transit (Chapter 25). The same Balmer and Ca II H&K lines this chapter builds appear in every galaxy's integrated spectrum, redshifted by anywhere from a percent (nearby galaxies) to a factor of several (the most distant galaxies DESI and JWST have found) — spectroscopy is how essentially every cosmological redshift in this book was actually measured.

**Exoplanet atmospheres.** During a transit, starlight grazes an exoplanet's upper atmosphere on its way to us, picking up narrow absorption features from whatever gases are there. JWST's transmission spectra of exoplanets have detected water, carbon dioxide, methane, and sulfur dioxide this way — Kirchhoff's third law, applied to a planet's atmosphere instead of a stellar photosphere, at a distance of hundreds of light-years.

**Molecular bands.** Below about 4,000 K, individual atomic lines share the spectrum with broad **molecular bands** — thousands of unresolved rotational-vibrational lines from molecules like TiO and VO, whose band heads pile up sharply at particular wavelengths (visible as the ramps in this chapter's flagship once you push the temperature slider down into the M-star range). The **G-band**, a CH molecular feature near 430.5 nm prominent in G and early K stars, gave that whole spectral class its letter long before anyone knew it was a carbon compound rather than an atomic line.

Galaxy spectra go further still: they are the sum of billions of individual stellar spectra, so their absorption-line strengths (particularly the Ca II H&K and G-band region) encode the *average* stellar population's age and metal content, the basis of stellar-population synthesis used throughout extragalactic astronomy (Chapters 22 and 23).

## Where we go from here

One curated line list and two 1913-vintage equations get you remarkably far: the right colours, the right line strengths, the right ordering of the spectral sequence, and the right physical story for why it is ordered that way. The next chapter turns from stars to the gas between them — where the same Boltzmann and Saha machinery, run at far lower densities and temperatures, explains why the interstellar medium exists in sharply distinct thermal phases, and where the 21-cm line from this chapter becomes the primary tool for mapping it.
