## Halos first, galaxies second

<Photo src="/img/galaxy-formation/antennae.jpg" alt="Two colliding spiral galaxies, the Antennae, with tangled dust lanes and bright pink and blue knots of new stars" credit="NASA, ESA, and the Hubble Heritage Team (STScI/AURA)-ESA/Hubble Collaboration" license="CC BY 4.0" href="https://esahubble.org/images/heic0615a/" size="wide" aspect="1 / 1">
**Galaxy formation in the act.** The Antennae (NGC 4038/4039), about 45 million light-years away, are two spiral galaxies partway through a merger. The collision squeezes their gas into thousands of bright blue star clusters, and pink regions glow with hydrogen lit by newborn stars. In a few hundred million years the pair will probably settle into a single elliptical galaxy.
</Photo>

<Level max={1}>
Imagine the young universe as an almost perfectly smooth fog of matter, with tiny lumps. Gravity makes the lumps grow. Most of the mass is **dark matter**, invisible stuff that only feels gravity, and it gathers into huge round clouds called **halos**. Small halos form first and then crash together into bigger ones, like raindrops running together into puddles.

Ordinary gas falls into these halos too. But unlike dark matter, gas can shed energy by glowing. As it glows it sinks to the middle of the halo, spins up into a flat disk (like pizza dough tossed in the air), and turns into stars. A galaxy is that bright disk of stars sitting at the bottom of a much bigger, darker halo. The rest of this chapter asks three questions: how does the gas get in, why does so little of it become stars, and what do the giant black holes at galaxy centres have to do with it?
</Level>


A galaxy is a small, bright thing sitting at the bottom of a large, dark thing. The Milky Way's stars and gas weigh about $6\times10^{10}$ M☉ and mostly lie inside 20 kpc of the centre. The dark-matter halo around them (see [Chapter 21](/ch/milky-way/)) weighs about $1\text{–}1.5\times10^{12}$ M☉ and reaches out to roughly 250 kpc. So to understand galaxy formation you first need to know how halos form. Then you need to understand why so little of the ordinary matter ends up as stars.

The halo story is told in detail in [Chapter 28](/ch/structure/), so here is the summary. The early universe had tiny density fluctuations, roughly Gaussian, with slightly more power on small scales. Once matter dominated, overdense patches grew. Each patch expanded more slowly than its surroundings, reached a maximum size ("turnaround"), then collapsed and *virialised*: it relaxed into a roughly spherical, pressure-supported cloud of dark matter with density about 200 times the cosmic mean. Small patches collapse first because their fluctuations are largest. Those small halos then fall together into bigger ones. This bottom-up pattern is called **hierarchical assembly**. A Milky Way halo today is the result of thousands of mergers. Its major mergers mostly ended about 10 billion years ago, and it has been slowly accreting smaller lumps ever since.

<Aside>**Merger trees.** Simulators record which halos merged into which as a *merger tree*. The Milky Way's tree has a trunk (the "main progenitor") and a few notable branches: Gaia-Enceladus/Sausage, a dwarf of about $10^{9}$ M☉ in stars that merged about 10 Gyr ago, and Sagittarius, which is being torn apart right now.</Aside>

### Virial numbers

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
When gas falls into a halo it crashes into the gas already there and heats up, like a meteor burning in the air. Bigger halos pull harder, so their gas gets hotter: about 30,000 K in a small dwarf galaxy, about a million degrees around the Milky Way, and about 100 million degrees in a giant cluster of galaxies. That last gas is so hot it glows in X-rays, and X-ray telescopes see it directly.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">
A virialised halo of mass $M$ and radius $R$ has kinetic energy equal to half the magnitude of its potential energy. That fixes a characteristic circular speed $V_c = \sqrt{GM/R}$. It also fixes a temperature: gas that falls in at speed $V_c$ is shock-heated until its thermal energy per particle matches that kinetic energy. The result is the **virial temperature**

$$
\term{Tvir}{T_\text{vir}} = \frac{\term{mu}{\mu}\, \term{mp}{m_p} \term{Vc}{V_c}^2}{2\term{kB}{k_B}} \approx 3.6\times10^5\ \text{K}\left(\frac{\term{Vc}{V_c}}{100\ \text{km/s}}\right)^2,
$$

with $\mu \approx 0.6$ for ionised primordial gas. A dwarf halo with $V_c = 30$ km/s has $T_\text{vir} \sim 3\times10^4$ K. The Milky Way halo, with $V_c \approx 160$–220 km/s, has about $10^6$ K. A rich cluster, with $V_c \approx 1500$ km/s, has about $10^8$ K. The hot gas in clusters glows in X-rays, which is one way we know this picture is right.
</Fragment>
</Tiers>

## Why gas has to cool

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
Dark matter can't glow, so it stays puffed up forever. Gas can glow, and glowing is how it loses energy and sinks. So everything depends on a race between two clocks. One is how long it takes the gas to fall to the centre if nothing holds it up (the **free-fall time**). The other is how long it takes the gas to glow away its heat (the **cooling time**). If cooling wins, the gas sinks and makes stars. If falling wins, the gas stays as a hot, thin atmosphere around the galaxy.

How fast gas cools depends on its temperature. Gas at around 100,000 K cools very fast, because its atoms are constantly being knocked into excited states and then shining the energy away. Much hotter gas has lost all its electrons, so it can only cool slowly. Denser gas cools faster, because its particles collide more often.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">
Dark matter cannot radiate, so a dark halo stays puffy forever. Gas can radiate. Once it has been heated to $T_\text{vir}$, it sits in rough hydrostatic equilibrium, like a star's atmosphere, and it can only sink to the centre by *losing energy*. The whole of galaxy formation turns on a comparison of two clocks:

- the **free-fall time** $\term{tff}{t_\text{ff}} = \sqrt{3\pi / 32 \term{G}{G}\term{rho}{\rho}}$, how long the gas takes to collapse if nothing holds it up; and
- the **cooling time**, how long the gas takes to radiate away its thermal energy.

For gas with particle density $n$ at temperature $T$, (new to temperature and ideal gases? see [Appendix A7](/ch/primer-thermo/)) the thermal energy density is about $\tfrac32 n k_B T$. The radiated power per unit volume is $n^2\Lambda(T)$: two-body collisions excite atoms or accelerate electrons, so the rate goes as density squared. The function $\Lambda(T)$ is the **cooling function**, and it contains all the atomic physics (see [Chapter 6](/ch/ism/)).

<KeyEq title="Cooling time">
$$
\term{tcool}{t_\text{cool}} = \frac{\tfrac32\, \term{n}{n}\, \term{kB}{k_B} \term{T}{T}}{\term{n}{n}^2\, \term{lam}{\Lambda(T)}} \;\propto\; \frac{T}{n\,\Lambda(T)}
$$
<Fragment slot="where"><span data-term="n">$n$</span> is the particle number density, <span data-term="T">$T$</span> the temperature and <span data-term="lam">$\Lambda(T)$</span> the cooling function (erg cm³ s⁻¹). Gas that can cool within a free-fall time, $t_\text{cool} < t_\text{ff}$, collapses and can form stars. Gas that cannot cool stays as a hot, pressure-supported halo.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

The cooling function has a characteristic shape. Below about $10^4$ K hydrogen is neutral and cooling almost stops, apart from molecules and metals. Between $10^4$ and $10^{5.5}$ K there is a huge peak, at roughly $10^{-22}$–$10^{-21.5}$ erg cm³ s⁻¹ for gas with some metals: collisions excite hydrogen, helium, and highly ionised carbon and oxygen, which radiate the energy away as ultraviolet lines. Above about $10^7$ K the atoms are fully stripped and only bremsstrahlung (free–free emission) remains, with $\Lambda \propto T^{1/2}$ and a value about 30 times lower than the peak.
</Fragment>
</Tiers>

### Rees–Ostriker and Silk: a maximum galaxy mass

<Level max={1}>
In 1977, several astronomers realised this race sets a **maximum size for galaxies**. In halos up to about a trillion Suns, the gas is at a temperature where it cools quickly. In bigger halos it gets so hot, and is spread so thin, that it would take longer than the age of the universe to cool. That's why no galaxy has much more than a few hundred billion Suns' worth of stars, even though some dark halos are a thousand times heavier.
</Level>

<Level min={2} title="Which halos can cool in time?">

In 1977 Martin Rees and Jeremiah Ostriker, Joseph Silk, and James Binney independently asked a simple question: which halos can cool within a free-fall time? The answer depends on density and temperature, but it produces a clear boundary. For $t_\text{cool} \approx t_\text{ff}$ the critical mass is roughly $10^{11}$–$10^{12}$ M☉ and the critical size roughly 100 kpc.

The physics works like this. Below this mass, halos are cool enough to sit on the steep side of the $\Lambda(T)$ peak, and their gas cools almost as fast as it falls in. Above it, gas is heated above $10^6$ K into the bremsstrahlung regime. There cooling is slow, and the gas also sits at low density in the outer halo, so $t_\text{cool}$ becomes longer than the age of the universe. This is why no galaxy is much more massive than a few $\times10^{11}$ M☉ in stars, even though halos reach $10^{15}$ M☉. The biggest structures are groups and clusters, full of hot gas that has never cooled.

<Aside>**Why "cooling" makes things *collapse*.** Self-gravitating systems have negative heat capacity (see [Chapter 11](/ch/stellar-structure/)). A gas cloud supported by pressure that loses energy contracts, and by the virial theorem it *heats up*. Cooling doesn't make the gas cold in a lasting way. It lets the gas sink until something else, like rotation or star formation, stops it.</Aside>

</Level>

<History title="Why galaxies are the size they are" year="1977">
<Portrait src="/img/people/rees-martin.jpg" name="Martin Rees" years="b. 1942" credit="Roger Harris" license="CC BY 3.0" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Official_portrait_of_Lord_Rees_of_Ludlow_crop_2.jpg" />

In 1977 three papers appeared within months of each other, from Martin Rees and Jeremiah Ostriker at Cambridge, Joseph Silk at Berkeley, and James Binney at Oxford. All three made the same comparison of cooling time with free-fall time, and all found the same characteristic mass of about $10^{12}$ M☉. The next year Simon White and Rees added the missing piece: gas cools inside dark halos that had already formed by hierarchical clustering. That 1978 paper is the ancestor of every galaxy-formation model since, including the toy one in this chapter.
</History>

### Hot mode and cold mode

For decades, the textbook picture was that all infalling gas shocks to $T_\text{vir}$ at the virial radius and then cools slowly out of a hot atmosphere. Simulations in the 2000s (Kereš et al. 2005; Dekel & Birnboim 2006) complicated this. In halos below about $10^{11.5}$–$10^{12}$ M☉, the post-shock gas cools so fast that a stable shock cannot even form. Gas flows in at around $10^4$–$10^5$ K straight to the centre (**cold-mode accretion**). In massive halos at high redshift, the cosmic web feeds the halo through narrow, dense filaments. Their gas is dense enough to cool faster than it can be shock-heated, so **cold streams** penetrate deep into an otherwise hot halo. This is likely how galaxies at $z \sim 2$–3 sustained star-formation rates of $100$ M☉/yr. The streams are hard to observe directly because they are faint, but Lyman-α emitting nebulae around quasars and absorption lines in background sightlines are consistent with them.

## Disks, spin and angular momentum

<Level max={1}>
Gas that cools doesn't fall straight to a point, because it is spinning slightly. Every halo gets a gentle twist from the tug of its neighbours while it forms. The twist is tiny, just a few per cent of what would be needed to hold the halo up. But as the gas shrinks inward it spins faster, like a figure skater pulling in her arms, until the spin holds it up. The result is a flat, rotating disk, which is why spiral galaxies look the way they do. A simple version of this argument predicts disks a few kiloparsecs across (about 10,000–20,000 light-years), which is about right for the Milky Way.
</Level>

<Level min={2} title="Spin, and the size of disks">

Gas that cools does not fall to a point, because it has angular momentum. Tidal torques from neighbouring structure spin up every protohalo slightly during its expansion phase (Peebles 1969; Doroshkevich 1970; White 1984). The amount is characterised by a dimensionless **spin parameter**:

$$
\term{lambda}{\lambda} = \frac{J\,|E|^{1/2}}{\term{G}{G} M^{5/2}},
$$

where $J$ is the angular momentum and $E$ the total energy. Simulations find a log-normal distribution with median $\lambda \approx 0.035$. That is a small number: the halo is supported almost entirely by random motions, and rotation contributes only a few per cent. But when the gas cools and contracts, it keeps its specific angular momentum $j = J/M$ while it loses energy. (Angular momentum is reviewed in [Appendix A6](/ch/primer-mechanics/).) It spins up like an ice skater pulling in her arms, until rotation supports it. A simple calculation (Mo, Mao & White 1998) with a singular isothermal halo and an exponential disk gives the scale length

<KeyEq title="Disk size from halo spin">
$$
\term{Rd}{R_d} \approx \frac{\term{lambda}{\lambda}}{\sqrt 2}\, \term{Rvir}{R_\text{vir}}
$$
<Fragment slot="where"><span data-term="Rd">$R_d$</span> is the exponential scale length of the disk, <span data-term="lambda">$\lambda$</span> the halo spin parameter and <span data-term="Rvir">$R_\text{vir}$</span> the virial radius. This assumes the gas keeps the specific angular momentum of its halo. For the Milky Way, $\lambda = 0.035$ and $R_\text{vir} \approx 250$ kpc give $R_d \approx 6$ kpc. The observed value is about 2.5–3 kpc: the right order of magnitude, with the gap explained by halo concentration and by baryons pulling the halo in.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

<Level min={3} title="Deriving the disk size">
The derivation takes a few lines. For a singular isothermal sphere, $|E| = MV_c^2/2$ and $R_\text{vir} = GM/V_c^2$. Substituting into the definition gives $J = \sqrt2\,\lambda M V_c R_\text{vir}$. An exponential disk with flat rotation curve $V_c$ has $J_d = 2 M_d R_d V_c$. Setting $J_d/M_d = J/M$ gives $R_d = \lambda R_\text{vir}/\sqrt2$.
</Level>

That this works at all is remarkable. Galaxy sizes are set by tidal torques from structures megaparsecs away. The details were famously *hard* to get right in simulations, though: in the 1990s gas lost most of its angular momentum during mergers and formed disks ten times too small (the "angular momentum catastrophe"). The cure turned out to be feedback, which stops gas from cooling too early inside small clumps. Keep this in mind for the next sections.

</Level>

## The flagship: watching a galaxy assemble

Time to see all of this happen. The simulation below starts at $z \approx 8.5$ with about forty lumps of dark matter and gas, a slow infall, and a slight net rotation. Everything falls together into one halo of $1.5\times10^{12}$ M☉. The gas follows a few simple rules: it heats to the local virial temperature, cools if $t_\text{cool} < t_\text{ff}$, settles into a disk, and forms stars with a two-billion-year depletion time. Supernova winds and an AGN can push back.

That drives the stellar mass towards the full baryon budget, about $f_b M_h \approx 2.4\times10^{11}$ M☉ here. This is the classic **overcooling problem** of the 1990s. <Hood title="gravity, sub-grid gas and the honest caveats">
**Gravity.** Every particle feels the softened gravity of 2048 "source" dark-matter particles, which carry all of the mass, including the baryons. Direct summation over sources costs $O(N \times 2048)$: 134 million pair interactions per step at 65k particles. The WGSL kernel loads sources in tiles of 256 into workgroup shared memory, so each source is read from global memory once per workgroup rather than once per particle. The same loop also accumulates a compact kernel $W = (1-r^2/h^2)^2$ over nearby sources, which gives a local density, centroid, mean velocity and velocity dispersion almost for free:

```wgsl
for (var k = 0u; k < WG; k++) {
  let s = tilePos[k].xyz;
  let d = s - x;
  let d2 = dot(d, d);
  let ir = inverseSqrt(d2 + P.eps2);        // Plummer softening
  acc += d * (ir * ir * ir);
  let q2 = d2 * invh2;
  if (q2 < 1.0) {                            // compact kernel W = (1 - q^2)^2
    let w = (1.0 - q2) * (1.0 - q2);
    let vv = tileVel[k].xyz;
    wsum += w; wx += w * s; wv += w * vv; wv2 += w * dot(vv, vv);
  }
}
```

Integration is kick–drift leapfrog (velocities at half steps, [Chapter 2](/ch/orbits/)) with a fixed step of 6 Myr and Plummer softening of 6 kpc. With only 2048 sources the halo is noticeably *grainy*: two-body relaxation time is about $N/(8\ln N) \approx 34$ crossing times, so over 13 Gyr the inner halo relaxes somewhat artificially. Production codes use $10^8$–$10^{10}$ particles with tree or particle-mesh gravity ([Chapter 28](/ch/structure/)).

**Gas recipes (per gas particle, per step).**
1. *Temperature:* $T = \max(10^4\,\text{K}, \mu m_p \sigma^2/3k_B)$, where $\sigma$ is the local dark-matter velocity dispersion. This stands in for virial shock heating.
2. *Cooling:* $t_\text{cool}$ from the formula above, using a toy $\Lambda(T)$ with the right peak and bremsstrahlung tail, and a gas density $f_b\rho$. If $t_\text{cool} < t_\text{ff}$, the gas's radial and vertical velocities relative to the local halo are damped on a free-fall time, while its tangential velocity is kept. That is the essence of "radiate energy, keep angular momentum", and it makes disks.
3. *Star formation:* cold gas above a density threshold turns into a star particle with probability $\Delta t / t_\text{dep}$, $t_\text{dep} = 2$ Gyr, the observed molecular-gas depletion time.
4. *Supernova winds:* each star-forming gas particle is instead ejected with probability $\eta\,\Delta t/t_\text{dep}$, with a fixed kick of about 260 km/s and mass loading $\eta \propto 1/\sigma^2$ ("energy-driven" winds). Winds escape shallow wells but fall back as fountains in deep ones.
5. *AGN:* in halos hotter than $10^{5.9}$ K, diffuse gas is not allowed to cool ("radio mode": jets reheat the atmosphere), and dense central gas is sometimes kicked out along the spin axis ("quasar mode").

**What is cosmetic.** Gas has no pressure forces at all: there is no hydrodynamics, no shocks, no cold streams. Gas does not source gravity (the dark-matter sources include its mass). All disks settle in the global $x$–$y$ plane, because vertical damping uses the global spin axis. The start is a hand-built clumpy sphere, not cosmological initial conditions, and "cosmic time" is just the code clock converted to Gyr ($t_\text{unit} = \sqrt{L^3/GM} = 1.52$ Gyr for $L = 250$ kpc, $M = 1.5\times10^{12}$ M☉). The black hole itself is not modelled.

**Precision.** All GPU arithmetic is f32. Units are chosen so positions, velocities and densities are $O(10^{-2}\text{–}10^{3})$, well within f32 range, and softening keeps $1/r^3$ bounded. Physical constants appear only as two pre-computed factors, the $T$ and $t_\text{cool}$ conversions, computed in double precision on the CPU. Random numbers come from a PCG hash of particle index and step number, so a seed and a set of controls give the same history on any GPU. Each frame dispatches two to eight compute passes, one render pass into an `rgba16float` target with additive blending, and a tonemap. Every twelfth frame a tiny counter kernel is read back asynchronously for the readouts.

**What real simulations do.** IllustrisTNG (AREPO, a moving-mesh hydro code), EAGLE (SPH) and FIRE (meshless finite-mass hydro, resolving individual star-forming regions) solve the equations of hydrodynamics with radiative cooling tables that depend on metallicity and the UV background. They use sub-grid models for star formation, stellar winds, supernovae, black-hole seeding, accretion and two modes of AGN feedback. These recipes are *calibrated* to reproduce the stellar mass function and galaxy sizes today. What they then predict, including morphology, colours, gas content and evolution, is the real test.
</Hood>

## The galaxy zoo

Galaxies come in a remarkably small number of shapes. Edwin Hubble's classification (1926, redrawn in 1936 as the famous "tuning fork") sorted them into **ellipticals** (E0–E7, from round to flattened), **lenticulars** (S0, disks without spiral arms or much gas), **spirals** (Sa–Sd, with ever looser arms and smaller bulges), barred spirals (SBa–SBd) and **irregulars**. Hubble called ellipticals "early type" and spirals "late type", and the names stuck even though no one thinks ellipticals evolve into spirals.

<Aside>**Galaxy Zoo.** In 2007, 150,000 volunteers classified about a million Sloan galaxies by eye in the first Galaxy Zoo project. Among their finds: blue ellipticals, red spirals, and the "green peas", compact starbursts that turned out to be local analogues of the galaxies JWST now finds at $z > 6$.</Aside>

<History title="Sorting the nebulae" year="1926">
<Portrait src="/img/people/hubble-edwin.jpg" name="Edwin Hubble" years="1889–1953" credit="Johan Hagemeyer, 1931" license="Public domain" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Studio_portrait_photograph_of_Edwin_Powell_Hubble_(cropped).JPG" />

In 1924 Edwin Hubble found Cepheid variables in the Andromeda "nebula" on plates from the 100-inch Hooker telescope. That settled the Great Debate: the spiral nebulae were galaxies far outside our own. Two years later he published a classification of about 400 of them, sorted by how smooth or knotty they looked. He called ellipticals "early" and spirals "late", borrowing the language of stellar spectral types, and he was careful to say the words implied no evolution. Readers ignored the caveat for decades. The scheme itself survived almost untouched, and Hubble's real legacy, the expanding universe, came three years later ([Chapter 25](/ch/expansion/)).
</History>

### Red sequence and blue cloud

A more physical classification comes from colour. Plot the optical colour of galaxies against their stellar mass (a colour–magnitude diagram) and you find two populations:

- the **blue cloud**: star-forming disk galaxies, whose light is dominated by short-lived hot, blue O and B stars; and
- the **red sequence**: *quiescent* galaxies, mostly ellipticals and S0s, whose stars are old and red, with a tight colour–mass relation set mostly by metallicity.

Between them lies a sparsely populated **green valley**. The small number of galaxies there tells us galaxies cross it quickly, in about 1 Gyr. Something *quenches* star formation, and it acts fast. Above about $3\times10^{10}$ M☉ most galaxies are red, and below it most are blue. Since $z \approx 2$ the mass in the red sequence has roughly doubled, while the blue cloud's has changed little: galaxies keep moving from blue to red.

### Morphology–density relation

In 1980 Alan Dressler found that the fraction of ellipticals and S0s rises from about 20% in the field to about 80% in the cores of rich clusters. Environment matters. Clusters strip gas from infalling spirals with **ram pressure** (the galaxy moving at 1000 km/s through $10^8$ K gas feels a strong headwind; "jellyfish" galaxies with trailing tentacles of stripped gas are the smoking gun). Satellites also lose their hot gas supply ("strangulation"), and repeated high-speed encounters ("harassment") thicken their disks.

### Mergers make ellipticals

The other route to an elliptical is a major merger. [Chapter 22](/ch/galactic-dynamics/) shows that when two disks of similar mass collide, violent relaxation scrambles the ordered orbits into a hot, roughly spheroidal system. Toomre & Toomre (1972) proposed this, and simulations have confirmed it. Gas in the merging disks is driven to the centre by tidal torques, which fuels a burst of star formation and feeds the central black hole. The most massive ellipticals probably grew mainly through many "dry" (gas-poor) minor mergers, which puffs them up without adding young stars. That explains why they are both old and largely assembled late.

## Why galaxy formation is inefficient

Here is the most important plot in the subject. Count halos by mass in a simulation, and count galaxies by stellar mass in a survey. Then match them in order, the most massive galaxy to the most massive halo, and so on down. This technique is called **abundance matching**. It gives the **stellar-to-halo mass relation**:

<Sim name="galaxy-formation-shmr" minHeight={420}>
**How much of each halo becomes stars?** The fit (Moster, Naab & White 2013) peaks at about 4% of the halo mass, or roughly **20–25% of the available baryons**, at $M_h \approx 10^{11.8}$ M☉, close to the Milky Way. Drag across the plot to read off the stellar mass of a halo. Move the redshift slider to see the peak shift to slightly higher masses in the past. The dashed curve is $z = 0$.
</Sim>

Every halo starts with the cosmic baryon fraction $f_b = \Omega_b/\Omega_m \approx 0.157$. If all of it became stars, the curve would be a flat line at 0.157. Instead it is a sharp peak, with efficiencies falling to below 1% at both ends. Two different physical processes cut off the two sides.

**Small halos: supernova feedback (and reionisation).** A $10^{10}$ M☉ halo has an escape speed of about 60 km/s. A single supernova releases about $10^{51}$ erg, and about one supernova forms for every 100 M☉ of stars. That gives $10^{49}$ erg per solar mass of stars formed, or a speed of about 700 km/s if all of it went into kinetic energy. Even if 90% of the energy is radiated away, supernovae can easily expel the gas of a dwarf. The ratio of gas ejected to stars formed, the *mass loading* $\eta$, scales as $V_c^{-2}$ if the winds are energy-driven, or $V_c^{-1}$ if momentum-driven. Reionisation adds a second effect: after $z \approx 6$, the UV background heats the intergalactic medium to about $10^4$ K, and halos with $V_c \lesssim 20$–30 km/s can no longer hold onto their gas at all. This is why the smallest dwarfs, like the ultra-faint satellites of the Milky Way, have only a few thousand M☉ of stars in $10^{8}$–$10^{9}$ M☉ halos.

**Big halos: long cooling times and AGN feedback.** Above $10^{12}$ M☉ the Rees–Ostriker argument already means cooling is slow. But not slow *enough*: X-ray observations show that cluster cores should cool at hundreds of M☉/yr (the "cooling flow" problem), yet they form far fewer stars. Something keeps adding heat. The culprit is the central supermassive black hole. X-ray images of Perseus and other clusters show cavities hundreds of kpc across, inflated by radio jets, with enough $PV$ work to offset the cooling. That is the subject of the next section.

<Fermi title="Can a black hole unbind its galaxy's gas?">
A black hole growing to mass $M_\text{BH}$ radiates about $\epsilon M_\text{BH} c^2$ with $\epsilon \approx 0.1$. For $M_\text{BH} = 10^8$ M☉ that is $10^{-1} \times 2\times10^{38}\,\text{kg}\times 9\times10^{16}\,\text{m}^2\text{s}^{-2} \approx 2\times10^{54}$ J.

The binding energy of the gas in its host is about $M_\text{gas}\sigma^2$. For a bulge with $M_\text{gas} = 10^{11}$ M☉ $= 2\times10^{41}$ kg and $\sigma = 200$ km/s, this is $2\times10^{41}\times 4\times10^{10} \approx 10^{52}$ J.

The black hole's output is **about 100 times** the gas binding energy. Coupling even a few per cent of it to the gas is enough to reshape the galaxy. That is the energetic case for AGN feedback, and why a black hole with only 0.1% of the bulge mass matters.
</Fermi>

## Supermassive black holes

Every massive galaxy seems to host a central black hole of $10^6$–$10^{10}$ M☉. The Milky Way's, Sgr A\*, weighs $4.3\times10^6$ M☉, measured from stellar orbits (Nobel Prize 2020) and imaged by the EHT in 2022. M87's weighs $6.5\times10^9$ M☉. When gas falls onto them through an accretion disk, they become **active galactic nuclei** (AGN), the most luminous persistent objects in the universe.

### The Eddington limit

<Level max={1}>
There is a speed limit on how fast a black hole can feed. Gas falling in gets very hot and shines, and that light pushes outward on the gas. The more gas falls in, the brighter it shines and the harder it pushes back. At some brightness, the **Eddington limit**, the push of light balances the pull of gravity, and extra gas gets blown away instead of swallowed. The limit is about 30,000 times the Sun's brightness for every Sun's worth of black-hole mass. A billion-Sun black hole shining at its limit outshines a whole galaxy of a hundred billion stars about a hundred times over.
</Level>

<Level min={2} title="Radiation pressure against gravity">

How bright can an accreting object be? Radiation pressure pushes outward on the infalling gas. The gas is mostly ionised hydrogen. Photons scatter off electrons with the Thomson cross-section $\sigma_T$, and electrostatic forces drag the protons along. At distance $r$ from a source of luminosity $L$, the photon momentum flux is $L/(4\pi r^2 c)$, so the outward force on each electron–proton pair is

$$
F_\text{rad} = \frac{\term{sigT}{\sigma_T} L}{4\pi r^2 \term{c}{c}}.
$$

Gravity pulls inward mostly on the proton: $F_\text{grav} = GMm_p/r^2$. Both scale as $1/r^2$, so the distance drops out. Set them equal:

</Level>

<KeyEq title="Eddington luminosity">
$$
\term{Ledd}{L_\text{Edd}} = \frac{4\pi \term{G}{G} \term{Mbh}{M}\, \term{mp}{m_p} \term{c}{c}}{\term{sigT}{\sigma_T}} \approx 1.26\times10^{31}\ \text{W}\left(\frac{M}{\text{M}_\odot}\right) \approx 3.3\times10^4\,L_\odot \left(\frac{M}{\text{M}_\odot}\right)
$$
<Fragment slot="where"><span data-term="Ledd">$L_\text{Edd}$</span> is the luminosity at which radiation pressure on electrons balances gravity on protons. <span data-term="Mbh">$M$</span> is the central mass, $m_p$ the proton mass <C k="mp" /> and <span data-term="sigT">$\sigma_T$</span> the Thomson cross-section <C k="sigmaT" />. Spherically accreting gas cannot shine brighter than this for long; disks and beaming allow modest super-Eddington phases.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

A black hole of <Var name="Mbh" value={1e9} min={1e2} max={1e11} log digits={2} /> M☉ has $L_\text{Edd} =$ <Out name="LEdd" />. A bright quasar at $10^{9}$ M☉ outshines its entire host galaxy of $10^{11}$ stars by a factor of about 100.

### Accretion efficiency and the Salpeter time

Gas spiralling in through a thin disk radiates the binding energy at its innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO, [Chapter 19](/ch/black-holes/)). That efficiency $\epsilon = L/\dot M c^2$ is 5.7% for a non-spinning black hole and up to 42% for a maximally spinning one. The usual fiducial value is $\epsilon \approx 0.1$, about 15 times more efficient than hydrogen fusion. To sustain $L_\text{Edd}$ with efficiency <Var name="eff" value={0.1} min={0.04} max={0.42} digits={2} />, our black hole must swallow <Out name="Mdot" />.

<Level max={1}>
Because the feeding limit grows with the black hole's mass, a black hole feeding at the limit grows by the same *percentage* every so often, like money earning compound interest. It roughly triples every 50 million years or so. That sounds fast, but going from 100 Suns to a billion Suns takes about 16 of those triplings: roughly 800 million years of nonstop feeding.
</Level>

<Level min={2} title="The Salpeter growth law">
Only a fraction $1 - \epsilon$ of the infalling mass ends up in the hole. The rest leaves as radiation. So a black hole accreting at the Eddington rate grows as

$$
\frac{d\term{Mbh}{M}}{dt} = \frac{(1-\term{eps}{\epsilon})}{\term{eps}{\epsilon}}\frac{\term{Ledd}{L_\text{Edd}}}{\term{c}{c}^2} = \frac{\term{Mbh}{M}}{\term{tS}{t_S}}, \qquad \term{tS}{t_S} = \frac{\term{eps}{\epsilon}}{1-\term{eps}{\epsilon}}\,\frac{\term{sigT}{\sigma_T} \term{c}{c}}{4\pi \term{G}{G} \term{mp}{m_p}} \approx \frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}\times 450\ \text{Myr}.
$$

The growth is **exponential** (a refresher on exponentials is in [Appendix A2](/ch/primer-functions/)), with e-folding time (the **Salpeter time**) of <Out name="tSal" /> for the efficiency above. It doesn't depend on the mass, because both the luminosity limit and the mass scale linearly with $M$.

</Level>

A seed of <Var name="Mseed" value={100} min={10} max={1e6} log digits={2} /> M☉, perhaps a remnant of a Population III star ([Chapter 16](/ch/stellar-death/)), needs <Out name="nFold" /> e-folds to reach $10^9$ M☉, which takes <Out name="tGrow" /> of uninterrupted Eddington-limited accretion. The universe was only 700 Myr old at $z = 7.5$, when the first billion-solar-mass quasars already shone.

**The race against the cosmic clock.** The orange curve is a black hole growing continuously at a fixed fraction of its Eddington rate from a seed formed at the chosen redshift. Blue points are some of the most distant black holes known, with masses uncertain by factors of about 3. Can a 100 M☉ seed make a $10^9$ M☉ quasar by $z = 7.5$? Try lowering $\epsilon$ (which helps growth), raising the seed to $10^5$ M☉ (a "direct-collapse" seed), or allowing mildly super-Eddington accretion.

The figure shows the problem. Light seeds only work if they accrete at the Eddington limit, continuously, from $z \approx 25$, with low radiative efficiency. Real black holes spend most of their lives far below Eddington. Hence two classes of solution: **heavy seeds** of $10^4$–$10^6$ M☉, formed by direct collapse of pristine gas clouds in atomic-cooling halos, or by runaway mergers in dense star clusters; and **super-Eddington** phases, where photons are trapped in the inflow and the luminosity limit no longer caps the accretion rate.

### The M–σ relation

In 2000, two teams (Ferrarese & Merritt; Gebhardt et al.) found that black-hole mass correlates tightly with the velocity dispersion of the host's bulge:

$$
\term{Mbh}{M_\text{BH}} \approx 3\times10^8\,\term{Msun}{\text{M}_\odot} \left(\frac{\term{sigmav}{\sigma}}{200\ \text{km/s}}\right)^{4.4},
$$

with only about 0.3 dex of scatter (Kormendy & Ho 2013). Equivalently, $M_\text{BH} \approx 0.5\%$ of the bulge's stellar mass. This is strange: the black hole's sphere of influence, $GM_\text{BH}/\sigma^2 \sim 10$ pc, is a thousand times smaller than the bulge. Something must connect them. The favourite explanation is feedback self-regulation. A black hole grows until its momentum-driven outflow ($L_\text{Edd}/c$) can push the gas out of the bulge. Setting $L_\text{Edd}/c \approx$ the weight of the gas shell gives $M_\text{BH} \propto \sigma^4$ (King 2003), close to the observed slope.

<Aside>**Coevolution, maybe.** The cosmic black-hole accretion rate density tracks the star-formation history below (both peak at $z \approx 2$) with a ratio of about $10^{-3}$, which is further evidence the two grow together. But JWST finds many early black holes *overmassive* relative to their hosts, so the relation may have been established from the black-hole side first.</Aside>

In 1962 Cyril Hazard and colleagues used the Moon as a knife-edge: timing when it covered the radio source 3C 273 fixed the source's position to about a second of arc. It lined up with a 13th-magnitude "star". Maarten Schmidt at Palomar took its spectrum and found broad emission lines at wavelengths nothing matched. On 5 February 1963, writing up a paper, he noticed the lines' spacing followed the hydrogen Balmer series, shifted 16% to the red. At that redshift ($z = 0.158$), 3C 273 lies about 2 billion light-years away and outshines the Milky Way a hundredfold from a region small enough to vary within months. Schmidt later recalled going home and telling his wife that something terrible had happened at the office. Explaining such power from so small a volume led straight to accretion onto supermassive black holes, proposed by Salpeter and by Zel'dovich in 1964 and developed by Lynden-Bell in 1969.
</History>

### One engine, many faces: AGN unification

Historically AGN were named by how they looked: **Seyfert galaxies** (bright nuclei in spirals), **quasars** (point-like, so luminous that the host is hard to see), **radio galaxies** with huge lobes, and **blazars** (violently variable, featureless spectra). Types 1 and 2 were distinguished by whether their spectra showed broad emission lines (thousands of km/s wide) or only narrow ones.

The **unified model** (Antonucci 1993; Urry & Padovani 1995) says most of these are the same engine seen from different angles:

- a black hole and hot accretion disk, radiating mostly in the UV (the "big blue bump");
- the **broad-line region**, fast clouds within light-days to light-months of the hole;
- a dusty **torus** (more likely a clumpy, flared, outflowing structure) at parsec scales, which absorbs the UV and re-radiates it in the infrared;
- the **narrow-line region**, slower gas extending hundreds of pc out; and
- in about 10% of AGN, a pair of relativistic **jets** perpendicular to the disk.

Look over the torus and you see the broad lines: a type 1 Seyfert or quasar. Look through it and they are hidden: a type 2. (Spectropolarimetry proved this: NGC 1068 shows broad lines in *polarised* light, scattered into view by electrons above the torus.) Look straight down the jet and relativistic beaming amplifies it into a blazar. A second parameter is accretion rate. At high Eddington ratio the disk is thin and radiatively efficient ("quasar mode"). Below about 1% of Eddington it becomes a hot, puffed-up, inefficient flow that mostly launches jets ("radio mode"). The same split appears in the feedback recipes of every cosmological simulation.

### Superluminal motion

<Photo src="/img/galaxy-formation/m87-jet.jpg" alt="The bright core of galaxy M87 with a thin blue jet extending towards the upper right" credit="The Hubble Heritage Team (STScI/AURA) and NASA/ESA" license="CC BY 4.0" href="https://esahubble.org/images/opo0020a/" size="text" aspect="1724 / 1800">
**A jet 5,000 light-years long.** Hubble's view of M87's jet, powered by the same black hole as the image above. The blue light is synchrotron radiation from electrons spiralling in magnetic fields at nearly the speed of light. Hubble has watched knots in this jet move across the sky at up to six times the speed of light.
</Photo>

In the 1970s, radio interferometers watched blobs in quasar jets move across the sky at *several times the speed of light*. The quasar 3C 279 shows apparent speeds of 20c. Nothing is breaking relativity; the light-travel time is playing a trick.

<Level max={1}>
Here is the trick. Suppose a blob in the jet is flying almost straight at us, at nearly the speed of light. It gives off a flash of light, then moves a long way towards us before giving off a second flash. The second flash has a much shorter trip to make, so the two flashes arrive at our telescope much closer together than they were sent. The blob seems to have crossed the sky in far less time than it really took, so its apparent speed can be many times the speed of light. Nothing actually moves faster than light. The figure below shows the effect.
</Level>

<Level min={2} title="Deriving the apparent speed">
A blob moves at speed $\beta c$ at angle $\theta$ to our line of sight. It emits a pulse at $t = 0$ and another at $t = \Delta t$. In between, it moves $\beta c\,\Delta t\cos\theta$ toward us, so the second pulse has that much less distance to travel. The observed interval is compressed:

$$
\Delta t_\text{obs} = \Delta t\,(1 - \term{beta}{\beta}\cos\term{theta}{\theta}).
$$

The transverse distance covered is $\beta c\,\Delta t\sin\theta$. So the apparent transverse speed is

<KeyEq title="Apparent superluminal speed">
$$
\term{bapp}{\beta_\text{app}} = \frac{\term{beta}{\beta}\sin\term{theta}{\theta}}{1 - \term{beta}{\beta}\cos\term{theta}{\theta}}
$$
<Fragment slot="where"><span data-term="bapp">$\beta_\text{app}$</span> is the apparent speed across the sky in units of $c$. <span data-term="beta">$\beta$</span> is the true speed and <span data-term="theta">$\theta$</span> the angle to the line of sight. Setting $d\beta_\text{app}/d\theta = 0$ gives a maximum at $\cos\theta = \beta$, where $\beta_\text{app} = \beta\term{gamma}{\gamma}$. For Lorentz factor $\gamma = 10$, blobs can appear to move at nearly $10c$.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

</Level>

Wavefronts bunch up on the observer's side because the blob nearly keeps pace with its own light. Right: $\beta_\text{app}$ against viewing angle. Drag across the plot to change $\theta$. The same Doppler factor explains why we usually see only *one* jet: the approaching jet is boosted and the receding one dimmed, by factors that can exceed $10^4$ ([Chapter 17](/ch/relativity/)).

### Quenching

We can now assemble the story of why galaxies stop forming stars. Candidate mechanisms include:

- **Halo quenching:** above about $10^{12}$ M☉ new gas is shock-heated and cools slowly, and radio-mode AGN keep it hot.
- **Quasar-mode feedback:** a burst of accretion, often triggered by a merger, drives winds that clear gas out of the centre. Outflows of hundreds of M☉/yr at 1000 km/s are observed in molecular gas.
- **Morphological quenching:** a massive bulge stabilises the gas disk against collapse.
- **Environmental quenching:** ram-pressure stripping and strangulation in groups and clusters, which is why satellites are more often red.

Simulations like IllustrisTNG reproduce the red sequence mainly through kinetic AGN feedback that turns on once the black hole exceeds about $10^8$ M☉. Observationally, quiescence correlates better with central stellar density and black-hole mass than with anything else.

## The cosmic star-formation history

Pull all of this back to the scale of the universe. Measure the star-formation rate per unit comoving volume at each redshift, from UV light corrected for dust, infrared re-emission, H-α and radio. You get the **Madau plot** (Madau et al. 1996; Madau & Dickinson 2014), one of the great summaries of observational cosmology. The Madau & Dickinson fit is

$$
\term{psi}{\psi}(\term{z}{z}) = 0.015\,\frac{(1+\term{z}{z})^{2.7}}{1 + [(1+\term{z}{z})/2.9]^{5.6}}\ \ \text{M}_\odot\,\text{yr}^{-1}\,\text{Mpc}^{-3}.
$$

<Sim name="galaxy-formation-madau" minHeight={400}>
**When the stars were made.** Star formation rose steeply for the first 3 Gyr, peaked at $z \approx 1.9$ ("cosmic noon", 10.5 Gyr ago) about **9 times higher than today**, and has declined ever since. Drag to read values. Switch the x-axis to cosmic time to see how lopsided it is: half of all stars had formed by $z \approx 1.3$ (4.9 Gyr after the Big Bang), and three-quarters by $z \approx 0.7$.
</Sim>

The rise is gravity at work: halos grow, cool gas flows in along cold streams, and star formation follows the accretion. The decline has three causes. Cosmic expansion lowers densities and slows accretion. More of the mass sits in halos above the quenching scale. And galaxies are running out of fuel: the gas depletion time is 1–2 Gyr, while inflow has slowed. The universe is past its prime. Today's star formation rate is only about 2 M☉/yr in a Milky Way-sized galaxy and falling.

<Aside>**Don't forget recycling.** About 30–40% of the mass of stars formed goes back to the interstellar medium through winds and supernovae (the "return fraction"), so the stellar mass density today is less than the integral of $\psi$. The Madau–Dickinson fit assumes a Salpeter IMF. Changing to a Chabrier IMF lowers all values by a factor of 1.6.</Aside>

## JWST surprises at cosmic dawn

Since 2022, JWST has pushed the frontier from $z \approx 11$ to beyond $z = 14$. The spectroscopic record is JADES-GS-z14-0 at $z = 14.2$, just 290 Myr after the Big Bang, and MoM-z14 at $z = 14.4$ followed in 2025. Three findings stand out, each with honest uncertainty.

**Too many bright galaxies.** At $z > 10$, JWST finds UV-bright galaxies up to ten times more abundant than most pre-launch models predicted. The early claims of "universe-breaking" massive galaxies at $z \sim 7$–9 mostly softened once spectra arrived: some were AGN, and masses were overestimated. But the excess of bright galaxies at $z > 10$ is robust. Candidate explanations, none of which require new cosmology: star formation was more efficient in dense early halos where feedback had no time to act; it was *bursty*, so the brightest are temporarily lit up; there was less dust; or the IMF was top-heavy, making more light per unit mass.

**Little red dots.** JWST found a population of compact, very red sources at $z \approx 4$–8 with broad H-α lines, dubbed "little red dots". They have a V-shaped spectrum: blue in the UV, red in the optical. If the broad lines indicate AGN, the black holes are $10^6$–$10^8$ M☉ and often overmassive relative to their hosts. But they are strangely X-ray weak and show little variability or hot dust. By 2025–26 a leading interpretation is that they are black holes accreting inside dense gas cocoons, so the red colour comes from extremely dense gas rather than dust. Their nature is still actively debated.

**Early, heavy black holes.** UHZ1, seen in X-rays by Chandra behind a lensing cluster at $z = 10.1$, has a black hole of about $4\times10^7$ M☉, comparable to its host's stellar mass. GN-z11 at $z = 10.6$ appears to host a $\sim10^6$ M☉ accreting black hole. These strongly favour heavy seeds or super-Eddington growth: return to the Eddington figure and see what it takes to reach UHZ1.

None of these yet threatens ΛCDM. They do tell us that the physics of the first galaxies (star-formation efficiency, feedback, dust, black-hole seeding) was different from what we assumed based on the nearby universe.

## Where we go from here

Galaxy formation compresses into a surprisingly short list of principles. Dark matter builds halos hierarchically. Gas can only follow if it cools faster than it falls, which limits galaxies to a narrow range of halo masses. Angular momentum from tidal torques turns cooled gas into disks, and mergers turn disks into ellipticals. Feedback from supernovae at the low-mass end and black holes at the high-mass end keeps the whole process inefficient, peaking at about 20% of baryons in Milky Way-mass halos. The universe made most of its stars around $z \approx 2$ and has been winding down since.
