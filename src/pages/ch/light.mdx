## Waves, photons, and the spectrum we can't see

<Photo src="/img/light/cassiopeia-a-multiwavelength.jpg" alt="A composite image of the Cassiopeia A supernova remnant, showing a roughly circular shell built from overlaid magenta gamma-ray, blue-green X-ray, yellow visible-light, red infrared and orange radio emission" credit="NASA/DOE/Fermi LAT Collaboration, CXC/SAO/JPL-Caltech/Steward/O. Krause et al., and NRAO/AUI" license="Public domain" href="https://images.nasa.gov/details/GSFC_20171208_Archive_e002183" size="wide" aspect="1920 / 1453">
**One object, five telescopes.** Cassiopeia A, a supernova remnant 11,000 ly away, photographed simultaneously in gamma rays (Fermi), X-rays (Chandra), visible light (Hubble), infrared (Spitzer) and radio (the VLA). Each band traces different physics — and three of these five telescopes only work in space, for reasons this section explains.
</Photo>

Light is an oscillation of the electromagnetic field, and it is also a stream of particles. Both descriptions are exact, and an engineer should be fluent in the arithmetic of each.

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
As a wave, light is characterised by a wavelength (the distance between crests) and how fast it travels — always the same speed, $c$, in a vacuum. As a particle, light comes in discrete chunks called photons, and a *shorter* wavelength means each photon packs *more* energy. A single X-ray photon carries thousands of times more energy than a single photon of visible light.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">
As a wave, light of frequency $f$ and wavelength $\lambda$ travels at $c = \lambda f$. As a particle, each photon carries a fixed energy set by that frequency: $E = hf$, where $h$ is Planck's constant. A visible photon ($\lambda \approx 550$ nm) carries $E \approx 2.25$ eV; an X-ray photon ($\lambda \approx 0.1$ nm) carries $E \approx 12{,}000$ eV — about five thousand times more energetic, for a wavelength five thousand times shorter.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l3">
As a wave, light of frequency $\nu$ and wavelength $\lambda$ travels at $c = \lambda\nu$. As a particle, each photon carries a fixed energy and momentum set by that same frequency:

<KeyEq title="Photon energy and momentum">
$$
\term{E}{E} = h\term{nu}{\nu} = \frac{hc}{\term{lam}{\lambda}}, \qquad \term{p}{p} = \frac{E}{c} = \frac{h}{\lambda}
$$
<Fragment slot="where">$h$ is <C k="h" />. <span data-term="E">E</span> is the photon's energy, <span data-term="nu">ν</span> its frequency, <span data-term="lam">λ</span> its wavelength and <span data-term="p">p</span> its momentum. A visible photon ($\lambda \approx 550$ nm) carries $E \approx 2.25$ eV; an X-ray photon ($\lambda \approx 0.1$ nm) carries $E \approx 12$ keV.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>
</Fragment>
</Tiers>

<Level min={3} title="Where p = E/c comes from">
From special relativity (Chapter 17), $E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2$. Setting $m=0$ collapses this to $E = pc$, matching $p = E/c$ above. A massless particle is not "$mv$ with $m=0$" — it is a distinct kind of object that must move at $c$ or not exist.
</Level>

The particle picture matters whenever a single interaction is discrete: a photon ionising an atom, a pixel on a CCD registering one count, or the pressure that photons exert when they scatter (radiation pressure, momentum $p$ per photon, which becomes decisive in Chapter 15's stellar winds and Chapter 23's AGN feedback). The wave picture matters whenever many photons interfere or diffract, and whenever we want to talk about *continuous* fields of radiation — which is most of this chapter. (New to the quantum idea that light comes in discrete packets? See [Appendix A9](/ch/primer-quantum/); a refresher on waves is in [Appendix A8](/ch/primer-waves/).)

<Aside>**Why photons don't have mass, but do have momentum.** Momentum without mass feels wrong from everyday intuition ($p=mv$), but that formula is only the low-speed limit of $E^2=(pc)^2+(mc^2)^2$. Light has no low-speed limit — it always moves at $c$.</Aside>

### The electromagnetic spectrum and why we need space telescopes

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
The spectrum runs continuously from radio (long, gentle waves) through microwave, infrared ("heat"), visible light, ultraviolet, X-ray, to gamma rays (short, energetic waves). The universe shines in every one of these colours, most of which your eyes cannot see at all: cold dust glows in the far-infrared, stars glow mostly in the visible/near-infrared, and violent, hot processes — shredded stars, particle jets — glow in X-rays and gamma rays.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">
The spectrum runs continuously from radio ($\lambda \gtrsim 1$ m) through microwave, infrared, visible ($380$–$780$ nm), ultraviolet, X-ray, down to gamma rays ($\lambda \lesssim 10$ pm). Astronomically interesting sources exist across the entire range: cold dust glows in the far-infrared, stellar photospheres peak in the optical or near-infrared, accretion disks and coronae blaze in X-rays, and nuclear/particle processes make gamma rays.
</Fragment>
</Tiers>

Earth's atmosphere is a good filter for exactly one purpose — keeping us alive — and a bad one for astronomy outside two narrow "windows": the optical/near-infrared window (with gaps from water vapour) and the radio window. Ozone absorbs essentially all UV shortward of $\approx 300$ nm; water and CO₂ absorb most of the infrared in bands; the ionosphere reflects long-radio wavelengths back into space; and X-rays and gamma rays are absorbed high in the atmosphere by photoionisation, which is a mercy for astronauts but means an X-ray telescope on the ground sees nothing at all.

<Aside>**In the real universe…** this is not a historical curiosity. JWST observes at 0.6–28 μm specifically because that infrared range is where the reionisation-era galaxies, cool substellar objects, and dust-enshrouded star formation that ground telescopes cannot reach are brightest — redshifted starlight and thermal dust emission both peak there. Chandra and XMM-Newton do the same job for the X-ray sky (hot accretion flows, X-ray binaries, cluster gas); both are necessarily space telescopes.</Aside>

<Photo src="/img/light/cosmic-cliffs-nircam.jpg" alt="A glowing amber-and-blue landscape of dust resembling craggy cliffs, with a diffuse blue star-forming region above and pinpoint stars scattered across the frame" credit="NASA, ESA, CSA, and STScI" license="CC BY 4.0" href="https://esawebb.org/images/weic2205a/" size="wide" aspect="1728 / 1000">
**The "Cosmic Cliffs" in Carina, seen only because JWST looks in the infrared.** This is the edge of NGC 3324, a young star-forming region. Infrared light passes through the dust that would hide these embedded, still-forming stars from a visible-light telescope — and every wavelength shown here is blocked by Earth's atmosphere, which is why NIRCam flies a million miles from the ground.
</Photo>

That is the whole reason an X-ray, UV, or far-infrared telescope has to launch on a rocket rather than sit on a mountain: it isn't about being "above the weather", it's about being above gases whose molecular and electronic transitions happen to sit exactly in the band you want to observe.

<History title="The thermometer beyond the red" year="1800">
<Portrait src="/img/people/herschel.jpg" name="William Herschel" years="1738–1822" credit="Lemuel Francis Abbott, 1785" license="Public domain" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:William_Herschel01.jpg" />

William Herschel — already famous for discovering Uranus in 1781 — was measuring how much heat different colours of sunlight carried. He passed sunlight through a prism and moved a thermometer through the spectrum from violet to red, then, out of curiosity, moved it just *past* the red end, into the region where no colour was visible at all. The thermometer there read the *highest* temperature of the whole experiment.

Herschel had found "calorific rays" invisible to the eye — what we now call infrared radiation — decades before anyone understood light as an electromagnetic wave, let alone knew that visible light was only a sliver of a much larger spectrum. His accidental discovery was the first hint that there was more to light than what we can see, the idea this whole section is built on.
</History>

## Radiometric quantities, precisely

Astronomers casually say "brightness", but there are several distinct, precisely defined radiometric quantities, and conflating them is the single most common error engineers make when they first pick up an astronomy problem.

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
- **Luminosity**: the total power a source puts out, like the wattage of a light bulb.
- **Flux**: how much of that power actually lands on a given patch of your detector — this is what "how bright does it look" really means, and it depends on distance.
- **Intensity** (or *surface brightness*): how bright a small resolved patch of the source itself looks, independent of how far away you are — the surprising, load-bearing idea of this section.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">
- **Luminosity** $L$ (W): total power radiated by a source, in all directions, summed over all wavelengths.
- **Flux** $F$ (W m⁻²): power per unit area crossing a surface — what a detector of known area actually collects.
- **Intensity** (specific intensity) $I$ (W m⁻² sr⁻¹): power per unit area, per unit solid angle, along a single ray, usually also per unit frequency ($I_\nu$). This is the fundamental quantity; flux and luminosity are both built from it.
</Fragment>
</Tiers>

For an isotropic point source at distance $d$, the luminosity spreads over a sphere of area $4\pi d^2$, giving the inverse-square law:

<KeyEq title="Inverse-square law">
$$
\term{F}{F} = \frac{\term{L}{L}}{4\pi \term{d}{d}^2}
$$
<Fragment slot="where"><span data-term="F">F</span> is the flux received at distance <span data-term="d">d</span> from an isotropic source of luminosity <span data-term="L">L</span>. This is why distant things look faint even if they are intrinsically brilliant — quasars at $d \sim$ Gpc outshine our Sun by a factor of $10^{13}$ yet are invisible to the naked eye. (New to this kind of algebra with powers? See [Appendix A2](/ch/primer-functions/).)</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

### Why surface brightness doesn't care about distance

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
Here is the counter-intuitive part. Point a telescope that can *resolve* a star's disk (see many pixels across it, not just one dot) at that star, then move the star ten times farther away. Each pixel that still lands on the star's disk looks **exactly as bright as before**. The star only looks fainter overall because its disk now covers fewer pixels — the *total* light collected drops, but the brightness *per resolved patch* does not. That per-patch quantity is called surface brightness, and it simply doesn't care about distance, as long as you can still resolve the source.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l3">
$I_\nu$ along a ray in empty space (no absorption, no emission) is **exactly conserved**, no matter how far the ray travels. Consider a small patch of emitting surface with area $dA$ and a detector with area $dA'$ at distance $d$, both tilted so their normals make angle $\theta,\theta'$ with the line joining them. The power reaching the detector is $I_\nu\, dA\cos\theta\, d\Omega$, where $d\Omega = dA'\cos\theta'/d^2$ is the solid angle the detector subtends as seen from the source. But by exactly the same construction run backwards, the source subtends solid angle $dA\cos\theta/d^2$ as seen from the detector. Multiply through and the $d^2$ cancels: the intensity you *measure*, looking at a resolved surface, is the same as the intensity *emitted*, independent of $d$.
</Fragment>
</Tiers>

<Aside>**The catch: "resolved".** Conservation of $I_\nu$ assumes your beam samples the source's own surface, not the whole source as an unresolved point. Move a star far enough away and it stops filling even one pixel; then all its flux piles into that pixel, and *that* pixel's brightness falls with distance in the ordinary $1/d^2$ way. Resolved surface brightness and unresolved point-source flux are different regimes of the same physics.</Aside>

This is why a nearby patch of the Sun's photosphere and an equally-sized, equally-hot patch of a photosphere on a star 100 pc away look *exactly as bright per unit solid angle* through a telescope that resolves them — the star is fainter overall only because it subtends a smaller solid angle, not because each resolved element is dimmer. Surface photometry (surface brightness in mag/arcsec²) exploits exactly this fact, and it's why measuring a galaxy's *surface* brightness profile is distance-independent while measuring its total flux is not.

Each *resolved* pixel samples the star's own surface intensity $I_\nu$, which is conserved along the ray. What changes is that the star's disk now covers 100× fewer pixels (angular area $\propto 1/d^2$), so the *total* flux collected falls by 100× — consistent with the inverse-square law — even though no individual pixel dims. ## Cavity modes and the ultraviolet catastrophe

Now turn to *thermal* radiation: the light emitted by any object simply because it has a temperature $T > 0$. The cleanest idealisation is a **blackbody**: a perfect absorber and emitter, in thermal equilibrium, with no preferred surface features. Model it as radiation trapped in a cavity with perfectly reflecting walls.

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
Before quantum mechanics, physicists tried to work out how much light a hot cavity should glow with by treating the light inside as a set of standing waves, and giving every possible wave an equal, small ration of the available heat energy. The trouble: there is no limit to how many standing waves of shorter and shorter wavelength you can fit in a box, so this recipe predicts the box should glow with *infinite* power, mostly at the shortest wavelengths. Real hot objects obviously don't do this — you don't get fried by ultraviolet rays and X-rays just from a warm oven. This absurd prediction is the **ultraviolet catastrophe**, and fixing it took a genuinely new idea.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">
Classically (19th-century physics, no quanta), a cavity of size $\ell$ supports standing electromagnetic waves — modes — at wavelengths $\lambda = 2\ell/n$ for integer $n$, in each of 3 spatial dimensions and 2 polarisations. There is no shortest wavelength, so there is no limit to how many modes exist. Classical physics then assigns every mode, however short its wavelength, an equal share $k_BT$ of thermal energy (equipartition). Multiplying an ever-growing number of modes by a fixed energy per mode gives an energy density that diverges at short wavelengths (high frequency) — the **ultraviolet catastrophe**.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l3">
Counting how many modes fit between frequency $\nu$ and $\nu+d\nu$ (this is a purely geometric exercise, counting lattice points inside a spherical shell in $k$-space) gives a **mode density**

$$
g(\nu)\,d\nu = \frac{8\pi\nu^2}{c^3}\,d\nu \quad \text{(per unit volume)}.
$$

Classical statistical mechanics (equipartition) assigns every mode a mean energy $k_BT$, regardless of its frequency. Multiplying mode density by energy per mode gives the **Rayleigh–Jeans law**:

<KeyEq title="Rayleigh–Jeans law (classical, wrong)">
$$
B_\nu^{\rm RJ}(\term{T}{T}) = \frac{2\term{nu}{\nu}^2}{c^2}\,k_BT, \qquad B_\lambda^{\rm RJ}(T) = \frac{2ck_BT}{\term{lam}{\lambda}^4}
$$
<Fragment slot="where">Correct at low frequency (radio), but the energy density $\int_0^\infty B_\nu^{\rm RJ}\,d\nu$ diverges — every classical cavity, no matter how cold, would radiate infinite power into ever-shorter wavelengths.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>
</Fragment>
</Tiers>

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
Planck's fix (1900) keeps the wave-counting but changes the rule for how energy is handed out. Instead of every mode getting a smooth, equal ration regardless of its wavelength, energy can only be paid into a mode in fixed-size coins, worth $h\nu$ — bigger coins for shorter (higher-frequency) waves. At any finite temperature, there's only so much loose thermal change lying around: modes whose coin size is much bigger than the typical thermal energy essentially never get paid, so they contribute almost nothing. Short-wavelength modes are suppressed not because there are fewer of them, but because quantisation makes them expensive to excite.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l3">
The fix, due to Planck, keeps the *mode counting* exactly as above — that part of the classical argument was fine — and replaces equipartition with quantisation: each mode of frequency $\nu$ can only hold energy in packets of $h\nu$, and in thermal equilibrium the number of packets in a mode follows the Bose–Einstein occupation number $\bar n = 1/(e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1)$ (a refresher on the Boltzmann factor behind this kind of statistics is in [Appendix A7](/ch/primer-thermo/)). The mean energy per mode is then $h\nu\,\bar n$, not $k_BT$: at low frequency ($h\nu \ll k_BT$) this expands to $\approx k_BT$, recovering Rayleigh–Jeans, but at high frequency ($h\nu \gg k_BT$) it is suppressed exponentially — there simply isn't enough thermal energy lying around to excite a single quantum that large. Multiplying mode density by this quantum mean energy gives Planck's law:

<KeyEq title="Planck's law">
$$
B_\nu(\term{T}{T}) = \frac{2h\term{nu}{\nu}^3}{c^2}\,\frac{1}{e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1}, \qquad B_\lambda(T) = \frac{2hc^2}{\term{lam}{\lambda}^5}\,\frac{1}{e^{hc/\lambda k_BT}-1}
$$
<Fragment slot="where">$B_\nu$ and $B_\lambda$ are both specific intensities (W m⁻² Hz⁻¹ sr⁻¹ and W m⁻² m⁻¹ sr⁻¹ respectively) of an ideal thermal emitter — they depend on <span data-term="T">T</span> alone, not on composition. $B_\nu\,d\nu = -B_\lambda\,d\lambda$ relates them (the minus sign because <span data-term="nu">ν</span> and <span data-term="lam">λ</span> run opposite ways); this is why they peak at different places, below. (New to exponentials like $e^x$? See [Appendix A2](/ch/primer-functions/).)</Fragment>
</KeyEq>
</Fragment>
</Tiers>

<History title="An act of desperation" year="1900">
<Portrait src="/img/people/planck.jpg" name="Max Planck" years="1858–1947" credit="Unknown author, 1933" license="Public domain" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Max_Planck_1933.jpg" />

By 1900, Max Planck had spent years trying to fit the measured shape of blackbody radiation with a formula built from ordinary, continuous thermodynamics — and failing. He knew Wien's formula worked well at short wavelengths and Rayleigh's at long ones, but no single classical expression matched the data everywhere. In October 1900 he found, essentially by curve-fitting, a formula that interpolated between the two limits and matched every measurement precisely.

The formula worked, but Planck didn't yet know *why*. Over the next weeks he searched for a physical derivation and found he had to assume that a cavity's oscillators could only exchange energy in discrete packets of size $h\nu$ — an assumption he called, in his own words decades later, "an act of desperation," done because "a theoretical interpretation had to be found at any cost, whatever the price." He initially saw it as a mathematical trick, not a claim about nature. It was Einstein, five years later, who took the quantum seriously as a real property of light itself (explaining the photoelectric effect), and Planck's reluctant formula became the opening move of quantum mechanics.
</History>

<Fermi title="How wrong is the ultraviolet catastrophe, quantitatively?">
Integrate the classical energy density $u_\nu^{\rm RJ} = (4\pi/c)B_\nu^{\rm RJ} \propto \nu^2$ from some UV cutoff $\nu_{\rm min}$ to $\infty$: it's already infinite. Even integrating the other way, from $0$ to a cutoff $\nu_{\rm min}$, gives a *finite* but already-large number that grows without bound as $\nu_{\rm min}\to 0$ is relaxed the other direction — the point is there is no natural stopping scale in the classical formula at all. Planck's law provides exactly that scale, $\nu \sim k_BT/h$, self-consistently set by the temperature. At $T = 300$ K, $k_BT/h \approx 6\times10^{12}$ Hz (far infrared) — wavelengths shorter than about 10 μm are exponentially suppressed rather than classically overcounted.
</Fermi>

<Predict question="If you cool a cavity to absolute zero, what happens to the classical (Rayleigh–Jeans) prediction for its total radiated power?" choices={['Goes to zero, same as Planck', 'Stays infinite regardless of T', 'Becomes negative']} answer={1}>
Rayleigh–Jeans integrated over all frequencies is $\int_0^\infty (2\nu^2/c^2)k_BT\,d\nu$, which diverges for *any* $T>0$. The mode-counting is temperature-independent; only Bose–Einstein occupancy introduces the exponential cutoff that makes the total finite and, crucially, makes it scale sensibly as $T^4$ (next section).
</Predict>

## Wien's law and the Stefan–Boltzmann law

### The peak: Wien's displacement law

<Level min={3} title="Deriving the peak wavelength">
$B_\lambda(T)$ has a maximum. Differentiate, set to zero, and you get a transcendental equation $(5 - x)e^x = 5$ (with $x = hc/\lambda k_BT$) whose numerical root gives $x \approx 4.965$. (A refresher on derivatives and root-finding is in [Appendix A4](/ch/primer-calculus/).)
</Level>

<KeyEq title="Wien's displacement law">
$$
\lambda_{\rm peak}\,\term{T}{T} = \term{bwien}{b}, \qquad b \approx 2.898\times10^{-3}\ \text{m·K}
$$
<Fragment slot="where">Hotter objects peak at shorter wavelengths. <span data-term="bwien">b</span> is Wien's constant. The Sun ($T_{\rm eff}=5772$ K) peaks at $\approx 502$ nm — green, not that it looks green (see below). The CMB (2.725 K) peaks at $\approx 1.06$ mm, deep in the microwave.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

<Aside>**$B_\nu$ and $B_\lambda$ peak at different wavelengths — this is not an error.** $B_\lambda$ peaks where $hc/\lambda k_BT \approx 4.965$; $B_\nu$ peaks where $h\nu/k_BT\approx 2.821$, which converts to a *longer* wavelength than the $B_\lambda$ peak by a factor of $\approx 1.76$. The reason is the nonlinear change of variables $d\lambda = -(c/\nu^2)d\nu$: equal-width frequency bins near the peak correspond to unequal-width wavelength bins, which shifts where the "most power per bin" lands. Always be explicit about which one you mean.</Aside>

A star's <Var name="Twien" value={5772} min={100} max={100000} log /> K blackbody peaks at <Out name="lambdaPeak" />.

<History title="A law found before the function it came from" year="1893">
<Portrait src="/img/people/wien.jpg" name="Wilhelm Wien" years="1864–1928" credit="Wikimedia Commons" license="Public domain" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Wilhelm_Wien.jpg" />

Wilhelm Wien found the displacement law seven years before Planck's full spectral formula existed, by a clever thermodynamic argument (adiabatic compression of radiation in a cavity, plus Doppler shifting) that fixed *how* the peak wavelength must scale with temperature without deriving the complete curve. He also proposed a full spectral formula in 1896 that fit the high-frequency data of the day beautifully — the "Wien approximation" you can overlay in the flagship sim — but it failed for long wavelengths, the gap that later pushed Planck toward the full quantum formula. Wien won the 1911 Nobel Prize in Physics for the displacement law alone, two years before Planck's more fundamental work was similarly recognised in 1918.
</History>

### The total: Stefan–Boltzmann law

<Level min={3} title="Integrating Planck's law over all wavelengths">
Integrate $B_\lambda$ over all wavelengths and all outward directions of a hemisphere (an extra factor of $\pi$, not $4\pi$ — for a surface, contributions only leave into the outward $2\pi$ steradians, weighted by $\cos\theta$, which integrates to $\pi$) to get the total power emitted per unit area:

$$
F = \pi\int_0^\infty B_\lambda\,d\lambda = \frac{2\pi h c^2}{15}\left(\frac{k_B}{hc}\right)^4 T^4 \int_0^\infty \frac{x^3}{e^x-1}\,dx, \qquad x \equiv \frac{hc}{\lambda k_BT}.
$$

The dimensionless integral is a classic: $\int_0^\infty x^3/(e^x-1)\,dx = \Gamma(4)\zeta(4) = 6\cdot\frac{\pi^4}{90} = \frac{\pi^4}{15}$ (expand $1/(e^x-1)$ as a geometric series $\sum_{n\ge1}e^{-nx}$, integrate term-by-term with $\int_0^\infty x^3e^{-nx}dx = 6/n^4$, and sum $\sum 1/n^4 = \pi^4/90$ — we'll skip the tedious convergence bookkeeping; a refresher on integrals and series is in [Appendix A4](/ch/primer-calculus/)). Substituting collapses all the constants into one.
</Level>

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
Add up Planck's law over every wavelength, and something remarkable happens: nearly all the messy constants cancel, leaving a strikingly simple result. The power radiated by each square metre of a hot surface depends on nothing but its temperature, raised to the *fourth* power.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">
<KeyEq title="Stefan–Boltzmann law">
$$
\term{F}{F} = \sigma \term{T}{T}^4, \qquad \sigma \approx 5.670\times10^{-8}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\text{K}^{-4}
$$
<Fragment slot="where">$\sigma$ is <C k="sigma" />. <span data-term="F">F</span> is the power radiated per unit area at temperature <span data-term="T">T</span>. Double the temperature, radiate 16× the power per unit area.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l3">
<KeyEq title="Stefan–Boltzmann law">
$$
\term{F}{F} = \sigma \term{T}{T}^4, \qquad \sigma \equiv \frac{2\pi^5k_B^4}{15h^3c^2} \approx 5.670\times10^{-8}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\text{K}^{-4}
$$
<Fragment slot="where">$\sigma$ is <C k="sigma" />, built entirely from $h$, $c$, $k_B$.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>
</Fragment>
</Tiers>

For a sphere of radius $R$ radiating as a blackbody at its **effective temperature** $T_{\rm eff}$ (defined by this very relation — it's the temperature a blackbody of the same radius and luminosity would have, whether or not the real object is a perfect blackbody):

<KeyEq title="Luminosity of a spherical blackbody">
$$
\term{L}{L} = 4\pi \term{Rstar}{R}^2\sigma \term{Teff}{T_{\rm eff}}^4
$$
<Fragment slot="where">This is *the* formula that turns a star's photosphere into a point on the HR diagram (Chapter 14): measure <span data-term="L">L</span> and <span data-term="Teff">T_eff</span> (from the spectrum) and you get <span data-term="Rstar">R</span> for free. It is also the reason giant stars are cool but huge, and white dwarfs are hot but tiny — luminosity trades radius against temperature to the fourth power.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

<Fermi title="How much does the Sun's luminosity change if T_eff rose by 1%?">
$L \propto T^4$, so $\Delta L/L \approx 4\,\Delta T/T = 4\%$. Small temperature swings in stars matter a lot; conversely, main-sequence lifetimes (Chapter 14) are exquisitely sensitive to mass, which sets $T_{\rm eff}$ steeply via $L \propto M^{3.5}$.
</Fermi>

<Hood title="rendering a physically correct colour from a spectrum">
The star swatch and disk in the flagship sim, and the colour locus figure below, are not looked up from a table — they are computed from Planck's law through the same pipeline the human eye uses, in `src/lib/physics/blackbody.ts`:

1. **Evaluate the spectrum.** Sample $B_\lambda(T)$ across the visible band (380–780 nm here, at 5 nm steps — coarse enough to be cheap, fine enough that the CIE curves below are locally smooth).
2. **Integrate against colour-matching functions.** The CIE 1931 $\bar x(\lambda),\bar y(\lambda),\bar z(\lambda)$ curves encode the eye's three cone responses (approximated here by a fast multi-lobe Gaussian fit, not table lookup):
   ```ts
   export function cieXYZ(nm: number): [number, number, number] {
     const x = 1.056 * g(nm, 599.8, 37.9, 31.0) + 0.362 * g(nm, 442.0, 16.0, 26.7) - 0.065 * g(nm, 501.1, 20.4, 26.2);
     const y = 0.821 * g(nm, 568.8, 46.9, 40.5) + 0.286 * g(nm, 530.9, 16.3, 31.1);
     const z = 1.217 * g(nm, 437.0, 11.8, 36.0) + 0.681 * g(nm, 459.0, 26.0, 13.8);
     return [x, y, z];
   }
   ```
   Summing $B_\lambda(T)\cdot(\bar x,\bar y,\bar z)$ over the sampled wavelengths gives tristimulus values $(X,Y,Z)$ — the same three numbers your retina effectively reports for *any* spectrum, thermal or not.
3. **Convert to linear sRGB** with the standard $3\times3$ matrix, then **gamma-encode** ($u \le 0.0031308$: multiply by 12.92; otherwise $1.055\,u^{1/2.4}-0.055$) to get displayable sRGB.
4. **Normalise and handle out-of-gamut.** Blackbody chromaticities are normalised so the brightest channel is 1 (`blackbodyRGB` reports *colour*, not absolute brightness — a 3 K and a 30,000 K blackbody can have wildly different total power but the function only answers "what hue"). Monochromatic wavelength colours (`wavelengthRGB`, used for the visible-band rainbow) can have a *negative* linear-RGB component — a saturation the sRGB gamut cannot represent — which is desaturated by adding white until all three channels are non-negative, then renormalised.

Because this is real colorimetry, not an artist's palette, it reproduces a genuinely surprising fact: **no blackbody looks green.** Green sits in the middle of the visible band, and a thermal spectrum is smooth and broad — by the time $\bar y$ (green-sensitive) is firing hard, $\bar x$ and $\bar z$ (red/blue-sensitive) are already firing too, because they overlap green's response curve substantially, and a Planck spectrum simply doesn't have a narrow enough hump to excite one cone response without the others. Every blackbody chromaticity, from 1000 K to $\infty$, lies on one curve in colour space — the **Planckian locus** — and that curve passes nowhere near the green corner of the gamut. (A gas-discharge lamp, by contrast, *can* look green — it emits sharp spectral lines, not a smooth thermal continuum.)

Cost: one sim frame recomputes $B_\lambda$ and CIE at ~80 wavelength samples, a $3\times3$ matrix multiply, and a gamma encode — microseconds, and the result is cached per rounded-Kelvin in `blackbodyRGB` since colour only needs to be recomputed when $T$ actually changes.
</Hood>

### Colour index and bolometric correction

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
Rather than measure a star's whole spectrum, astronomers often just compare how bright it looks through two coloured filters — a blue one and a green-yellow one. A hotter, bluer star looks relatively brighter through the blue filter; a cooler, redder star looks relatively brighter through the yellow one. That comparison, expressed as a single number called a **colour index**, is a cheap stand-in for temperature. Because it's measured through specific filters, though, it also misses whatever light falls outside them — a correction factor (the **bolometric correction**) is needed to get back to the star's *true* total light output.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l2">
Astronomers rarely measure a full spectrum for every star; instead they measure flux through two or more standard filters (e.g. Johnson $B$ centred near 440 nm, $V$ near 550 nm) and form a **colour index**, $B-V$, in magnitudes (recall magnitudes are $-2.5\log_{10}$ of a flux ratio — Chapter 1). A hotter star is brighter in $B$ relative to $V$, so $B-V$ is *negative* for hot blue stars and *positive* for cool red ones — the Sun's is $+0.63$.

Because a magnitude system measures light only within specific filters, the *total* (bolometric) luminosity requires a **bolometric correction** $BC = m_{\rm bol} - V$, a (negative, usually) offset that accounts for all the light outside the $V$ band — largest in magnitude for very hot stars (most of their flux is UV) and very cool stars (most of their flux is infrared), smallest for Sun-like G stars whose peak sits right in the visible.
</Fragment>
</Tiers>

<Fermi title="Estimating B−V from Planck ratios alone">
Ignoring real filter response curves and using just the ratio $B_\lambda(440\text{ nm})/B_\lambda(550\text{ nm})$, converted to magnitudes and zero-pointed to the Sun's known $B-V=0.63$, reproduces the right *sign* and *order of magnitude* of real colour indices across spectral types (this is exactly what the "Colour index" readout in the flagship sim reports) — good enough for a back-of-envelope estimate, even though real photometry integrates over broad filter+CCD response curves and isn't perfectly Planckian (see the Sun vs. blackbody comparison next).
</Fermi>

### The Sun's spectrum vs. a perfect blackbody

The Sun's actual spectrum is *close* to a $T_{\rm eff}=5772$ K blackbody but not identical: Fraunhofer absorption lines (Chapter 5) notch out narrow wavelength ranges where the cooler outer photosphere absorbs specific atomic transitions, and the UV is somewhat depleted relative to a perfect Planck curve because those photons are preferentially absorbed higher up. "Blackbody" is an idealisation that real stars — and real anything — only approximate; the approximation is excellent for broadband energetics (Stefan–Boltzmann, Wien's law) and poor for line-by-line spectroscopy, which is precisely why Chapter 5 exists as a separate subject.

<Aside>**Brightness temperature.** For sources that *aren't* thermal blackbodies (synchrotron radio jets, masers), radio astronomers still quote a "brightness temperature" $T_b$: whatever temperature a blackbody would need to match the *observed* $I_\nu$ at that one frequency, via the Rayleigh–Jeans relation $T_b \equiv I_\nu c^2/2\nu^2 k_B$. It is a convenient unit conversion, not a claim that the source is thermal — some radio sources report $T_b \sim 10^{12}$ K, far hotter than any real plasma, a tall tale about synchrotron emission, not temperature.</Aside>

## Planetary equilibrium temperature

<Tiers>
<Fragment slot="l1">
A planet catches sunlight over its disk (a circle, seen from the star) but radiates its own heat away from its entire surface (a sphere). Balance what comes in against what goes out, at whatever temperature makes the books balance, and you get the planet's baseline temperature — before any atmosphere gets involved.
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="l3">
A planet absorbs starlight over its cross-section ($\pi R_p^2$) and re-radiates as a (roughly) uniform blackbody over its full surface ($4\pi R_p^2$), fraction $(1-A)$ of the incident flux absorbed (albedo $A$ reflects the rest straight back into space). Setting absorbed power equal to emitted power at equilibrium:

$$
\pi R_p^2\,(1-A)\,\frac{L_\star}{4\pi a^2} = 4\pi R_p^2\,\sigma T_{\rm eq}^4 \ \Longrightarrow\ T_{\rm eq} = T_\star\sqrt{\frac{R_\star}{2a}}\,(1-A)^{1/4}.
$$
</Fragment>
</Tiers>

<KeyEq title="Planetary equilibrium temperature">
$$
\term{Teq}{T_{\rm eq}} = \term{Teff}{T_\star}\left(\frac{\term{Rstar}{R_\star}}{2\term{aOrb}{a}}\right)^{1/2}(1-\term{Alb}{A})^{1/4}
$$
<Fragment slot="where"><span data-term="Teff">T★</span>, <span data-term="Rstar">R★</span> are the star's effective temperature and radius, <span data-term="aOrb">a</span> is the orbital distance, and <span data-term="Alb">A</span> is the planet's Bond albedo (fraction of incident light reflected, not absorbed). Notice $R_p$ cancelled entirely — <span data-term="Teq">equilibrium temperature</span> doesn't care how big the planet is, only how big and hot its star is and how far away it orbits.</Fragment>
</KeyEq>

A star of <Var name="Tstar2" value={5772} min={2500} max={40000} log /> K, planet at <Var name="a2" value={1} min={0.05} max={5} log /> AU with albedo <Var name="A2" value={0.3} min={0} max={0.95} step={0.01} />, has equilibrium temperature <Out name="Teq" />.

<Aside>**This is not the surface temperature.** Earth's $T_{\rm eq}\approx255$ K (−18 °C) is well below its actual mean surface temperature of 288 K (+15 °C): the extra 33 K is the **greenhouse effect** — an atmosphere transparent to incoming visible light but partly opaque to outgoing infrared traps some of the re-radiated heat before it escapes. Venus's atmosphere pushes this to an extreme: $T_{\rm eq}\approx 232$ K, but the actual surface sits at 737 K, a +500 K runaway greenhouse from its dense CO₂ atmosphere. The formula above sets the *radiative* baseline; the atmosphere (or lack of one, for the Moon) does the rest.</Aside>

## Where we go from here

Planck's law fixes the *continuum*: the smooth thermal glow every hot object makes, purely as a function of temperature. It says nothing about *composition* — sodium and helium and iron all glow with the identical blackbody curve at the same $T$. The sharp absorption and emission lines layered on top of that continuum are where composition, ionisation state and quantum mechanics's discrete energy levels finally enter the story, and unpicking them — Boltzmann and Saha equations, why the spectral sequence runs O B A F G K M and not alphabetically — is [Chapter 5](/ch/spectra/). The effective temperatures and radii this chapter teaches you to extract from a spectrum are also exactly the two coordinates of the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram at the heart of [Chapter 14](/ch/main-sequence/), and the Stefan–Boltzmann law you derived here reappears, barely changed, as the radiation-pressure term inside a star's structure equations in [Chapter 11](/ch/stellar-structure/).
