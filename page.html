Part IV · Relativity & Compact Objects — Chapter 20

Gravitational Waves

When two black holes spiral together they shake spacetime itself. By the time the ripple reaches Earth it stretches a 4-kilometre ruler by a thousandth of a proton, and we can hear it.

Depth of explanation

Ripples in the metric

In Newtonian gravity, if the Sun vanished, the Earth would feel the change instantly. Newton himself found this “action at a distance” absurd. General relativity fixes it: gravity is the geometry of spacetime, and changes in that geometry propagate at a finite speed. They propagate as gravitational waves: travelling wrinkles in the metric, sourced by accelerating masses, moving at the speed of light cc = 2.9979 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹
Speed of light
Exact by definition of the metre..

In Chapter 19 we met the full, non-linear Einstein equations near black holes. Far from any source, spacetime is almost flat, and that is where waves are easy to understand. So we linearise.

Linearised gravity

◐Intuitive version

Imagine spacetime as a vast, stiff trampoline. Heavy objects sit in dents, and that is gravity. Now whirl two heavy balls around each other very fast: the trampoline does not just dent, it ripples, and the ripples spread outward at the speed of light. Those ripples are gravitational waves. (New to waves? See Appendix A8.)

Unlike ripples on water, they do not move things up and down. As a wave passes, space itself is stretched in one direction and squeezed in the other, back and forth. Everything in its path, you included, gets very slightly taller and thinner, then shorter and wider. The effect is absurdly small: a wave from a black-hole collision a billion light-years away changes the size of the Earth by less than the width of an atomic nucleus.

x²High-school math version

General relativity describes gravity with the metric, a table of numbers that says how to turn a coordinate difference into a real distance. Far from any source, space is almost flat, so we write the metric as “flat space plus a tiny correction”:

g=η+h,h≪1.\term{g}{g} = \term{eta}{\eta} + \term{h}{h}, \qquad \term{h}{h} \ll 1.g=η+h,h≪1.

Here η\etaη is ordinary flat spacetime and hhh is a small, dimensionless number called the strain. Einstein’s equations then say that hhh obeys a wave equation, like sound or light: disturbances in hhh travel outward at speed ccc (see Appendix A8 for waves), and their size falls off as 1/distance. A strain hhh means that a ruler of length LLL changes length by about hLhLhL.

⌛ History1916–1957
Do gravitational waves exist?

Einstein predicted gravitational waves in 1916, months after finishing general relativity, and got the formula for their power wrong by a factor of two; he corrected it in 1918, giving the quadrupole formula we still use. Then he spent two decades unsure whether the waves were real or an artefact of his coordinates. Arthur Eddington quipped that some kinds of wave in the theory travelled “with the speed of thought”, because a change of coordinates could make them go at any speed.

In 1936 Einstein and his assistant Nathan Rosen sent Physical Review a paper claiming gravitational waves did not exist. The anonymous referee, Howard Robertson, pointed out that their troublesome singularity was just a badly chosen coordinate system. Einstein, unused to anonymous peer review, withdrew the paper in indignation and never published in that journal again, but the version that finally appeared quietly reversed the conclusion.

The question was settled at the 1957 Chapel Hill conference. Felix Pirani showed how a wave changes the separation of free particles, and Richard Feynman offered the “sticky bead” argument: thread two beads loosely on a rigid rod; the passing wave slides them back and forth along it, friction heats the rod, so the wave must carry energy. Joseph Weber, in the audience, went home and began building the first detector.

The transverse–traceless gauge

◐Intuitive version

A gravitational wave squeezes and stretches only across its direction of travel, never along it. And whatever it stretches one way, it squeezes the other way by the same amount. There are two basic patterns, called plus (+) and cross (×): the plus pattern stretches up–down while squeezing left–right, and the cross pattern does the same along the diagonals. Any gravitational wave is a mix of the two.

x²High-school math version

A gravitational wave only acts across its direction of travel (it is transverse, like light), and it always stretches one direction while squeezing the perpendicular one by the same amount (it is traceless: no net change of area). That leaves just two independent patterns, the wave’s two polarisations: h+h_+h+​ (“plus”) stretches along xxx while squeezing yyy, and h×h_\timesh×​ (“cross”) does the same along the diagonals. Light’s polarisation pattern repeats when you rotate it by 360°; a gravitational-wave pattern repeats after 180°, and rotating the plus pattern by just 45° turns it into the cross pattern.

∫Engineering math version

Even in Lorenz gauge there is leftover freedom. Use it all up and a plane wave travelling along zzz can be written with only two independent numbers. This is the transverse–traceless (TT) gauge:

Transverse: nothing in the ttt or zzz rows, so the wave only acts in the plane perpendicular to its motion. Traceless: the diagonal entries are +h++h_++h+​ and −h+-h_+−h+​, so whatever it stretches in one direction it squeezes in the other. The two numbers h+h_+h+​ (“plus”) and h×h_\timesh×​ (“cross”) are the wave’s two polarisations. Electromagnetic waves also have two polarisations, but they are vectors, and a vector pattern repeats after rotating by 360°. The gravitational polarisations are tensors, and they repeat after 180°: rotate the plus pattern by 45° and it becomes the cross pattern.

What a wave does to a ring of masses

◐Intuitive version

Picture a circle of tiny floating beads in space, facing the wave. As it passes, the circle is squashed into an oval pointing up–down, then into an oval pointing left–right, back and forth at the wave’s frequency. The beads are not pushed by any force: the space between them changes. That is exactly what a detector like LIGO measures, with mirrors in place of beads.

x²High-school math version

Coordinates in TT gauge are tied to freely falling particles, so a single particle does not “move” in them. But the proper distance between two free particles does change. For particles at separation (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) in the transverse plane, to first order in hhh the physical displacement is

Why the quadrupole?

◐Intuitive version

Why don’t all moving masses make strong waves? Electric charges radiate light when they simply jiggle back and forth. Masses cannot do the gravitational version of that: if one part of an isolated system lurches one way, the rest must lurch the other way (momentum is conserved; see Appendix A6), so the centre of mass never jiggles. The first thing that can change is the shape of the mass distribution: how stretched-out or lopsided it is. A spinning dumbbell or two stars orbiting each other keep changing their shape as seen from outside, so they radiate. A perfectly round spinning ball does not.

x²High-school math version

Electric charges radiate when they jiggle back and forth: the “dipole”, charge times position, oscillates. The gravitational version is mass times position, which is just the total mass times the position of the centre of mass. For an isolated system the centre of mass moves at constant speed (momentum conservation), so this “mass dipole” can never oscillate, and gravity has no dipole radiation. The same goes for a pulsating round star: its total mass does not change, so it stays silent.

What can change is the quadrupole moment III, roughly mass × (size)², measuring how stretched-out the mass distribution is along each direction. For a dumbbell of mass MMM and length 2R2R2R spinning end over end, I∼MR2I \sim MR^2I∼MR2 and it oscillates twice per turn. Binaries and lumpy neutron stars have a changing III; a spinning sphere or cylinder does not.

∫Engineering math version

Electromagnetic radiation is dominated by the dipole: a charge sloshing back and forth radiates power P∝d⃗¨ 2P \propto \ddot{\vec d}^{\,2}P∝d¨2, where d⃗=∑q r⃗\vec d = \sum q\,\vec rd=∑qr is the dipole moment (Larmor’s formula). Gravity has no dipole radiation, and the reason is momentum conservation.

The gravitational analogue of the dipole moment is the mass dipole d⃗=∑m r⃗=MR⃗cm\vec d = \sum m\,\vec r = M\vec R_{\rm cm}d=∑mr=MRcm​. So the source term that would drive dipole radiation vanishes identically. The “magnetic” dipole is the angular momentum ∑m r⃗×v⃗\sum m\,\vec r\times\vec v∑mr×v, which is also conserved. In electromagnetism the charge-to-mass ratio differs between particles (electrons and protons slosh differently), so the dipole survives; in gravity every “charge” is its own inertial mass, and the equivalence principle kills the dipole.

Monopole too. A spherically symmetric star that pulsates does not radiate either. The monopole moment is the total mass–energy, which is conserved, and Birkhoff’s theorem says the exterior of any spherical body is static Schwarzschild. You need a lopsided, changing mass distribution.

The quadrupole formula by dimensional analysis

◐Intuitive version

How strong are the waves? The answer involves the speed of light raised to the fifth power in the denominator, which is why they are so weak. Only a system that is both extremely compact (packed nearly as tightly as a black hole) and extremely fast (moving at a sizeable fraction of light speed) radiates a lot. For such a system the potential power is staggering: up to about 3.6×10523.6\times10^{52}3.6×1052 watts, more than all the stars in the visible universe. For anything on Earth, the power works out to essentially nothing, as the estimate below shows.

The inspiral chirp

◐Intuitive version

For two stars or black holes in orbit, the waves come out at twice the orbital frequency, because the pair looks the same after half a turn. The power depends ferociously on how close they are: halve the distance between them and the power goes up 32 times. The Earth–Sun system radiates about 200 watts, a desk lamp’s worth, and would take 102310^{23}1023 years to spiral in. Two 30-solar-mass black holes 350 km apart radiate 104910^{49}1049 watts.

Orbital decay and the chirp mass

◐Intuitive version

The energy carried off by the waves comes out of the orbit, so the two bodies sink closer together. Merger and ringdown

The quadrupole formula assumes slow motion and weak fields. Both fail at the end. Around the innermost stable circular orbit (a≈6GM/c2a \approx 6GM/c^2a≈6GM/c2 for a test particle, Chapter 19) the orbit stops being an orbit and the holes plunge. From there on only numerical relativity, solving the full Einstein equations on supercomputers (a problem cracked in 2005 by Frans Pretorius and, weeks later, by the Brownsville and NASA Goddard groups), gives the waveform. In practice, analyses use waveform families (effective-one-body, phenomenological, NR surrogates) calibrated to hundreds of such simulations.

Qualitatively: the frequency and amplitude peak as the horizons merge into a single, distorted, spinning black hole. That remnant then sheds its lumps by ringing like a struck bell. The ringing is a set of quasinormal modes: damped sinusoids whose frequencies and damping times depend only on the final mass and spin (the no-hair theorem made audible). For the dominant mode of a remnant with spin χ≈0.7\chi \approx 0.7χ≈0.7,

fQNM≈1.7 kHz(10 M⊙Mf),τ≈0.6 ms(Mf10 M⊙).f_{\rm QNM} \approx 1.7\,\text{kHz}\left(\frac{10\,M_\odot}{M_f}\right),\qquad \tau \approx 0.6\,\text{ms}\left(\frac{M_f}{10\,M_\odot}\right).fQNM​≈1.7kHz(Mf​10M⊙​​),τ≈0.6ms(10M⊙​Mf​​).

A 62 M⊙62\,M_\odot62M⊙​ remnant rings at about 280 Hz and dies away within a few milliseconds. Measuring two or more modes and checking they agree with one Kerr black hole is “black-hole spectroscopy”, now a routine test of GR with O4 events.

Where the energy goes. GW150914 radiated 3 M⊙c2≈5×10473\,M_\odot c^2 \approx 5\times10^{47}3M⊙​c2≈5×1047 J, most of it in the last 0.2 s. Its peak luminosity, 3.6×10493.6\times10^{49}3.6×1049 W, exceeded the combined light output of every star in the observable universe by a factor of ~50.

Hearing the first chirps

The Hulse–Taylor pulsar

The first evidence came not from a detector but from a clock. In 1974 Russell Hulse and Joseph Taylor found PSR B1913+16: a pulsar (a spinning neutron star, Chapter 18) in a 7.75-hour, highly eccentric (e=0.617e = 0.617e=0.617) orbit around another neutron star. Pulsars are superb clocks, and timing them for years revealed the orbit’s periastron advancing by 4.2° per year (the relativistic precession of Mercury’s orbit, 35,000 times faster), the Einstein delay, and from these the two masses: 1.4381.4381.438 and 1.390 M⊙1.390\,M_\odot1.390M⊙​.

With both masses known, the quadrupole formula (extended to eccentric orbits by Peters and Mathews in 1963) predicts, with no free parameters, how fast the orbit must shrink. For an orbital period of 7.752 hours and eccentricity 0.617, the period should change at …, and the two stars merge in …. ⌛ History1960–2015
From Weber's bars to LIGO

Joseph Weber built the first detectors in the 1960s: aluminium cylinders two metres long, ringing at 1660 Hz, wrapped in piezoelectric sensors. In 1969 he announced coincident events between bars 1000 km apart. Nobody could reproduce them, and the implied source strength was absurd, but he had made the field an experimental science.

In 1972 Rainer Weiss at MIT, preparing a course on general relativity, worked out as an exercise how a laser interferometer could measure such tiny strains, and listed every noise source that would fight it. That report still reads like a blueprint for LIGO. At Caltech, Kip Thorne built the theory and science case, and Ronald Drever brought optical tricks such as power recycling. After decades of prototypes, fights over cost and a management overhaul under Barry Barish in the 1990s, initial LIGO ran from 2002 to 2010 and saw nothing, as expected at its sensitivity. Advanced LIGO switched on in September 2015 and heard GW150914 within days. Weiss, Thorne and Barish shared the 2017 Nobel Prize; Drever had died that March.

GW150914

On 14 September 2015, two days into the first observing run and before it had officially started, both Advanced LIGO detectors recorded a 0.2-second chirp sweeping from 35 to 250 Hz, with peak strain 1.0×10−211.0\times10^{-21}1.0×10−21. Hanford and Livingston saw it 7 milliseconds apart, consistent with a wave crossing 3000 km at ccc. Detectors: measuring a thousandth of a proton

The Michelson interferometer

LIGO is a Michelson interferometer with 4-km arms at right angles. Laser light is split, sent down both arms, reflected back and recombined. A plus-polarised wave from overhead stretches one arm and squeezes the other, so the light returning from the two arms acquires a phase difference, and the output port brightens and dims in step with h(t)h(t)h(t). The instrument is a differential ruler: it is insensitive to anything that changes both arms equally (laser frequency noise, for instance), and it has a broad antenna pattern, so it hears the whole sky at once rather than pointing.

≈ How small is 10⁻²¹?

A strain of h=10−21h = 10^{-21}h=10−21 over L=4L = 4L=4 km changes the arm length by

ΔL∼hL=10−21×4×103 m=4×10−18 m.\Delta L \sim hL = 10^{-21}\times4\times10^3\ \text{m} = 4\times10^{-18}\ \text{m}.ΔL∼hL=10−21×4×103 m=4×10−18 m.

A proton is 1.7×10−151.7\times10^{-15}1.7×10−15 m across. So LIGO measures arm-length changes about 1/400 of a proton’s diameter, using mirrors made of 102510^{25}1025 atoms whose surfaces jiggle thermally by far more than that. It works because the laser beam is ~10 cm wide and averages over trillions of atoms, and because the measurement is a frequency-resolved one: only noise near 100 Hz matters. The equivalent measurement for the Earth–Sun distance would be accurate to the width of an atom.

Several tricks turn a tabletop Michelson into that. Fabry–Pérot cavities in each arm bounce the light ~300 times, making the effective arm 1200 km long. Power recycling returns the light that would leave the bright port, building 750 kW of circulating power from a 100 W laser. Signal recycling shapes the frequency response. And since O3, squeezed light (a quantum state of the vacuum with reduced phase fluctuations) is injected into the dark port, and in O4 it is frequency-dependent, reducing quantum noise across the band.

Noise

The sensitivity is set by a noise budget with three main pieces, which you can see in the shape of LIGO’s curve in the figure further down:

  • Seismic and Newtonian noise below ~10–20 Hz. Mirrors hang from four-stage pendulums on active isolation platforms, suppressing ground motion by 101010^{10}1010 at 10 Hz. But the gravitational pull of moving ground and air (“Newtonian noise”) cannot be shielded, which is why future detectors plan to go underground.
  • Thermal noise at 50–200 Hz: Brownian motion in the mirror coatings and suspension fibres, reduced by fused-silica monolithic suspensions and low-loss coatings.
  • Quantum shot noise at high frequency (photon counting statistics) and radiation-pressure noise at low frequency (random photon kicks). Squeezing trades one against the other.

The result is a bucket-shaped amplitude spectral density with a minimum of about 3×10−24 Hz−1/23\times10^{-24}\ \text{Hz}^{-1/2}3×10−24 Hz−1/2 near 200 Hz at design sensitivity.

Matched filtering

◐Intuitive version

A signal a thousand times weaker than the detector noise cannot be seen by eye. Instead, computers compare the data with a huge library of predicted chirp shapes, sliding each one along the data to see how well it lines up. Random noise lines up only by chance; a real chirp lines up cycle after cycle, so the match builds and builds. This is matched filtering, the same idea radar uses to spot faint echoes.

x²High-school math version

A signal of h∼10−21h \sim 10^{-21}h∼10−21 in data whose rms noise is larger is not visible by eye. It is found the way a radar finds a weak echo: by matched filtering. The optimal method is to correlate the data with the expected signal shape (a template), giving each frequency more weight where the detector is quieter. The result is the signal-to-noise ratio ρ\rhoρ; a candidate needs ρ\rhoρ above about 8. A chirp with NNN cycles builds up its SNR roughly like N\sqrt NN​ times the strength of a single cycle, so long chirps are found even when each individual cycle is buried.

If the noise is Gaussian and you know the signal shape h(t)h(t)h(t), the optimal linear statistic is the correlation of the data with the template, weighted by the inverse noise spectrum:

For engineers: this is a cross-correlation done in the frequency domain (Appendix A8 covers Fourier transforms) (one FFT of the data, one multiply per template, one inverse FFT gives ρ\rhoρ for every arrival time at once), after whitening the data by dividing by the noise amplitude spectrum. Using a complex (analytic) template maximises over the unknown phase for free. A signal with NNN cycles builds up SNR like N\sqrt NN​ times the per-cycle amplitude, so long, coherent chirps are found even when every individual cycle is buried.

Because we don’t know the masses in advance, searches correlate against a template bank: hundreds of thousands of waveforms tiling the space of masses and spins so densely that no real signal loses more than ~3% of its SNR. Candidates need ρ≳8\rho \gtrsim 8ρ≳8 in the network plus consistency checks (a χ2\chi^2χ2 test that the SNR accumulates across frequency the way a real chirp would, and coincidence between detectors), and their significance is calibrated by time-shifting one detector’s data against another’s thousands of times to build an empirical background.

Note the width of the SNR peak: templates within a few per cent of the true chirp mass still match well, because the phase evolution, which is what the filter locks onto, depends on M\mathcal MM to leading order. The individual masses (through η\etaη) and the spins only enter at higher post-Newtonian order, which is why they are measured far less precisely.

The gravitational-wave sky in 2026

LIGO, Virgo and KAGRA: the O4 catalogue

The fourth observing run, O4, ran from May 2023 to November 2025 with the two LIGO detectors, Virgo and (for parts of it) KAGRA in Japan, reaching binary-neutron-star ranges of 150–170 Mpc for LIGO. It roughly tripled the catalogue: around 90 confident detections from O1–O3 grew by more than 200 significant public alerts in O4, and the cumulative count now exceeds 300 candidates. A merger is found every two to three days. Highlights so far:

  • GW230529: a merger of a ~1.4 M⊙1.4\,M_\odot1.4M⊙​ neutron star with a compact object of about 2.52.52.5–4.5 M⊙4.5\,M_\odot4.5M⊙​, squarely in the “lower mass gap” between the heaviest neutron stars and the lightest black holes seen in X-ray binaries. The gap is less empty than we thought.
  • GW231123: the most massive binary yet, with two black holes of roughly 100100100 and 140 M⊙140\,M_\odot140M⊙​ merging into a ~225 M⊙225\,M_\odot225M⊙​ remnant, both spinning very fast. Such masses lie in or above the pair-instability gap, where stars should explode without leaving a black hole (Chapter 16), suggesting the components were themselves products of earlier mergers in dense clusters.
  • GW250114: the loudest event yet (network SNR ~80), which let LVK measure several ringdown modes and confirm Hawking’s area theorem (the total horizon area increased) at high confidence.

The population now tells its own story: a peak of black-hole masses near 10 M⊙10\,M_\odot10M⊙​ and a second bump near 35 M⊙35\,M_\odot35M⊙​, mostly small spins, and a merger rate of roughly 20–40 Gpc⁻³ yr⁻¹ for binary black holes. The next run, O5, is planned after upgrades later this decade, and third-generation detectors (Einstein Telescope in Europe, Cosmic Explorer in the US, with 10–40 km arms) are in design for the late 2030s.

LISA: gravitational waves from space

On Earth, seismic noise walls off everything below ~10 Hz. Supermassive black-hole binaries, with M∼105\mathcal M \sim 10^5M∼105–107 M⊙10^7\,M_\odot107M⊙​, radiate at millihertz. The Laser Interferometer Space Antenna, adopted by ESA in January 2024 with NASA participation and planned for launch around 2035, will fly three spacecraft in a triangle with 2.5-million-km arms, trailing the Earth around the Sun. Its test masses float freely inside the spacecraft, which fly around them. LISA Pathfinder (2015–2017) showed the required free-fall is achievable, and beat its requirement.

LISA will hear supermassive black holes merging out to redshift ~15, stellar-mass objects spiralling into them (extreme mass-ratio inspirals, which map the spacetime of a Kerr hole over 10510^5105 orbits), and tens of thousands of white-dwarf binaries in our own Galaxy, some already known as “verification binaries” from their light curves. So many of them overlap that below ~3 mHz they will form a confusion foreground: the first time a noise source will be the Milky Way itself.

Pulsar timing arrays and the nanohertz background

At nanohertz frequencies (periods of years), the detector is the Galaxy. A pulsar timing array monitors dozens of millisecond pulsars, each ticking with a stability that rivals atomic clocks. A passing wave stretches the Earth–pulsar path and shifts the arrival times of pulses by tens of nanoseconds. Individual pulsars cannot distinguish that from their own spin noise, but a gravitational-wave background produces a distinctive correlation between pairs of pulsars as a function of their angular separation on the sky: the Hellings–Downs curve, a quadrupolar fingerprint.

In June 2023, NANOGrav (15 years, 68 pulsars), together with the European, Parkes, Chinese and Indian PTAs, announced evidence at the 3–4σ level for this correlation, and a common red-noise process of amplitude hc≈2.4×10−15h_c \approx 2.4\times10^{-15}hc​≈2.4×10−15 at a frequency of 1/yr. The most likely source is the combined hum of supermassive black-hole binaries in galaxy nuclei across the universe, each with periods of months to years and millions of years from merger. Its amplitude is somewhat higher than many models predicted, which may say something about how quickly galaxy mergers bring black holes together (Chapter 23), or hint at more exotic early-universe sources. The International PTA data combination aims at a 5σ detection over the next few years.

The gap between LISA and LIGO (0.1–10 Hz) is the target of proposed mid-band missions such as DECIGO.

Where we go from here

A century after Einstein doubted they were real, gravitational waves are a routine observational channel. They give us things light cannot: the masses and spins of black holes that emit nothing else, distances without a ladder, a direct probe of strong-field gravity, and a view through the dust of galaxy centres. Every event so far agrees with general relativity, from the phase of the inspiral to the pitch of the ringdown.

The ideas of this chapter recur throughout the rest of the course. The mergers seen here are the endpoints of the stellar evolution in Chapters 11–19, and the nanohertz background connects to the growth of supermassive black holes in the galaxy mergers of Chapter 23. The standard sirens enter the cosmology of Chapter 25. And somewhere beyond all current detectors lies a primordial background, from inflation itself, whose imprint on the polarisation of the cosmic microwave background (Chapter 27) is one of the most sought-after signals in physics.
