CourseGPT has read a billion tokens of children’s stories, and it knows a great deal about them: how they begin, which names go with which pronouns, what happens after a dog finds a ball. But it can only do one thing with that knowledge: continue whatever text it is given. Ask it for “a story about a girl named Mia who loses her kite by the river” and it will continue the request as if it were the start of a story, which it is not.

**Fine-tuning** is how a pre-trained model is adapted to a task: keep training it, from its pre-trained weights, on a small dataset of examples of the new behaviour. Howard and Ruder showed in 2018 that fine-tuning a pre-trained language model beat training task-specific models from scratch :cite[howard2018], and GPT-style models have been adapted this way ever since. For assistants the examples are instructions paired with good responses, and training on them is **supervised fine-tuning** (SFT), the first step of turning a base model into a chat model :cite[ouyang2022]. Chapter 21 adds the second step, learning from preferences.

## An instruction format

We need instructions paired with responses that follow them. For stories, we can make them for free: take a story, pull out the main character’s name (the word after “named”) and three words that appear in it, and write the instruction the story satisfies:

```
Write a story about Lucy that uses the words: gasped, lesson, candle.
Story: Once upon a time there was a little girl named Lucy. …<|endoftext|>
```

Every story is a correct response to its own instruction. The three words are chosen among words that are fairly rare — used in fewer than 3% of stories — so that satisfying the instruction takes a deliberate choice, not luck. Of the validation stories, 61% name a character; we use those, 40,000 of them for training.

Instruction datasets for real assistants are made the same way in spirit — from existing text reshaped into tasks, as in FLAN :cite[wei2022flan], or written by people, or generated by other models. Quality matters more than quantity: LIMA fine-tuned a 65-billion-parameter model on just 1,000 carefully chosen examples and found it responded about as well as models tuned on far more :cite[zhou2023lima].

## Training only on the response

The training example is the concatenation of instruction and story, but the loss should only count the story. The instruction is given, and there is nothing to be gained by learning to predict it; worse, training on it would teach the model to write instructions. **Loss masking** sets the target at every instruction position to an ignored value, so the average runs over response tokens only — including the final `<|endoftext|>`, which teaches the model to stop.

## LoRA: fine-tuning a few directions

Full fine-tuning updates all 30 million parameters, and needs the gradients and optimiser state for all of them: 16 bytes per parameter in mixed precision (Chapter 14). For CourseGPT that is nothing. For a 70-billion-parameter model it is more than a terabyte, and every fine-tuned version is another full copy of the weights.

Fine-tuning, though, changes the weights only a little, and Aghajanyan and colleagues found that the change has a low **intrinsic dimension**: a few hundred well-chosen directions in parameter space are enough to fine-tune well :cite[aghajanyan2021]. **LoRA** (low-rank adaptation) :cite[hu2022lora] builds that into the method. Each weight matrix $W$ is frozen, and only a low-rank update is trained:

:::equation{#lora caption="LoRA: a frozen weight matrix plus a trainable low-rank update, scaled by α/r."}
$$
W' \;=\; W \;+\; \frac{\alpha}{r}\, \term{A}{A}\, \term{B}{B}, \qquad A \in \mathbb R^{n \times r},\;\; B \in \mathbb R^{r \times m},\;\; r \ll \min(n, m)
$$
:::

```terms
A:
  label: "$A$ — the down-projection"
  what: n × r, initialised at random. It picks r directions in the input space.
B:
  label: "$B$ — the up-projection"
  what: r × m, initialised to zero, so W′ = W at the start and fine-tuning begins exactly from the pre-trained model.
  effect: Together, A and B have r(n + m) parameters instead of nm. For a 512 × 2,048 matrix at rank 16, that is 40,960 instead of about a million — 4%.
```

During training the update is applied as $\mathbf x W + \frac{\alpha}{r}(\mathbf x A) B$, never forming $AB$. Afterwards it can be **merged**, $W' = W + \frac{\alpha}{r}AB$, and the model runs exactly as fast as before. Or it can be kept separate: one base model, and a small file of LoRA weights per task, swapped in as needed. **QLoRA** :cite[dettmers2023qlora] goes further and keeps the frozen base model in 4-bit precision (Chapter 16), which is how 65-billion-parameter models came to be fine-tuned on a single GPU.

::exercise{id="lora"}

Is the weight change of a full fine-tune really low-rank? We can check: train CourseGPT fully, subtract the original weights, and look at the singular values of the difference.

::lora-spectrum

The answer, for our fine-tune, is: not very. A 512 × 512 matrix has 512 singular values, and the top 16 hold between 19% and 54% of the change’s energy (its squared Frobenius norm) — most in the first layer’s queries, least in the middle of the network. The rest is spread thinly over hundreds of directions. Yet, as the next section shows, rank-16 LoRA learns most of what full fine-tuning learns. Both can be true: much of a full fine-tune’s change is small, scattered and unnecessary, the drift of 30 million parameters each nudged by noisy gradients, and a low-rank update can capture the part that matters for the task without reproducing the rest. This is also what the intrinsic-dimension experiments found: a random low-dimensional subspace is enough to reach most of a fine-tune’s performance, not to reproduce its weights.

LoRA is one of a family of **parameter-efficient** fine-tuning methods. **Adapters** insert small MLPs into each block :cite[houlsby2019]; **prompt tuning** learns a few embedding vectors prepended to every input and trains nothing else :cite[lester2021]. LoRA won out because it adds no computation after merging and works at every scale.

## Measured: full fine-tuning against LoRA

We fine-tuned CourseGPT for 3,000 steps of 32 examples (about two and a half passes over the 40,000 pairs): once fully, with AdamW at a learning rate of $10^{-4}$, and three times with LoRA on every matrix of every block, at ranks 16, 4 and 1, with a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-3}$ (LoRA needs a higher rate, since its update starts at zero and moves in few directions). Then each model wrote a story for each of 200 held-out instructions.

::finetune-results

Fine-tuning taught CourseGPT the format — every fine-tuned model writes a story about the named character almost every time (98–99%, against 94% for the base model reading the same instruction) — but using the three chosen words is hard for it. The base model used 23% of them, mostly by chance or by copying from the instruction; full fine-tuning raised that to 46%, and LoRA to 41%, 36% and 34% at ranks 16, 4 and 1. Only 10% of the full fine-tune’s stories used all three words and the name. The words are rare by design, and fitting three of them into a coherent story is a real test for a 30-million-parameter model: in the samples, it often starts a perfectly good story about Tom and simply forgets the words. More rank buys more of the task, and full fine-tuning, with 25 times as many trainable parameters as rank-16 LoRA, learned the most.

The costs differ less than the parameter counts suggest. LoRA’s memory use was 4.45 GiB against 4.71 for full fine-tuning: for a model this small, activations dominate, and what LoRA saves — gradients and Adam’s two moments for the frozen weights, 12 bytes per parameter in float32 — comes to only 0.36 GB for 30 million parameters. For a 70-billion-parameter model the same saving is 840 GB.

## Forgetting

A network trained on a new task tends to lose some of what it knew, a problem known since the early days of neural networks as **catastrophic forgetting** :cite[kirkpatrick2017]. The plain-story loss in the table measures it: how well each fine-tuned model still predicts ordinary TinyStories without an instruction. Every fine-tune made CourseGPT slightly worse at plain stories, and broadly, the more it learned, the more it forgot: the loss rose from 1.653 bits per token to 1.746 after full fine-tuning (+0.09), 1.718 with rank-16 LoRA (+0.07), and about 1.70 at ranks 4 and 1 (+0.05). LoRA’s few directions leave the rest of the model untouched. Biderman and colleagues found the same pattern at scale, and titled their paper accordingly: *LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less* :cite[biderman2024].

:::history{year=2021 title="LoRA" people="Edward Hu, Yelong Shen, Phillip Wallis, Zeyuan Allen-Zhu, Yuanzhi Li, Shean Wang, Lu Wang and Weizhu Chen"}
When GPT-3 arrived in 2020, fine-tuning it meant storing a new copy of 175 billion parameters for every task. Hu and colleagues at Microsoft proposed training only low-rank updates to its attention matrices :cite[hu2022lora], and matched full fine-tuning with ten thousand times fewer trainable parameters and a third of the GPU memory. The method was simple enough to implement in an afternoon, and it became the default way to adapt open models: when Meta’s Llama weights became available in 2023, thousands of LoRA fine-tunes appeared within months, many trained on a single consumer GPU with QLoRA.
:::

## Lab: fine-tuning in PyTorch

```sh
uv run lmc ch20 finetune    # full and LoRA r = 16, 4, 1 (about 20 minutes on an RTX 4060 Ti)
uv run lmc ch20 evaluate    # instruction following and plain-story loss
uv run lmc ch20 spectrum    # singular values of the full fine-tune's weight change
uv run lmc ch20 export      # the merged LoRA model, for the browser
```

`add_lora` in `lmcourse/ch20.py` uses PyTorch’s parametrisation mechanism: each targeted weight is replaced by a small module computing $W + \frac{\alpha}{r}AB$, so the model’s own code is untouched, and `remove_parametrizations(…, leave_parametrized=True)` merges the update into the weight.

:::exercises
1. **Which matrices?** The original LoRA paper adapted only the attention’s query and value matrices. Train rank-16 LoRA on those only, then on the MLPs only. Which matters more for our task?
2. **Data size.** Fine-tune on 1,000, 5,000 and 40,000 examples for the same number of steps. How much data does instruction following need?
3. **Harder instructions.** Add a constraint on the ending (“The story ends sadly”) using a simple classifier of your own for the last sentence. Does LoRA still learn it?
4. **Many adapters.** Train two LoRA adapters for different instruction formats, and switch between them in the browser without reloading the base model.
:::

## Check your understanding

```quiz
q: "Why is the loss masked on the instruction tokens?"
options:
  - text: "The instruction is given at test time; the model should learn to produce responses, not to predict or write instructions."
    correct: true
    why: The gradient should come only from the behaviour we want.
  - text: Instruction tokens are too hard to predict.
    why: Difficulty is not the reason; relevance is.
  - text: It makes training faster.
    why: The forward and backward passes cost the same either way.
```

```quiz
q: "Why is LoRA's B initialised to zero?"
options:
  - text: "So that A·B = 0 and the adapted model starts exactly as the pre-trained one."
    correct: true
    why: Fine-tuning begins from the pre-trained behaviour, not from a random perturbation of it.
  - text: So that A receives no gradient.
    why: A receives a gradient once B moves away from zero.
  - text: To make the update full-rank.
    why: The update has rank at most r however it is initialised.
```

```quiz
q: "How many trainable parameters does rank-8 LoRA add to a 1,024 × 1,024 matrix?"
options:
  - text: "8 × (1,024 + 1,024) = 16,384, about 1.6% of the matrix."
    correct: true
    why: A is 1,024 × 8 and B is 8 × 1,024.
  - text: "8 × 1,024 = 8,192"
    why: That counts only one of the two factors.
  - text: 1,048,576, the same as the matrix.
    why: That would be full fine-tuning.
```
