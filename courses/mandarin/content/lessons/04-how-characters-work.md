There are some myths about Chinese characters. You need tens of thousands of them. Each one is a little picture. You just have to memorise them one by one.

None of these is true. A dictionary may list 50,000 characters, but most are archaic or obscure. **About 1,000 characters cover roughly 90% of everything you will read**, and the HSK 2 vocabulary uses fewer than 600. Very few characters are pictures any more. And most of them are built from a small kit of parts, in a way that gives you real clues to their meaning and sound.

This lesson opens the kit.

## Pictures that became characters

Some of the oldest characters really did start as drawings, scratched on turtle shells and ox bones over 3,000 years ago to ask the gods questions. Centuries of brush and ink smoothed and squared them, but some still resemble their pictures. Guess what each sketch shows, then tap to turn it over.

::pictographs

Squint at 山 and you can still see the mountain range; 木 still has its branches and roots; 门 has the doors of a saloon. Others, like 马 (a horse, turned on its end), need some imagination.


:::note
Pictographs are only about 4% of characters. They matter far more than that number suggests, though, because they are the **building blocks** of most other characters.
:::

## Putting ideas together

Ancient scribes ran out of things to draw quickly: how do you draw "rest" or "bright"? One answer was to combine pictures into an idea:

- 人 person + 木 tree = 休, a person leaning against a tree: **to rest**.
- 日 sun + 月 moon = 明, the two great lights: **bright**.
- 女 woman + 子 child = 好: **good**.
- 木 tree + 木 tree = 林, a grove; three trees, 森, a forest.

When a part sits on the left of a character it often gets squeezed. 人 becomes 亻 in 休, 你 and 他; 水 becomes three drops, 氵.

## Sound plus meaning: the real secret

Here is the most important idea in this lesson. About **80% of characters** are built the same way: one part hints at the **meaning** and the other at the **sound**.

Take 马 mǎ, horse. Add parts that suggest meaning, and you get a family of characters that all sound like *ma*:

| Character | Meaning part | Sound part | Reading | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 妈 | 女 woman | 马 mǎ | mā | mum |
| 吗 | 口 mouth (a spoken word) | 马 mǎ | ma | question particle |
| 码 | 石 stone | 马 mǎ | mǎ | code, number |
| 骂 | 口口 two mouths | 马 mǎ | mà | to scold |

Or take 青 qīng (blue-green). Add water and you get 清 qīng, clear water; add the sun, 晴 qíng, sunny; add speech, 请 qǐng, please; add a heart, 情 qíng, feeling.

The sound hint is only a hint: sounds have drifted over 3,000 years, and tones often differ. But it helps more often than not, and the meaning part is usually spot on. When you meet a new character with 氵 on the left, it almost certainly has something to do with water.

::char-builder{kind="sound"}

```choose
title: Read the clues
items:
  - prompt: 河 has 氵 (water) on the left and 可 *kě* on the right. What does it probably mean?
    zh: 河
    noPinyin: true
    options: [a river, a song, to hit, a horse]
    answer: 0
    explain: 河 hé, "river". Water gives the meaning; 可 once gave the sound.
  - prompt: 请 means "please". Which part gives the meaning?
    zh: 请
    options: [讠 speech, 青 blue-green, both]
    answer: 0
    explain: Saying please is a speech act, so 讠 (speech) gives the meaning, and 青 qīng the sound of qǐng.
  - prompt: 吗 *ma* sounds like 马 *mǎ*. Why does it have 口 on the left?
    zh: 吗
    options: [It is about horses' mouths, It is a spoken word with no meaning of its own, It means "to eat"]
    answer: 1
    explain: 口 marks many particles and sounds, words that only exist in speech.
  - prompt: Which character is probably about **feelings**?
    options: [清, 晴, 情, 请]
    answer: 2
    explain: 情 qíng has the heart radical 忄.
```

## Words are made of characters

Here is the second big secret: **a character is not a word**. Most modern Chinese words have two characters, and their meanings combine in ways that are often delightful:

| Word | Literally | Means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 电脑 diànnǎo | electric brain | computer |
| 电话 diànhuà | electric speech | telephone |
| 电影 diànyǐng | electric shadow | film |
| 电视 diànshì | electric vision | television |
| 火车 huǒchē | fire vehicle | train |
| 汉字 hànzì | Han (Chinese) characters | Chinese characters |
| 学生 xuésheng | study + person | student |

Once you know 电 means electricity, a whole family of modern words opens up. This is why learning characters gets *easier* the more you know: new words are mostly old characters in new combinations.

:::culture
Chinese characters were once used across East Asia. Japanese still uses them (as *kanji*), Korean used them until the 20th century, and so did Vietnamese. Mainland China simplified about 2,000 characters in the 1950s and 60s: 馬 became 马, 媽 became 妈. Taiwan, Hong Kong and Macau still use the traditional forms. This course, like the HSK, uses simplified characters.
:::

:::key
- About 1,000 characters cover around 90% of everyday text.
- A few characters are pictures; some combine two meanings; most (about 80%) pair a meaning part with a sound part.
- Most words have two characters, and their meanings combine: 电 + 脑 = electric brain = computer.
:::
