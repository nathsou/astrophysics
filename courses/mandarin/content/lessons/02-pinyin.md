The capital of China used to be written *Peking* in English. Now it is *Beijing*. The city did not move and its name did not change: the spelling system did. Since 1958 the official way to write Chinese in Latin letters has been **pinyin** (拼音, literally "spell sound"), and every textbook, dictionary, phone keyboard and street sign in mainland China uses it.

Pinyin is excellent, consistent and easy to learn, with one catch: it was designed for Chinese, not for English speakers. About a dozen letters stand for sounds you would never guess. *Q* is a kind of *ch*. *X* is a kind of *sh*. *C* is *ts*. Learn those now, once, and you will never mispronounce a name in the news again.

## Anatomy of a syllable

Every Mandarin syllable has the same simple shape:

| Part | What it is | In 好 *hǎo* |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Initial** | the consonant at the start (sometimes none) | h |
| **Final** | everything after it | ao |
| **Tone** | the pitch shape, marked on the final | 3rd (the dip) |

There are 21 initials and about 36 finals, and not every combination exists. All told, Mandarin has only about **400 syllables**, about 1,300 with tones. English has thousands. This is why Chinese has so many words that sound alike, and why characters, which tell them apart on the page, have survived so long.

Most initials are close enough to English: m, f, n, l, s and h (which is a little raspier, like the *ch* in Scottish *loch*). The rest of this lesson is about the ones that are not.

## Puff or no puff: b and p

Hold your hand in front of your mouth and say *pie*. Feel the puff of air? Now say *spy*. The *p* in *spy* has almost no puff.

Mandarin cares about that puff more than English does. The pairs below differ **only** in the puff of air, not in whether your voice is on:

| No puff | Puff | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| b, like the *p* in *spy* | p, like *p* in *pie*, with a strong puff | 八 bā / 趴 pā |
| d, like the *t* in *sty* | t, a strong *t* | 大 dà / 他 tā |
| g, like the *k* in *sky* | k, a strong *k* | 哥 gē / 可 kě |

So Mandarin *b* sounds a little like English *b* and a little like *p*. Do not worry about it; just keep the puff for p, t and k, and make it generous.

## Three families of hissing sounds

This is the one genuinely new thing in pinyin. Mandarin has three sets of sounds where English has roughly one. They differ in **where your tongue is**.

**1. z, c, s: tongue tip behind the top teeth.**
- **z** is the *ds* in *kids*. (Not an English *z*.)
- **c** is the *ts* in *cats*, with a puff. (Never a *k* or an *s*.)
- **s** is an ordinary *s*.

**2. zh, ch, sh, r: tongue tip curled up and back,** touching the roof of your mouth behind the ridge. This gives a deeper, "hollow" sound.
- **zh** is like the *j* in *judge*, said with the tongue curled back.
- **ch** is like *ch* in *church*, curled back, with a puff.
- **sh** is like *sh* in *shirt*, curled back.
- **r** is somewhere between the *s* in *pleasure* and an English *r*.

**3. j, q, x: tongue tip down behind your lower teeth,** the middle of your tongue pressed up, lips spread as if smiling.
- **j** is like the *j* in *jeep*, but with a smile.
- **q** is like the *ch* in *cheap*, smiling, with a puff. Yes, *q*.
- **x** is like the *sh* in *sheep*, smiling. Yes, *x*.

:::tip
A neat trick: j, q and x are only ever followed by *i* or *ü*. So whenever you see one, smile. Zh, ch and sh are the opposite: tongue back, lips relaxed.
:::

Listen to the three families side by side:

```dialogue

zh ch sh: 知 · 吃 · 十 | zhī · chī · shí (know · eat · ten)
j q x: 鸡 · 七 · 西 | jī · qī · xī (chicken · seven · west)
```

:::fun
Ten is 十 shí and four is 四 sì. Even native speakers from southern China, whose dialects do not have the curled-back sounds, mix them up. There is a tongue twister about it: 四是四，十是十 "four is four, ten is ten".
:::

## Vowels with a twist

The finals are mostly what they look like, with a few surprises. These are the ones to learn:

| Spelling | Sounds like | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **e** | the *u* in *duh*, said from the back of the throat | 喝 hē (drink) |
| **ü** | say *ee* while rounding your lips as for *oo* (French *u*, German *ü*) | 女 nǚ (woman) |
| **i** after z c s zh ch sh r | not *ee* at all: just hold the consonant and buzz | 是 shì, 四 sì, 吃 chī |
| **ian** | *yen*, not *yan* | 天 tiān (day) |
| **ui** | *way* | 对 duì (correct) |
| **iu** | *yo* | 六 liù (six) |
| **un** | *won* (after j q x: *ün*) | 春 chūn (spring) |
| **ong** | the *ung* in German *jung*, rounded | 中 zhōng (middle) |
| **ou** | *oh* | 走 zǒu (walk) |
| **ao** | *ow* in *cow* | 好 hǎo (good) |

One sneaky rule: after **j, q, x** and **y**, the letter *u* is really **ü** and the dots are dropped, because the plain *u* can never follow them. So 去 qù is said *qǜ*, and 鱼 yú is *yǘ*.

```choose
title: Vowel check
items:
  - prompt: How does the **i** in 是 *shì* sound?
    options: [Like the ee in "see", Like the i in "sit", A buzz on the sh with no real vowel]
    answer: 2
  - prompt: How do you say *tiān*?
    options: [tee-ahn, tee-yen, tie-an]
    answer: 1
  - prompt: In 去 *qù*, the u is really…
    options: [u as in "food", ü (ee with rounded lips), u as in "cup"]
    answer: 1
  - prompt: Which word did you hear?
    audio: 女
    listen: true
    options: [nǚ, nǔ]
    answer: 0
    explain: 女 nǚ, "woman", with ü. 努 nǔ (effort) has a plain u.
```

## Spelling rules: y, w and the apostrophe

Syllables without an initial get a *y* or *w* in front, only for spelling:

- *i* becomes **yi** (一 yī), *ia* becomes **ya**, *ie* becomes **ye**, and so on.
- *u* becomes **wu** (五 wǔ), *uo* becomes **wo** (我 wǒ), *ui* becomes **wei**.
- *ü* becomes **yu** (鱼 yú).

When a syllable starting with a vowel would be ambiguous, an apostrophe separates it. The city of 西安 is *Xī'ān* (two syllables: *xī* and *ān*), not *xiān* (one syllable, like 先 "first").

:::culture
Pinyin spellings of names go straight into English, so now you can say them. The president: Xi Jinping (*shee jin-ping*). The first emperor: Qin Shi Huang (*chin shr hwahng*). Tsinghua University is the old spelling of 清华 Qīnghuá; Peking University kept its old name in English. And the dumpling chain Din Tai Fung uses an older system still.
:::

## Typing pinyin

You will type a lot of pinyin in this course. The convention is the **tone number** after each syllable: `ni3 hao3`. Neutral tones get 5 (or nothing), and **v** stands in for ü: `nv3` is nǚ.

```pinyin
title: Hear it, type it
listen: true
items:
  - zh: 谢谢
    en: thank you
  - zh: 中国
    en: China
  - zh: 再见
    en: goodbye
  - zh: 北京
    en: Beijing
  - zh: 吃
    en: to eat
  - zh: 七
    en: seven
  - zh: 女
    en: woman
```

```words
谢谢 | thank you
再见 | goodbye
中国 | China
北京 | Beijing
吃 | to eat
喝 | to drink
七 | seven
十 | ten
四 | four
女 | woman
```

```match
title: Sounds to spellings

  - [吃, chī]
  - [七, qī]
  - [十, shí]
  - [四, sì]
  - [喝, hē]
  - [女, nǚ]
```

:::key
- A syllable is an initial, a final and a tone; Mandarin has only about 400 syllables.
- b/d/g have no puff of air; p/t/k have a strong one.
- z c s: tongue behind the teeth (z = *ds*, c = *ts*). zh ch sh r: tongue curled back. j q x: tongue down, smiling (q ≈ *ch*, x ≈ *sh*).
- After z c s zh ch sh r, *i* is a buzz, not *ee*. After j q x y, *u* is really *ü*.
:::
