Say *ma*. Now say it the way you would if someone told you something surprising: *ma?* Now as if you were scolding a dog: *ma!*

In English those are three moods of the same syllable. In Mandarin they are three different words, and there is a fourth one too. Here is the sentence every learner meets in their first week:

> 妈妈骂马吗？ *Is Mum scolding the horse?*

Five syllables, four of them some kind of *ma*, and a native speaker hears no ambiguity at all. By the end of this lesson, neither will you. Tap any Chinese in this course to hear it and see what it means.

## Pitch carries meaning

Every language uses pitch, the musical height of your voice. English uses it for *attitude*: a rise for a question, a fall for finality, a wobble for sarcasm. Mandarin uses it for *identity*. Each syllable comes with its own pitch shape, called a **tone**, and changing the shape changes the word.

There are four tones, plus a light, short "neutral" one we will meet later. The linguist Yuen Ren Chao drew them on a five-level scale, 1 at the bottom of your comfortable range and 5 at the top:

::tone-contours

Give each one a few listens, then read on.

1. **First tone, high and level (55).** Sing a note near the top of your speaking range and hold it. No movement. Think of a doctor asking you to say "aaah".
2. **Second tone, rising (35).** Start mid-range and climb, like a surprised "What?" or the end of "Really?".
3. **Third tone, low and dipping (214).** Drop low, lower than feels polite, then bounce back up. In fluent speech the bounce often disappears and only the low part is left. That low part is what matters.
4. **Fourth tone, falling (51).** Start high and drop sharply, like a firm "No!" or "Stop!". It is short and decisive.

:::tip
Exaggerate. Learners almost always make their tones too flat, because English speakers are trained to keep pitch movements small and polite. Your first tones should feel too high, your third tones too low, and your fourth tones almost rude. You can soften them later; nobody ever learned clear tones by starting timid.
:::

## Train your ear

The fastest way to hear tones is a short, focused game. You hear a syllable; you say which tone it was. Missed ones come back later in the round, and the tones you confuse most get drawn more often next time.

::tone-trainer{count=12}

Most people find pairs that blur together. The classic ones:

| Often confused | Why | What to listen for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2nd and 3rd | Both end higher than they start | The 3rd tone goes *down* first, and sits much lower |
| 1st and 4th | Both start high | The 1st stays put; the 4th collapses |
| 4th and the end of a question | English questions rise, so we hear statements as flat | Ignore the sentence; follow the syllable |

:::fun
Tones are not unusual. More than half of the world's languages are tonal, from Thai and Vietnamese to Yoruba, Zulu and Navajo. If anything, English is the odd one out.
:::

## Your voice, your range

Tones are not fixed notes. A child's first tone is far higher than a bass singer's, and both are correct, because tones are measured *against your own range*. "High" means high for you.

That is what the **tone mirror** shows. Record yourself and your pitch is drawn over the target shape. Your recording never leaves your device: the analysis happens in your browser. The mirror also learns where your voice usually sits, so it gets better at judging the low third tone as you use it.

::tone-mirror

A few tips that make the mirror (and real listeners) happier:

- Say each syllable on its own, slowly, a little longer than feels natural.
- For the third tone, go *low*. Think "growl", not "sing".
- If the mirror hears a first tone when you meant a second, start lower and rise further.

## Writing tones down

Chinese characters do not show tones at all; you just have to know them. Learners use **pinyin**, the official spelling of Chinese in Latin letters, which puts a little mark on each syllable that looks like the tone's shape:

| Tone | Mark | Example | With a number |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | ā (flat line) | mā | ma1 |
| 2nd | á (rising) | má | ma2 |
| 3rd | ǎ (dip) | mǎ | ma3 |
| 4th | à (falling) | mà | ma4 |
| neutral | a (no mark) | ma | ma5 or ma0 |

When typing, you can write the number after the syllable instead of the mark: `ma3` becomes mǎ. Every pinyin exercise in this course accepts both.

The mark goes on a vowel. When there are several, it goes on **a** or **e** if there is one (*hǎo*, *méi*), on the **o** of **ou** (*gǒu*), and otherwise on the last vowel (*guì*, *liú*). Lesson 2 has more on spelling.

## Your first words

You now have everything needed to pronounce real Chinese words. Here are four you will use every day:

```words
妈妈 | mum
爸爸 | dad
马 | horse
你好 | hello
```

Notice two things. First, the second 妈 in 妈妈 has **no tone mark**: it is a **neutral tone**, short and light, as if the word runs out of energy. Many doubled words work like this, including 爸爸 and 谢谢 (thanks).

Second, 你好 is written *nǐ hǎo*, two third tones, but nobody says it that way. Try saying two deep dips in a row and you will feel why. Lesson 3 explains what actually happens.

```tones
title: Tone detective
items:
  - zh: 妈妈
    en: mum
  - zh: 爸爸
    en: dad
  - zh: 马
    en: horse
  - zh: 好
    en: good
  - zh: 是
    en: to be
  - zh: 八
    en: eight
```

Now go back to the sentence from the start. Read it with the pinyin, and listen to how each tone does its job:

> 妈妈骂马吗？ *Is Mum scolding the horse?*

The last syllable, 吗, is the neutral-tone *ma*, the one that turns a statement into a yes-or-no question. So the sentence contains the first tone, a neutral tone, the fourth, the third, and another neutral. A whole tone lesson in five syllables.

:::key
- A tone is a pitch shape, and it is part of the word: change the shape, change the meaning.
- There are four tones and a neutral one: high level, rising, low dipping, falling, and short and light.
- Tones are relative to your own voice. Exaggerate them while learning.
- Pinyin marks the tones with marks that look like their shapes.
:::
