<Added label="Added for this edition">

A variable can occur in a formula in two ways. An occurrence is **bound** if it lies in the scope of a quantifier for that same variable, and **free** otherwise (<Ref k="fol:syn:fvs:defn:free-occ" />). A formula without free occurrences is a **sentence**. Sentences are the formulas that are simply true or false in a structure; formulas with free variables are true or false only relative to values for those variables.

</Added>

## Occurrences, not variables

Freedom is a property of *occurrences*. In $x = 0 \land \forall x\, x = x$ the first $x$ is free and the others are bound: the same variable can be both free and bound in one formula. The variable written right after a quantifier is not counted by the book’s definition: it just says which variable the quantifier binds.

## Scope

If $\forall x\, B$ is a subformula of $A$, then $B$ is the **scope** of that occurrence of $\forall x$. The free occurrences of $x$ in $B$ are bound by it — but not occurrences that are already bound by a quantifier inside $B$. In $\forall x\,(R(x, y) \rightarrow \exists y\, R(y, z))$, the $y$ in $R(x, y)$ is free, while the $y$ in $R(y, z)$ is bound by $\exists y$. Each bound occurrence is bound by exactly one quantifier: the innermost one around it for that variable.

## Why it matters

Free variables are the “inputs” of a formula: satisfaction depends on what the assignment gives them and on nothing else (<Ref k="fol:syn:ass:prop:satindep" />). They are also what substitution replaces. In the incompleteness chapters, a formula with one free variable $x$ expresses a *property of numbers*, and substituting a numeral for $x$ produces a sentence about a particular number.
