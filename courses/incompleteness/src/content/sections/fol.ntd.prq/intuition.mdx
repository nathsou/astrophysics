<Added label="Added for this edition">

With quantifiers, the order of inferences matters. $\forall$Intro and $\exists$Elim carry the eigenvariable condition, so the book's advice is to "take care of rules subject to the eigenvariable condition first": working backwards, they come early, which puts them *low* in the finished tree, where fewer assumptions are still open.

</Added>

## Choosing the eigenvariable

For $\exists x\, \lnot A(x) \rightarrow \lnot\forall x\, A(x)$, after $\rightarrow$Intro the book sets up $\exists$Elim with the assumption $[\lnot A(a)]^2$, choosing "a constant that does not appear in $\exists x\, \lnot A(x)$ or any assumptions that it depends on". Then $\forall$Elim — which has no condition — may use the *same* $a$: from $[\forall x\, A(x)]^3$ infer $A(a)$, and $\lnot A(a)$ with $A(a)$ gives $\bot$.

The final check the book recommends — "double check at this point that the eigenvariable condition has not been violated" — is what the checker does at every $\forall$Intro and $\exists$Elim. For $\forall$Intro, $a$ must not occur in $\forall x\, A(x)$ or in any assumption undischarged above it; for $\exists$Elim, not in $\exists x\, A(x)$, in $C$, or in any undischarged assumption other than the $A(a)$ being discharged.

## Undischarged assumptions

The second and third examples derive a sentence from assumptions that stay undischarged: $\exists x\,(A(x) \land B(x))$ and $\forall x\,(B(x) \rightarrow C(x, b))$ give $\exists x\, C(x, b)$. Here $b$ is a constant that occurs in an assumption, so it could *not* serve as the eigenvariable of the $\exists$Elim; the book uses $a$.
