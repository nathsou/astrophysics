The arithmetization of syntax turned properties of formulas into computable functions and relations on numbers. This chapter closes the loop: every computable function can be **represented** in a very weak theory of arithmetic, $\mathbf Q$. So $\mathbf Q$ — and any theory that extends it — can *prove* the individual facts about any computation.

</Added>

## A weak theory

$\mathbf Q$ has eight axioms: successor is injective and never $0$, every number other than $0$ is a successor, the recursion equations for $+$ and $\times$, and a definition of $<$. It has no induction. It cannot even prove $\forall x\, \forall y\, (x + y) = (y + x)$. What it can do is calculate: for any particular numbers it proves $\overline 2 + \overline 3 = \overline 5$, and $\overline 2 \neq \overline 3$.

## What representing means

A formula $A_f(x_0, \ldots, x_k, y)$ represents $f$ if, whenever $f(n_0, \ldots, n_k) = m$, $\mathbf Q$ proves

- (a) $A_f(\overline{n_0}, \ldots, \overline{n_k}, \overline m)$ — the formula holds of the right value, and
- (b) $\forall y\,(A_f(\overline{n_0}, \ldots, \overline{n_k}, y) \rightarrow y = \overline m)$ — and of no other.

So inside $\mathbf Q$, the formula behaves exactly like the graph of $f$ — one input at a time. Nothing is claimed about $\mathbf Q$ proving general laws about $f$.

## The strategy

The book shows every computable function is representable by building formulas for a small set of basic functions (zero, successor, projections, addition, multiplication, $\chi_=$) and showing composition and minimization preserve representability. Primitive recursion is avoided: the β-function shows it can be replaced by minimization.

In Explore mode you build a function from those pieces, compute it, and see its representing formula grow part by part — and the derivations in $\mathbf Q$ of both clauses for your input, generated from the computation and checked step by step.
