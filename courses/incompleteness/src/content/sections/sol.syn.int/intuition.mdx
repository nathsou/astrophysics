First-order logic quantifies over the objects of the domain. **Second-order logic** also quantifies over relations and functions on the domain. Alongside $\forall x$ it has $\forall X$, where $X$ is a relation variable ranging over all subsets of $|\mathfrak M|$ (or all $n$-place relations), and $\forall u$, where $u$ is a function variable ranging over all functions on $|\mathfrak M|$.

</Added>

## The same structures, more to say

The structures are the same as in first-order logic. What changes is the language and what satisfaction does with it. In the **standard semantics** used throughout this chapter, $\forall X$ ranges over *every* subset of the domain — not just the ones some formula happens to define.

The book’s first example: $\forall X\, \forall x\,(X(x) \lor \lnot X(x))$ and $\exists X\, \forall x\,(X(x) \lor \lnot X(x))$ are sentences of second-order logic, and both are true in every structure — every set either contains a given object or not.

## What it buys

With quantifiers over sets and functions, second-order logic can say things no first-order sentence can: that the domain is infinite, that it is countable, that one relation is the transitive closure of another, and — crucially for arithmetic — the induction axiom as a single sentence, which pins down $\mathfrak N$ up to isomorphism (section 8.7). It can express a good deal of set theory without a membership symbol (sections 8.11–8.13).

## What it costs

That power has a price, shown in sections 8.8–8.10: second-order logic has no sound and complete proof system, it is not compact, and the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem fails. The categorical characterisation of $\mathfrak N$ does not escape incompleteness; it relocates it into the logic.

<Callout title="Explore">
On a finite domain, $\forall X$ has finitely many values — $2^n$ subsets of an $n$-element domain — and $\forall u$ has $n^n$. The workbench evaluates second-order sentences by running through all of them, which is only feasible for tiny domains.
</Callout>
