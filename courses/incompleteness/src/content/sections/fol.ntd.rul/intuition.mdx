<Added label="Added for this edition">

A derivation is a tree. Its topmost sentences are **assumptions**; every other sentence stands below one, two or three sentences — its **premises** — and follows from them by a rule of inference. Some rules may **discharge** assumptions, and a shared **label** records which.

</Added>

## Assumptions

The book's definition is purely positional: an assumption is any sentence in the topmost position of a branch. Nothing is said about *which* sentences may be assumed — any sentence may. What matters is whether the assumption is still **undischarged** at the end: the undischarged assumptions are what the conclusion depends on.

## Inferences, premises, conclusions

An inference has one, two or three premises above a line and one conclusion below it. The rules come in pairs: an **introduction** rule puts a connective or quantifier into the conclusion ($A$ and $B$ give $A \land B$), an **elimination** rule takes one out of a premise ($A \land B$ gives $A$). There is also =Intro, which has no premises at all: $t = t$ follows outright.

## Labels

Four propositional rules and one quantifier rule may discharge assumptions: $\lnot$Intro, $\rightarrow$Intro, $\lor$Elim, $\bot_C$ and $\exists$Elim. The discharged assumption is written $[A]^n$ and the discharging inference carries the same number $n$. The label is bookkeeping — it says *which* assumptions a given inference uses up — so a derivation with several assumptions of the same sentence can discharge some of them and not others.

Labels are notation, not part of the definition of a derivation (<Ref k="fol:ntd:der:sec" />): a derivation is a tree of sentences, and the labels only make visible which inference discharges which assumption. An inference that discharges nothing needs no label, though the book sometimes writes one. This edition's checker adds one convention of its own: an assumption *with* a label that no inference below discharges is reported as an error, since its label promises a discharge that never happens.
