In the pure calculus, a λ-term is a variable, an application $(MN)$, or an abstraction $(\lambda x.\,M)$. Everything else in this section is about reading terms: which parentheses may be dropped, which λ an occurrence of a variable belongs to, and when two terms count as the same.

## The clauses

The book's definition has four clauses: variables, constants $a, b, c, \dots$, applications $(MN)$ and abstractions $(\lambda x.\,M)$. The calculus without constants is the **pure** calculus; the rest of the chapter uses it, so there lowercase letters are variables and uppercase letters stand for terms. Officially every application and every abstraction carries its own parentheses. Nothing else is a term: there are no numbers and no operations like $+$.

## Leaving out parentheses

The book’s conventions:

- **Application associates to the left.** $MNPQ$ is $(((MN)P)Q)$: first $M$ is applied to $N$, and the result to $P$, and so on.
- **λ reaches as far to the right as possible.** $\lambda x.\,MNP$ is $\lambda x.\,((MN)P)$, not $((\lambda x.\,M)N)P$.
- **One λ for several variables.** $\lambda xyz.\,M$ is $\lambda x.\lambda y.\lambda z.\,M$.

So $\lambda xy.\,xxyx\,\lambda z.\,xz$ is $\lambda x.\,(\lambda y.\,((((xx)y)x)(\lambda z.\,(xz))))$. Explore mode shows both forms of any term you type. (In this edition’s input, variables may have several letters, so separate them with spaces — `λx y. x x y x λz. x z` — or tick “single-letter variables” to type the book’s notation.)

## Free and bound

In $\lambda x.\,M$, the λ **binds** the free occurrences of $x$ in $M$; $M$ is its scope. An occurrence not bound by any λ is **free**. In $(\lambda z.\,yz)x$, $z$ is bound and $y$, $x$ are free. The same letter can occur both ways: in $x\,(\lambda x.\,x)$ the first $x$ is free and the second is bound.

## α-equivalence

Terms that differ only in the names of bound variables are **α-equivalent**, and the book treats them as the same term: $\lambda x.\,x$ and $\lambda y.\,y$ are both the identity function.

Renaming is not arbitrary, though. In $\lambda x.\lambda y.\,x$ (“return the first argument”) the outer $x$ cannot be renamed to $y$: that gives $\lambda y.\lambda y.\,y$, which returns the *second* argument, because the inner λy now captures the occurrence. A new name must not occur free in the scope, and must not be bound by an inner λ around an occurrence being renamed. The Explore mode’s renaming tool checks exactly these two conditions.
