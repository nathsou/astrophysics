<Added label="Added for this edition">

Apply the fixed-point lemma to $\lnot\mathsf{Prov}_T(x)$, where $\mathsf{Prov}_T(y)$ says “some number codes a $T$-derivation of the sentence with Gödel number $y$”. The result is a sentence $G_T$ with $\mathbf Q \vdash G_T \leftrightarrow \lnot\mathsf{Prov}_T(\ulcorner G_T\urcorner)$: $G_T$ says of itself that it is not provable in $T$.

</Added>

## Two halves, two hypotheses

- **If $T$ proved $G_T$**, a particular number $m$ would code the derivation, and — because the proof relation is represented in $\mathbf Q$ — $\mathbf Q$ would prove $\mathsf{Prf}_T(\overline m, \ulcorner G_T \urcorner)$, hence $\mathsf{Prov}_T(\ulcorner G_T\urcorner)$, hence $\lnot G_T$. So $T$ would be inconsistent. **Consistency** rules this out.
- **If $T$ proved $\lnot G_T$**, it would prove $\exists x\,\mathsf{Prf}_T(x, \ulcorner G_T\urcorner)$. But since $T$ does not prove $G_T$, no number codes a derivation, and $\mathbf Q$ refutes $\mathsf{Prf}_T(\overline n, \ulcorner G_T\urcorner)$ for each $n$ separately. That is not a contradiction — only an **ω-inconsistency**. Gödel needed ω-consistency here; Rosser later found a sentence for which consistency suffices.

The other hypotheses enter elsewhere: **computable axiomatization** makes the proof relation decidable, hence representable; **extending Q** transfers what $\mathbf Q$ proves to $T$.

Explore mode lays the two lemmas out step by step, with what justifies each step. Switch a hypothesis off and watch which steps — and which conclusions — lose their support.
