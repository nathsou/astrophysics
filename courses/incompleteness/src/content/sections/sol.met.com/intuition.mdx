A set of sentences is **finitely satisfiable** if each of its finite subsets is satisfiable. First-order logic is **compact**: finitely satisfiable sets are satisfiable. It is what produced non-standard models of arithmetic in <Ref k="mod:mar:nst:sec" />. Second-order logic is not compact (<Ref k="sol:met:com:thm:sol-undecidable" />).

</Added>

## The counterexample

Let $A^{\ge n}$ be the first-order sentence “there are at least $n$ elements”: $\exists x_1 \ldots \exists x_n$ with all $x_i$ pairwise distinct. Take

$$\Gamma = \{\lnot\mathrm{Inf}, A^{\ge 1}, A^{\ge 2}, A^{\ge 3}, \ldots\}.$$

- **Finitely satisfiable.** A finite $\Gamma_0 \subseteq \Gamma$ contains $A^{\ge n}$ only for $n$ up to some $k$. A domain with exactly $k$ elements satisfies all of them, and $\lnot\mathrm{Inf}$ because it is finite.
- **Not satisfiable.** A model of $\lnot \mathrm{Inf}$ is finite, of some size $k$, and then $A^{\ge k+1}$ is false.

## The entailment version

Equivalently: there are $\Gamma$ and $A$ with $\Gamma \vDash A$ but no finite $\Gamma_0 \subseteq \Gamma$ with $\Gamma_0 \vDash A$ (the book asks for an example). In first-order logic this cannot happen: by the completeness theorem $\Gamma \vDash A$ gives a derivation, and a derivation uses only finitely many premises. Non-compactness is another way of seeing that second-order logic has no sound and complete derivation system.
