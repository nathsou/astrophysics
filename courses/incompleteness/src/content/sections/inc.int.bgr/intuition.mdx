| 1904 | Ernst Zermelo | Cantor’s well-ordering principle proved from the axiom of choice, to much debate |
| 1900s–1910s | Henri Poincaré, L. E. J. Brouwer | criticism of impredicative and non-constructive reasoning; intuitionism |
| 1910–1913 | Whitehead and Russell | *Principia Mathematica*, with an axiom of infinity and an axiom of reducibility |
| 1921 | Hilbert (with Bernays, Ackermann, later Gentzen) | a program: prove, by finitary means, that formal systems for mathematics are consistent |
| 1931 | Kurt Gödel | the two incompleteness theorems |

## The question behind the chapter

By the 1920s there was a precise version of Hilbert’s hope: a formal theory (such as the one of *Principia*, or first-order arithmetic) that is **complete**, **decidable**, and provably **consistent** by elementary means. The rest of this book makes each of these words precise — <Ref k="inc:int:def:sec" /> defines them — and shows that the hope cannot be fulfilled.
