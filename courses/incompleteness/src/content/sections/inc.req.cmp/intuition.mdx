If $A_g(x, y)$ represents $g$ and $A_f(y, z)$ represents $f$, then “there is a $y$ with $A_g(x, y)$ and $A_f(y, z)$” should represent $h(x) = f(g(x))$. It does — and the proof shows why the *existential* quantifier is the right one.

## Where the witness comes from

To derive clause (a) for $h$ at $n$, $\mathbf Q$ needs to prove $\exists y\,(A_g(\overline n, y) \land A_f(y, \overline m))$. It does so by exhibiting a $y$: the numeral for $g(n)$, the intermediate value of the computation. The derivation is the computation, transcribed.

Clause (b) runs the other way. Suppose $A_g(\overline n, b) \land A_f(b, a)$ for some $b$ and $a$. Clause (b) for $g$ says $b = \overline{g(n)}$; substituting, $A_f(\overline{g(n)}, a)$; clause (b) for $f$ says $a = \overline m$. This is the proof of <Ref k="inc:req:cmp:prop:rep2" />, and Explore mode shows it as a checked derivation for your function, with the $\exists$-eliminations (one for each inner function) and the substitution steps in place.
