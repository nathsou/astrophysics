Logic talks about formulas. Arithmetic talks about numbers. Gödel’s idea was to make the second do the work of the first: to assign every formula a number, so that statements *about* formulas — “this is a formula”, “this is the result of substituting 3 for $x$”, “this sequence of formulas is a derivation” — become statements about numbers. Arithmetic can make statements about numbers. So, indirectly, it can make statements about formulas, including itself.

</Added>

## The plan of the chapter

The construction comes in layers, and each layer is something you can take apart in the workbenches:

1. **Symbols get codes.** Every symbol — logical symbols, variables, constants, function and predicate symbols — gets a number, in such a way that the kind of symbol and its index can be read back from the number (<Ref k="inc:art:cod:sec" />).
2. **Strings get Gödel numbers.** A term or formula is a finite string of symbols; its Gödel number codes the sequence of the symbols’ codes (<Ref k="inc:art:trm:sec" />, <Ref k="inc:art:frm:sec" />).
3. **Properties become computations.** “Is the Gödel number of a formula”, “is a free occurrence of”, “is the result of substituting” all become *primitive recursive* relations and functions on these numbers (<Ref k="inc:art:frm:sec" />, <Ref k="inc:art:sub:sec" />), and so does “this is a derivation of that” (<Ref k="inc:art:pnd:sec" />).

The third layer is the point. Primitive recursive functions are, as <Ref k="inc:req::chap" /> shows, *representable* in the very weak theory $\mathbf Q$. That is what lets arithmetic speak about its own syntax.

## Why numbers, and not strings?

We could study computable functions on strings directly. But the language of arithmetic has no names for strings — only $0$, successor, $+$, $\times$ and $<$. To have a theory of arithmetic like $\mathbf Q$ or $\mathbf{PA}$ reason about formulas, formulas have to be *made into* numbers. Nothing about this is deep in itself; what is striking is how much follows once it is done carefully.
