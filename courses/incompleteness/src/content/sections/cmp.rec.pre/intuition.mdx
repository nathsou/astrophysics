Every natural number is $0$, or the successor of a natural number. So to define $h$ on all of $\mathbb N$, it is enough to say (a) what $h(0)$ is and (b) how to get $h(y + 1)$ from $h(y)$. Two such equations are a **definition by primitive recursion**.

</Added>

## Two equations, one function

The book’s first example is

$$
h(0) = 1, \qquad h(y + 1) = 2 \cdot h(y).
$$

The first equation gives $h(0)$; the second, with $y = 0$, gives $h(1) = 2$; with $y = 1$, $h(2) = 4$; and so on. Since every number is reached from $0$ by adding $1$ finitely often, every $h(y)$ is reached this way — and there is only one function satisfying both equations: $h(y) = 2^y$.

## Parameters

Addition and multiplication have two arguments. Fix one of them, $x$, and recurse on the other:

$$
\mathrm{add}(x, 0) = x, \qquad \mathrm{add}(x, y + 1) = \mathrm{add}(x, y) + 1,
$$
$$
\mathrm{mult}(x, 0) = 0, \qquad \mathrm{mult}(x, y + 1) = \mathrm{add}(\mathrm{mult}(x, y), x).
$$

The parameter $x$ rides along unchanged; the recursion happens in the last argument.

## The general pattern

Name the right-hand sides. A function $h$ is defined by primitive recursion from $f$ and $g$ if

$$
\begin{aligned}
h(x_0, \ldots, x_{k-1}, 0) &= f(x_0, \ldots, x_{k-1}) \\
h(x_0, \ldots, x_{k-1}, y + 1) &= g(x_0, \ldots, x_{k-1}, y, h(x_0, \ldots, x_{k-1}, y)).
\end{aligned}
$$

The step function $g$ may use the parameters, the old argument $y$, and the **previous value** — but only that one previous value. That restriction is what “primitive” means. For $\mathrm{add}$, $f(x_0) = x_0$ and $g(x_0, y, z) = z + 1$; for $\mathrm{mult}$, $f(x_0) = 0$ and $g(x_0, y, z) = \mathrm{add}(z, x_0)$.
