First-order logic has a sound and complete derivation system: the valid sentences are exactly the derivable ones, and so they can be computably enumerated. (The completeness theorem is not proved in this book.) Second-order logic has no such system (<Ref k="sol:met:nax:cor:sol-not-axiomatizable" />).

</Added>

## Undecidable

First, second-order logic is undecidable (<Ref k="sol:met:nax:thm:sol-undecidable" />): a first-order sentence is valid in first-order logic iff it is valid as a second-order sentence, and first-order validity is already undecidable.

## Not axiomatizable

The stronger claim uses the categoricity of $\mathbf{PA}^2$ from <Ref k="sol:met:spa:sec" />. Let $P$ be the conjunction of its nine axioms. Since every model of $\mathbf{PA}^2$ is isomorphic to $\mathfrak N$,

$$\mathfrak N \vDash A \quad\text{iff}\quad \mathbf{PA}^2 \vDash A \quad\text{iff}\quad \vDash P \rightarrow A.$$

Now replace $0$ by an object variable $z$, $'$ by a function variable $u$, $+$ and $\times$ by two-place function variables, $<$ by a relation variable $L$, and quantify all of them universally. The result is a sentence of **pure** second-order logic, valid iff $P \rightarrow A$ is valid, iff $A$ is true in $\mathfrak N$.

So a sound and complete derivation system for second-order logic would give a computable enumeration of the sentences true in $\mathfrak N$. The book finishes with Tarski’s theorem (<Ref k="inc:inp:tar:thm:tarski" />): the enumeration would be representable in $\mathbf Q$, and then truth in $\mathfrak N$ would be definable in arithmetic, which it is not.

## What this means

The incompleteness phenomenon has not been escaped. First-order $\mathbf{PA}$ is incomplete, and its models include non-standard ones. $\mathbf{PA}^2$ has only standard models — but its consequence relation cannot be captured by any derivation system. The price of categoricity is paid in the logic.
