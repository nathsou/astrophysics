A set $S$ of numbers is computable — also called *decidable* — when its characteristic function $\chi_S$ (1 on members, 0 on non-members) is computable. Since “computable” means *total* computable, a decision procedure must answer yes or no for every input, and must never run forever.

</Added>

## Three notions, kept apart

The chapter now has three kinds of computability, and the book warns not to confuse them:

- a **partial computable function** gives a value on the inputs where it is defined and runs forever on the others;
- a **computable function** is a partial computable function that happens to be defined everywhere;
- a **computable set** is one whose characteristic function is a computable function: the procedure always stops, with answer $1$ or $0$.

Being defined is not the same as answering “yes”. A procedure that halts on members of $S$ and runs forever on non-members is *not* a decision procedure for $S$ — you never know when to give up waiting. Such procedures define the next section’s notion, computably enumerable sets.

## Relations

The same goes for relations $R(x_0, \ldots, x_{k-1})$: computable when $\chi_R$ is. The relation $T(e, x, s)$ of the normal form theorem is computable (even primitive recursive). The relation “$\varphi_e(x)$ is defined” — the halting relation — is not.
