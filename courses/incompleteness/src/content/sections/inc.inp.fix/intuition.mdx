The fixed-point lemma produces, for any property $B(x)$ of numbers, a sentence $A$ that $\mathbf Q$ proves equivalent to “$B$ holds of my own Gödel number”. Take $B$ to be “is not provable” and $A$ says of itself that it is not provable: Gödel’s sentence. Take $B$ to be the negation of a supposed truth predicate and $A$ becomes a liar sentence — so arithmetic cannot define its own truth (Tarski, <Ref k="inc:inp:tar:sec" />).

## Self-reference without “this sentence”

The language of arithmetic has no word for “this sentence”. Quine showed how to get self-reference anyway, with quotation. Take an expression with a gap for its subject, and precede it by its own quotation:

<QuineDiagonal />

In arithmetic, *quotation* is the numeral of a Gödel number, and *preceding by the quotation* is substituting that numeral for the free variable. The function doing this to Gödel numbers is $\mathrm{diag}$: it sends $\#E(x)\#$ to $\#E(\ulcorner E(x)\urcorner)\#$. It is primitive recursive — it is just substitution — so it is represented in $\mathbf Q$ by some formula $D_{\mathrm{diag}}(x, y)$.

## The construction

$$
E(x) \;=\; \exists y\,\bigl(D_{\mathrm{diag}}(x, y) \land B(y)\bigr), \qquad A \;=\; E(\ulcorner E(x)\urcorner).
$$

$E(x)$ says: “$B$ holds of the diagonalization of $x$” (the book writes it as $B(\mathsf{diag}(x))$ first, then removes the function symbol, which $\mathbf Q$ does not have, in favor of $D_{\mathrm{diag}}$). Its diagonalization $A$ therefore says: “$B$ holds of the diagonalization of $E(x)$” — and that diagonalization is $A$ itself.
