This chapter turns the tools of the previous two — Gödel numbering and representability in $\mathbf Q$ — into the incompleteness theorems. Its engine is one lemma: for any property $B(x)$ there is a sentence $A$ that the theory knows is equivalent to “$B$ holds of my own Gödel number”.

## Two theorems against Hilbert’s hopes

Hilbert wanted formal theories of arithmetic to be **complete** (every sentence or its negation derivable) and wanted **finitary consistency proofs** for them. The chapter proves that neither is available for any consistent, axiomatizable theory that is strong enough:

- the **first incompleteness theorem**: such a theory derives neither $G_T$ nor $\lnot G_T$ for a suitable sentence $G_T$ (for the second half Gödel needed ω-consistency; Rosser, with a modified sentence, needed only consistency);
- the **second incompleteness theorem**: such a theory, if it also satisfies the derivability conditions, does not derive its own consistency statement.

## Self-reference without “this sentence”

The liar sentence says “this sentence is false”. A formula cannot literally point at itself, but it can mention a *name* of a formula: the numeral $\ulcorner A\urcorner$ of its Gödel number. Keep three things apart, as the book does:

| | what it is | example |
|---|---|---|
| $A$ | a formula | <F f="∀x x = x" /> |
| $\#A\#$ | a natural number | its Gödel number |
| $\ulcorner A \urcorner$ | a term (a numeral) | $0'''\cdots'$ with $\#A\#$ primes |

Quine’s version of the paradox shows how a sentence can talk about itself by talking about a *description* of itself: “yields falsehood when preceded by its quotation” yields falsehood when preceded by its quotation. The fixed-point lemma formalizes exactly this trick, with “preceded by its quotation” becoming the diagonal function $\mathrm{diag}$ on Gödel numbers. You can try the trick on phrases of your own in <a href="#/s/inc.inp.fix?mode=intuition">the next section</a>.
