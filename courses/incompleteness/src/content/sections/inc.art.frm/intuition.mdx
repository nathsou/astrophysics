Once terms can be recognised by a primitive recursive computation, formulas follow the same pattern: atomic formulas are predicate symbols applied to terms, and every other formula is built from earlier ones by a connective or a quantifier. So $\mathrm{Frm}(x)$ — “$x$ is the Gödel number of a formula” — is primitive recursive too.

</Added>

## Encoding and decoding are both computations

Going from a formula to its number is mechanical: write the formula in official notation, look up each symbol’s code, and multiply the prime powers. Going back is also mechanical: factor the number, check that the primes $2, 3, 5, \ldots$ appear without gaps, subtract one from each exponent, look each result up as a symbol code, and parse the symbols. Each step can fail, and each failure means the number is not the Gödel number of a formula. That is the content of <Ref k="inc:art:frm:prop:frm-primrec" />, and the Explore workbench lets you run both directions.

## Official notation

The book writes $x = 0$ and $(x + y)$, but these are abbreviations. The official formula is $=(v_0, c_0)$ and the official term is $f^2_0(v_0, v_1)$, prefix notation with parentheses and commas. The Gödel number is computed from the official string; so is every question about positions, such as “is the $i$-th symbol a free occurrence of $x$?” (<Ref k="inc:art:frm:prop:freeocc-primrec" />).

One more thing is expanded before coding: in this book $\leftrightarrow$ and $\top$ are *defined* symbols, so $A \leftrightarrow B$ is coded as $(A \rightarrow B) \land (B \rightarrow A)$. Try <Try formula="x < y ↔ ∃z (z′ + x) = y">a biconditional</Try> in Explore mode.
