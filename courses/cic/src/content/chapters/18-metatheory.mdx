Why should anyone believe that CIC is consistent — that there is no proof of `False`? Throughout the course we have met properties that "hold for this calculus" and extensions that "break it". This chapter collects them: the **metatheory** of CIC, the theorems *about* the system that justify trusting theorems proved *in* it.

## The properties we want

<Definition title="Key metatheoretic properties">
- **Subject reduction**: reduction preserves types. If $\Gamma \vdash t : A$ and $t \longrightarrow t'$ then $\Gamma \vdash t' : A$.
- **Confluence**: if $t \twoheadrightarrow t_1$ and $t \twoheadrightarrow t_2$ then $t_1$ and $t_2$ reduce to a common term.
- **Strong normalisation**: every reduction sequence from a well-typed term is finite.
- **Consistency**: there is no closed term of type `False`.
- **Decidability of type checking**: there is an algorithm deciding $\Gamma \vdash t : A$.
- **Canonicity**: every closed term of an inductive type (without axioms) reduces to a constructor application — every closed `Nat` is a numeral.
</Definition>

They depend on each other in a precise way:

- **Normalisation ⇒ consistency.** A closed proof of `False` would normalise, by subject reduction, to a closed *normal* proof of `False`. A closed normal term of an inductive type must be a constructor application (canonicity of normal forms) — and `False` has no constructors.
- **Normalisation + confluence ⇒ decidable conversion.** To decide $A \equiv B$ (for β, δ, ι, ζ), normalise both and compare. (η and proof irrelevance need a slightly more careful algorithm, but the idea is the same.)
- **Decidable conversion ⇒ decidable type checking**, because the typing rules are syntax-directed except for conversion.

So everything rests on strong normalisation. That is why every chapter asked "does this still terminate?", and why the kernel's restrictions are exactly those needed to keep it true.

## How consistency is proved

A consistency proof for CIC must use *stronger* assumptions than CIC itself: Gödel's second incompleteness theorem forbids a sufficiently strong consistent system from proving its own consistency. Two approaches are standard.

**Normalisation proofs.** Extend Tait's reducibility method (Chapter 3) and Girard's reducibility candidates (Chapter 5) to the whole of CIC: interpret each type as a set of strongly normalising terms closed under certain operations, show every typed term lies in the interpretation of its type. For CIC this is a formidable undertaking, because types can be computed by large eliminations, and the interpretation of a universe must contain the interpretations of all smaller types. Benjamin Werner's 1994 thesis gave such a proof for CIC with inductive types; Bruno Barras formalised a large part of it in Coq.

**Models.** Interpret CIC in set theory: `Prop` by the two truth values $\{\emptyset, \{\emptyset\}\}$ (proof irrelevance!), `Type u` by a Grothendieck universe, Π-types by set-theoretic function spaces, inductive types by least fixed points. Werner (1997) showed that CIC with $n$ universes is consistent relative to ZFC plus $n$ inaccessible cardinals — and conversely that it can interpret that set theory, so the two are *equiconsistent*. Lean's type theory, with countably many universes, is correspondingly equiconsistent with ZFC plus countably many inaccessibles. Mario Carneiro's master's thesis (2019) worked this out in detail for Lean.

<Theorem title="Consistency of Lean's type theory (Carneiro 2019)">
Lean's type theory, including `Classical.choice`, `propext` and quotients, has a model in ZFC plus $\omega$ inaccessible cardinals; hence it is consistent if that set theory is.
</Theorem>

The model interprets `Prop` proof-irrelevantly and `Type` classically, which is why the classical axioms are validated. It does not prove that the *kernel implementation* is correct — for that, independent re-implementations and formal verification projects (Chapter 17) are needed.

## Where Lean's type theory is less tidy

Adding features to a type theory can compromise the nice properties even when consistency survives. Lean 4 knowingly accepts some such compromises in exchange for convenience:

- **Definitional equality is not transitive.** Chapter 14 showed a case: `pick (inl …) ≡ pick (inr …)` by proof irrelevance and `pick (inr …) ≡ false` by computation, yet the kernel does not accept `pick (inl …) ≡ false`. Abel and Coquand (2020) showed that with proof irrelevance, impredicative `Prop` and `Acc`'s large elimination, definitional equality is in fact **undecidable** — the kernel's algorithm is a sound but incomplete approximation of the declarative rules.
- **Subject reduction fails** in corner cases, as a consequence: reduction can turn a well-typed term into one the algorithm does not accept.
- **Canonicity fails with axioms.** A closed term built with `Classical.choice` or `propext` may be stuck: the kernel cannot compute it to a constructor.

<Playground errors title="Stuck on an axiom" code={`axiom myChoice : Nat        -- an axiom has no definition

None of these affect consistency: the model still exists. They are the price of definitional proof irrelevance and of classical reasoning. Rocq makes different trade-offs — its `Prop` is not definitionally proof-irrelevant (a separate sort `SProp` is), which keeps definitional equality decidable — and other systems (Chapter 20) try to have the best of both.

## Summary

- The key properties are subject reduction, confluence, strong normalisation, consistency, decidability and canonicity; normalisation implies consistency and, with confluence, decidability.
- Each kernel restriction blocks a known route to inconsistency; the course kernel lets you lift them and see the paradoxes appear.
- CIC is proved consistent by normalisation arguments (Werner) or by set-theoretic models; Lean's theory is equiconsistent with ZFC plus countably many inaccessibles.
- Lean trades a few tidy properties (transitivity and decidability of definitional equality, canonicity with axioms) for convenience, without compromising consistency.

<History title="The metatheory of CIC">
Coquand and Gallier proved strong normalisation of CoC in 1990 with a modular proof; Werner's thesis (1994) extended normalisation to CIC; his *Sets in types, types in sets* (1997) established the relationship with set theory. The MetaCoq project (from 2018) formalised Coq's metatheory, including a verified type checker, inside Coq, under the assumption of strong normalisation. Abel and Coquand's *Failure of normalization in impredicative type theory with proof-irrelevant propositional equality* (2020) showed the limits of definitional proof irrelevance; Carneiro's *The Type Theory of Lean* (2019) is the standard reference for Lean's metatheory.
</History>
