Every modern programming language has anonymous functions. In JavaScript you write `x => x + 1`; in Python `lambda x: x + 1`; in Haskell `\x -> x + 1`. The notation descends directly from a formal system Alonzo Church invented in the early 1930s, in which *everything* is an anonymous function: there are no numbers, no booleans, no data structures, no loops — only functions, applied to functions, returning functions.

It sounds like a toy. It is in fact a complete model of computation, as powerful as any programming language, and it is the backbone of every system in this course. CIC is, at its core, a λ-calculus with a very sophisticated type system bolted on. So we start here, without any types at all.

## Terms

The λ-calculus has exactly three kinds of expression, called **terms**.

<Definition title="λ-terms">
Given an infinite supply of variable names $x, y, z, \dots$, the terms are generated by the grammar

$$
M, N \;::=\; x \;\mid\; \lambda x.\, M \;\mid\; M\, N
$$

- a **variable** $x$;
- an **abstraction** $\lambda x.\, M$ — the function with parameter $x$ and body $M$;
- an **application** $M\,N$ — the function $M$ applied to the argument $N$.
</Definition>

That's all. There are a few conventions that keep terms readable:

- Application associates to the left: $f\,a\,b$ means $(f\,a)\,b$. A function of two arguments is a function that returns a function (this is called *currying*, after Haskell Curry).
- The body of a λ extends as far to the right as possible: $\lambda x.\, f\, x\, y$ means $\lambda x.\, ((f\,x)\,y)$, not $(\lambda x.\, f)\, x\, y$.
- Nested abstractions can be merged: $\lambda x\, y.\, M$ abbreviates $\lambda x.\, \lambda y.\, M$.

For example, $\lambda x.\, x$ is the identity function; $\lambda x\, y.\, x$ takes two arguments and returns the first; $\lambda f\, x.\, f\,(f\,x)$ takes a function and an argument and applies the function twice.

<Engineer title="Terms are trees">
Parentheses and conventions are only about writing terms as strings. The real object is a tree whose internal nodes are λ-nodes (one child) and application nodes (two children), and whose leaves are variables. Every implementation, including this course's kernel, works with such trees. The *Syntax tree* view in the labs below shows them.
</Engineer>

## Free and bound variables

In $\lambda x.\, M$, the λ **binds** the variable $x$ inside $M$. An occurrence of a variable that is not inside a λ binding it is **free**. In

$$
\lambda x.\, x\, y
$$

$x$ is bound and $y$ is free. A term with no free variables is **closed**. Think of a free variable as a global that the term refers to, and a bound variable as a function parameter.

Bound variables are *placeholders*: their names don't matter. $\lambda x.\, x$ and $\lambda y.\, y$ are the same function, just as `x => x` and `y => y` are the same JavaScript function. Renaming a bound variable (consistently, and without clashing with other names) is called **α-conversion**, and two terms that differ only by such renamings are **α-equivalent**. We will always consider α-equivalent terms to be *equal*. Taking that seriously — and implementing it correctly — is the subject of the next chapter.

## Computation: β-reduction

There is a single computation rule. Applying a function $\lambda x.\, M$ to an argument $N$ yields the body $M$ with $N$ substituted for $x$:

<Definition title="β-reduction">
$$
(\lambda x.\, M)\; N \;\;\longrightarrow_\beta\;\; M[x := N]
$$

A term of the form $(\lambda x.\, M)\, N$ is called a **redex** (reducible expression). A β-step may contract any redex anywhere inside a term — under λs, in function position, in argument position.
</Definition>

For example,

$$
(\lambda x.\, f\, x\, x)\; a \;\longrightarrow_\beta\; f\, a\, a
$$

and, contracting one redex at a time,

$$
(\lambda f\, x.\, f\,(f\,x))\; g\; a \;\longrightarrow_\beta\; (\lambda x.\, g\,(g\,x))\; a \;\longrightarrow_\beta\; g\,(g\,a).
$$

Here is the first interactive lab. The highlighted part of the term is the redex that will be contracted next; the argument being substituted is shaded blue. Press **step** to perform one β-reduction, or click any λ that heads a redex to reduce *that* redex instead. Edit the term to try your own.

<LambdaLab title="β-reduction" stdlib="false" code="(λf x. f (f x)) (λy. g y y) a" views="text,tree" />

Switch to the **Syntax tree** view and step again: a β-step takes an application node whose left child is a λ, deletes both, and grafts copies of the argument subtree where the bound variable occurred. The dashed arcs connect each variable occurrence to the λ that binds it.

<Warning title="Substitution is subtle">
The notation $M[x := N]$ hides a real difficulty. Consider $(\lambda x\, y.\, x)\; y$. Naively replacing $x$ by $y$ in $\lambda y.\, x$ gives $\lambda y.\, y$ — the identity function! But $\lambda x\, y.\, x$ is the function that returns its first argument *ignoring the second*, so applying it to $y$ should give "the function that ignores its argument and returns $y$". The free $y$ we passed in has been **captured** by the inner $\lambda y$. Correct, *capture-avoiding* substitution renames the inner binder first: the result is $\lambda y_1.\, y$. Try it in the lab above — the badge **α** marks steps where a renaming was necessary. Chapter 2 is devoted to getting this right.
</Warning>

## Normal forms and non-termination

A term with no redexes is in **normal form**. Normal forms are the "values" or "answers" of the λ-calculus: computation is the process of reducing a term to normal form.

Does every term have a normal form? No. The smallest counterexample is

$$
\Omega \;=\; (\lambda x.\, x\, x)\,(\lambda x.\, x\, x).
$$

It has exactly one redex, and contracting it gives back… $\Omega$ itself. The reduction never ends. Other terms grow without bound: $(\lambda x.\, x\, x\, x)(\lambda x.\, x\, x\, x)$ reduces to a longer term with the same redex, and so on forever.

<LambdaLab title="Terms without normal form" stdlib="false" code="(λx. x x) (λx. x x)" views="text,tree,graph" />

The **All reductions** view draws the *reduction graph* of a term: every term reachable by any sequence of β-steps, with an arrow for each step. For Ω the graph is a single node with a loop back to itself.

## Reduction strategies

A term can contain several redexes, and we are free to choose which to contract. Does the choice matter? Consider

$$
(\lambda x\, y.\, y)\;\Omega
$$

The function ignores its argument. If we contract the outer redex first, we get $\lambda y.\, y$ in one step — a normal form. If we insist on reducing the argument $\Omega$ first, we loop forever.

A **reduction strategy** is a rule for choosing the next redex. The classical ones are:

- **normal order**: always contract the *leftmost-outermost* redex. This is what lazy languages like Haskell (essentially) do.
- **applicative order**: always contract the *leftmost-innermost* redex — reduce arguments before substituting them. This is what strict languages like JavaScript, Python or OCaml do.
- **call by name** and **call by value**: the same two ideas, but never reducing under a λ. They stop at *weak head normal form*, which is what real programming languages compute: a function value is not evaluated further until it is called.

<LambdaLab title="Strategies matter" code="(λx y. y) OMEGA" views="text,graph" />

Use the strategy menu in the top-right corner of the lab. Under normal order the term reaches its normal form in a single step: the outer redex is contracted and `OMEGA` is simply discarded. Under applicative order the lab first unfolds the definition `OMEGA` (a *δ-step*; definitions are explained below) and then keeps reducing it, getting nowhere, until you give up.

<Theorem title="Standardisation (Curry & Feys, 1958)">
If a term has a normal form, then normal-order reduction reaches it.
</Theorem>

So normal order is the "safe" strategy. It can be inefficient — it may copy an unevaluated argument many times and then evaluate each copy separately — which is why real implementations of lazy languages use *sharing* (call by need). The course kernel uses a lazy, weak-head strategy in the same spirit.

## Confluence: the answer does not depend on the path

Different strategies may take different routes, and some routes never end. But can two routes that *do* end arrive at different answers? The fundamental theorem of the λ-calculus says no.

<Theorem title="Church–Rosser (1936)">
If $M \twoheadrightarrow N_1$ and $M \twoheadrightarrow N_2$ (where $\twoheadrightarrow$ means "reduces in zero or more steps"), then there is a term $P$ with $N_1 \twoheadrightarrow P$ and $N_2 \twoheadrightarrow P$.
</Theorem>

This property is called **confluence**. Pictorially: every "fork" in the reduction graph can be closed into a diamond. A consequence is that **normal forms are unique**: if a term had two different normal forms, neither could reduce further, so they could not be joined.

<LambdaLab title="Diamonds everywhere" stdlib="false" code="(λx. x x) ((λy. y) z)" view="graph" views="graph,text,tree" />

In the graph above, the term at the left can reduce in two ways — contract the outer redex, duplicating the inner one, or contract the inner redex first. The paths diverge and then meet again at the normal form $z\,z$. Hover over the nodes to see the terms; drag to pan and scroll to zoom. Try a term with more redexes, such as `(λx. x x x) ((λy. y) ((λz. z) w))`, and watch the diamonds multiply.

Confluence will matter enormously later. In a type theory, types can contain computation, and the kernel decides whether two types are equal by *computing* them. That is only sensible if the result of computation does not depend on the order in which the kernel happens to do it.

## Programming with nothing

We have only functions. Where are the numbers, the booleans, the data? The surprising answer is that data can be *represented* by functions — by what the data lets you do.

### Booleans

What can you do with a boolean? Choose between two alternatives. So let a boolean *be* its own `if-then-else`: a function that takes two arguments and returns one of them.

```lambda
TRUE  = λt f. t
FALSE = λt f. f
IF    = λb t f. b t f
```

`IF TRUE a b` reduces to `TRUE a b` and then to `a`. Once you have `IF`, the logical operations follow: `NOT = λb. b FALSE TRUE` — if `b` is true, return false, else true.

In the labs, a line of the form `NAME = term` is a **definition**. Using `NAME` later creates a *δ-redex*: a step that replaces the name by its definition (shown with a δ badge). The labs come with a standard library of the definitions in this section already loaded.

### Pairs

A pair of `a` and `b` is something that, given a *selector*, hands both components to it:

```lambda
PAIR = λa b s. s a b
FST  = λp. p TRUE
SND  = λp. p FALSE
```

`FST (PAIR a b)` reduces to `PAIR a b TRUE`, then to `TRUE a b`, then to `a`.

### Numbers

What can you do with a natural number $n$? Iterate something $n$ times. The **Church numeral** for $n$ is the function that takes $f$ and $x$ and applies $f$ to $x$, $n$ times:

$$
\overline{0} = \lambda f\, x.\, x \qquad
\overline{1} = \lambda f\, x.\, f\, x \qquad
\overline{2} = \lambda f\, x.\, f\,(f\,x) \qquad
\overline{3} = \lambda f\, x.\, f\,(f\,(f\,x))
$$

In the labs, a numeral like `3` is shorthand for its Church numeral. Arithmetic is iteration:

```lambda
SUCC = λn f x. f (n f x)          -- apply f once more
PLUS = λm n f x. m f (n f x)      -- m more applications after n
MULT = λm n f. m (n f)            -- iterate "apply f n times", m times
EXP  = λm n. n m                  -- iterate "multiply by m", n times
```

### The predecessor, and a trip to the dentist

Addition and multiplication are easy; subtraction is not. How do you *un*-apply a function? Church himself could not find the predecessor function. The solution came from his student Stephen Kleene, who (according to the story he later told) found it while at the dentist having his wisdom teeth removed. The trick: iterate on *pairs*. Start from $(0, 0)$ and repeatedly map $(a, b)$ to $(b, b+1)$. After $n$ steps the first component is $n-1$.

```lambda
PRED = λn. FST (n (λp. PAIR (SND p) (SUCC (SND p))) (PAIR 0 0))
```

The library uses a slicker but less readable version. Either way, computing the predecessor of $n$ takes time proportional to $n$ — a first hint that representing data by functions is elegant but inefficient. We will meet this problem again, in a typed setting, in Chapter 9, and it is one of the reasons CIC has inductive types built in.

<LambdaLab title="Kleene's predecessor" code={`PRED' = λn. FST (n (λp. PAIR (SND p) (SUCC (SND p))) (PAIR 0 0))
PRED' 3`} />

### Recursion without names

How do you write a recursive function when functions have no names to call themselves by? With a **fixed-point combinator**. The most famous is Curry's

$$
Y \;=\; \lambda f.\, (\lambda x.\, f\,(x\,x))\,(\lambda x.\, f\,(x\,x)).
$$

For any $F$, $Y\,F$ reduces to $F\,(Y\,F)$ (up to one more step of unfolding): $Y\,F$ is a *fixed point* of $F$. So to define a recursive function, write a non-recursive $F$ that takes "the function itself" as an extra argument, and tie the knot with $Y$:

```lambda
FACT = Y (λfact n. IF (ISZERO n) 1 (MULT n (fact (PRED n))))
```

<LambdaLab title="Recursion via Y" code={`FACT = Y (λfact n. IF (ISZERO n) 1 (MULT n (fact (PRED n))))
FACT 3`} view="text" views="text,tromp" maxSteps={3000} />

Press **normalise** (normal order). The computation takes a few hundred steps — the Church encoding is slow — but it gets there. Now switch to *applicative order* and try again: the strict strategy evaluates `fact (PRED n)` before `IF` gets to look at the condition, so the recursion never stops. (Strict languages use a variant of Y, the *Z combinator*, that delays the self-application behind a λ.)

## Everything is computable, nothing is decidable

With booleans, numbers, pairs and recursion we can encode any algorithm. In 1936 Church proposed that the λ-definable functions are exactly the functions that are "effectively calculable" by any means; Alan Turing, the same year, proposed his machines for the same purpose and soon proved the two notions equivalent. This is the **Church–Turing thesis**.

Universality has a price. The question "does this term have a normal form?" is exactly the halting problem, and it is **undecidable**: no algorithm answers it for all terms. Church proved this in 1936, before Turing's more famous version.

<Problem title="Why this is a problem for us">
We want a kernel that decides whether a term is a valid proof, and in type theory the kernel will need to *compute* with terms (Chapter 7 shows why). If computation might not terminate, the kernel might not terminate. Worse, a non-terminating term like $Y\,(\lambda x.\, x)$ can inhabit any type we like — it would be a "proof" of every proposition, including false ones.

The untyped λ-calculus is the perfect language for computation and a hopeless language for logic. In Part II we restrict it with **types**, which rule out terms like Ω and $Y$. The rest of the course is about getting back as much expressive power as possible without letting non-termination back in.
</Problem>

## A note on η

Besides β there is a second, weaker equation between λ-terms:

$$
\lambda x.\, M\, x \;=_\eta\; M \qquad (x \text{ not free in } M)
$$

A function that just passes its argument to $M$ *is* $M$, as far as anyone applying it can tell. This is **η-conversion** (eta). It is not a computation step in the labs, but it is part of the definitional equality of Lean's type theory: the kernel considers `fun x => f x` and `f` to be the same. We come back to it in Chapter 17.

</details>

## Summary

- The λ-calculus has three constructs: variables, abstraction, application — and one computation rule, β-reduction.
- Bound variable names are irrelevant (α-equivalence), but substitution must avoid capturing free variables.
- Terms may or may not have normal forms; normal-order reduction finds them when they exist.
- Reduction is confluent, so normal forms are unique.
- Data can be encoded as functions: booleans, pairs, numerals, and recursion via fixed-point combinators.
- The calculus is Turing-complete, and therefore termination is undecidable — which disqualifies it, as it stands, as a logic.

<History title="Church's two calculi">
Church introduced the λ-calculus in 1932 as part of a system of *logic*, in which functions and propositions coexisted. In 1935 his students Kleene and Rosser showed that system inconsistent — a paradox much like Russell's. Church kept the purely computational fragment, which became the λ-calculus we know, and proved its confluence with Rosser in 1936. Only in 1940 did he return to logic, with the *simple theory of types* — the subject of Chapter 3. For thirty years the untyped λ-calculus had no mathematical *meaning* beyond its syntax: a function that can be applied to itself did not correspond to anything in set theory. Dana Scott found the first models in 1969, founding the field of denotational semantics.
</History>
