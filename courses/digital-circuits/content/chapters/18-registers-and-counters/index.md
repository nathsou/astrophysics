A digital watch has a crystal that vibrates 32 768 times a second and a display that changes once. Between the two there is nothing but a chain of flip-flops, each one halving the frequency of the one before. Fifteen halvings turn 32 768 Hz into exactly 1 Hz, because 32 768 is 2<sup>15</sup>, and the crystal was cut to that odd-looking frequency for this reason. The chain is a counter, and it is the simplest thing you can build with the flip-flop of Chapter 17 once you have more than one.

```quiz
q: 'You feed a 32 768 Hz signal into a chain of flip-flops, each of which toggles on every rising edge of its input. How many flip-flops give you one pulse per second?'
options:
  - text: 15
    correct: true
    why: 'Each toggle flip-flop halves the frequency: 32 768 → 16 384 → … → 2 → 1. That is 15 halvings, and 2^15 = 32 768.'
  - text: 32 768
    why: 'That would be a counter that counts every pulse individually, one flip-flop per pulse. A binary counter needs only one flip-flop per *doubling*.'
  - text: 16
    why: 'Sixteen would give 0.5 Hz, one pulse every two seconds: 2^16 = 65 536.'
  - text: 8
    why: 'Eight halvings leave 128 Hz.'
```

This chapter takes flip-flops in rows. A **register** stores a word. A **counter** adds one at every tick. A **shift register** passes a bit along the row. Each is a small idea, and each is used in every computer: the program counter that Chapter 21 will build is a counter with a load input; the accumulator is a register; a serial port is a shift register; and the counters here divide the frequency of the crystal to make every slower clock in the machine.

## A register: a word that stays put

A :term[register]{id=register} is a row of flip-flops that share a clock and load together. An 8-bit register is eight D flip-flops with one wire for the clock. That much stores a byte, but it also changes the byte at every clock edge, whether or not you want it to. Real registers have a **load enable**: the register loads D only when EN is 1, and otherwise keeps what it has.

How do you make a flip-flop keep its value? Not by stopping its clock, though that is what a software person might try. Every clock edge must arrive at every flip-flop at the same moment (Chapter 17), and a gate in the clock path delays some edges and can produce runt pulses. The standard way is to leave the clock alone and change what is on D: feed D from a multiplexer that chooses between the new data (when EN is 1) and the flip-flop's own output (when it is 0). Holding is the flip-flop loading its own old value, once per cycle.

::circuit{src="18-registers-and-counters/circuits/register4.json" title="A register with a load enable" n="18.1" speed=1e-6 caption="Set D3 to D0 with the switches and press CLK (the round button). Nothing happens while EN is 0; the output Q keeps its old value however D changes. Set EN to 1, press CLK, and Q takes D. Each bit is a multiplexer (choosing between the data and the flip-flop's own Q) in front of a D flip-flop."}

In DCL the same idea is a single line, because the language makes holding explicit: a register that is not given a new value is an error, so “keep the old one” must be written down as `next value = value`.

::snippet-playground{id="register" n="18.2" tab="run" caption="A byte of storage in DCL. Set d to a value, set load to 1 and press Step clock: q takes it. Set load back to 0, change d and step again: q keeps the old value. Open the Circuit tab: eight flip-flops, each fed by a multiplexer whose other input is its own output, exactly the structure of Figure 18.1."}

:::programmer[A register is a variable]
In software, a variable keeps its value until you assign to it. In hardware nothing is “kept”: a flip-flop reloads something at every tick, and “keeping” means reloading itself. That is why `if load { d } else { value }` is not an optimisation to write, but the only thing a register can mean. It is also why a register is drawn with an enable: an assignment statement in a program that only runs `if (load)` becomes a multiplexer here.
:::

## Counting by halves: the ripple counter

Take the toggle flip-flop of Chapter 17, which changes state on every rising edge of its clock and so runs at half the clock's frequency. Now clock a second toggle flip-flop from the *output* of the first: it toggles once for every two edges of the original clock, so it runs at a quarter. A third runs at an eighth, and so on. Read the outputs together as a binary number, with the last stage as the least significant bit: the row counts.

The stage has to toggle when the previous output *falls*, not when it rises. The previous output falls exactly when a carry is due: in binary, adding one to …0111 turns all the ones to zeros and the first zero to a one, and each bit that falls is the signal for the next bit to flip. With a D flip-flop with Q̄ fed back to D, the clock of the next stage is the previous stage's Q̄, which rises exactly when Q falls.

::circuit{src="18-registers-and-counters/circuits/ripple4.json" title="A 4-bit ripple counter" n="18.3" speed=1e-7 window=800e-9 traces="CLK,Q0,Q1,Q2,Q3" caption="Four D flip-flops, each with its own Q̄ fed back to its D input (the flag N0 to N3), and each clocked by the previous stage's Q̄. The traces show a staircase: Q0 runs at half the clock, Q1 at a quarter, Q2 at an eighth, Q3 at a sixteenth. Read from Q3 down to Q0 as a binary number, the outputs count 0000, 0001, 0010, …"}

It is called a :term[ripple counter]{id=ripple-counter} because the carry ripples down the chain: the second stage does not see its clock edge until the first has changed. That saves gates, since there is no logic between the stages at all, and it is why the crystal in the watch has a ripple counter behind it. The CD4060 is a 14-stage ripple counter with an oscillator on the same chip: put a 32 768 Hz crystal on it and its last output runs at 32 768 / 2<sup>14</sup> = 2 Hz.:cite[ti-cd4060b]

:::history{year=1932 title="Scale of two" people="C. E. Wynn-Williams"}
Ernest Rutherford's Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge wanted to count particles from radioactive sources: pulses that could come far faster than a mechanical counter, with an armature to move, could follow. In 1932 C. E. Wynn-Williams showed how to make the counting fast: a chain of thyratron circuits, each of which passes on only every second pulse it receives, so that the slow mechanical meter at the end has to keep up with one pulse in every 2<sup>n</sup> of the ones that arrive.:cite[wynn-williams1932] He called it a :term[scale of two]{id=scale-of-two}.

The two-state circuit at the heart of every stage is the same idea as the trigger relay of Eccles and Jordan (Chapter 16). Every counter in this chapter is a descendant of it, now built from transistors and drawn as a D flip-flop with its output fed back.
:::

## Ripple against synchronous

There is a price for having no logic between the stages: the wait. Every flip-flop takes its clock-to-Q delay to respond, and in a ripple counter the delays *add*. The count is not valid until the last stage has changed, and until then the counter shows numbers it was never meant to show. Predict what that looks like before you look.

```quiz
q: 'A 4-bit ripple counter goes from 7 (0111) to 8 (1000). Each flip-flop takes 12 ns to respond to its clock. What does a logic analyser on the four outputs show, reading them as a binary number?'
options:
  - text: 7, then 8, 12 ns after the clock edge.
    why: 'That is what a counter with all its bits on one clock does. In a ripple counter each flip-flop is clocked by the one before, so they cannot all change at the same moment.'
  - text: '7, then 6, then 4, then 0, then 8: one wrong value for each stage the carry has to pass.'
    correct: true
    why: 'Bit 0 falls first (0110 = 6), which makes bit 1 fall 12 ns later (0100 = 4), then bit 2 (0000 = 0), and only then does bit 3 rise (1000 = 8). Three wrong values, 12 ns each.'
  - text: 7, then 15, then 8.
    why: 'The bits that change all change in the right direction, one after another, low bit first. Nothing rises early.'
```

The other way to build it is to give every flip-flop the *same* clock, and let each work out for itself whether it must toggle. Bit k toggles when every bit below it is 1 (that is what the carry of “add one” is), so the logic in front of each D input is an XOR of the bit's own value with an AND of all the lower bits. Then all the flip-flops change at the same instant, one clock-to-Q after the edge, and the count is valid immediately.

::circuit{src="18-registers-and-counters/circuits/sync4.json" title="A 4-bit synchronous counter" n="18.4" speed=1e-7 window=800e-9 traces="CLK,Q0,Q1,Q2,Q3" caption="All four flip-flops share one clock. Bit 0 always toggles (it is fed its own inverse); bit 1 toggles when Q0 is 1; bit 2 when Q0 and Q1 are both 1; bit 3 when Q0, Q1 and Q2 are all 1. The traces show the same staircase as the ripple counter, but every edge lines up with the clock."}

::counter-lab{n="18.5" caption="A ripple counter and a synchronous counter on a logic analyser. The row labelled count is the four bits read as a number; wrong values are drawn in red. Choose Ripple, step from 7 to 8 and see the three wrong values; choose Synchronous and they are gone. Set the decoder to watch for count 2 and read the tally underneath. Then press Start sound, switch on the outputs one by one and hear the octaves."}

:::lab[Watch the ripple]
1. **A step with one bit.** Leave the counter on Ripple and drag *Step from count* to 0 (0 to 1): only bit 0 changes, and there is nothing to ripple through. Then go to 7, 15, 3 and 11: the more ones at the bottom of the number, the longer the staircase.
2. **The decoder.** With *Decoder watches for* at 2, the decoder is an AND gate that should be high only when the count is 0010. Look at its tally: on the ripple counter it pulses more often than the number of times the counter really reaches 2. The extra pulses come at 3 → 4 (through 2), at 7 → 8 (through 6, 4, 0), and so on. Whatever reads the outputs sees the wrong number if it looks too early. Switch to Synchronous: the pulses and the correct counts agree.
3. **The limit.** With Ripple selected, drag *Clock to Q* up to 30 ns. Four stages take 120 ns and the clock period is 100 ns: the message says that the count is never valid.
4. **The octaves.** Press *Start sound*, switch on CLK and Q0, then Q1 and Q2. A note and the note an octave below it: the CLK is 1 760 Hz (the A two octaves above middle A), and each output is half of the frequency before, so exactly an octave lower. Both counters sound the same, because they divide the frequency in the same ratio.
:::

Both counters divide the frequency in the same way, so the choice is about *when you look*. A ripple counter is fine when only the last stage matters, as in the watch, where nothing looks at the middle bits, or when the count is only read slowly. It is a bad choice when something must read the whole number after every clock: a decoder, a comparator, an address. That is why the program counter of Chapter 21 is synchronous.

The speed limits differ, too. The synchronous counter must obey the same rule as every synchronous circuit (Chapter 17): the clock period must be at least t<sub>cq</sub> + t<sub>pd</sub> + t<sub>su</sub>, where t<sub>pd</sub> is the delay of the AND and XOR in front of the flip-flop. The ripple counter's *clock* can run much faster, because nothing but one flip-flop's toggle has to fit in a period, but its *answer* takes n × t<sub>cq</sub> to be right.

When you need a slower rhythm, you do what the synchronous counter does: keep the fast clock and use an *enable* that is high one cycle in N. The `tick-gen` exercise of Chapter 29 is exactly that.

## Shifting

A :term[shift register]{id=shift-register} is a chain of flip-flops in which each one's D input is the previous one's Q, and all share a clock. At every edge each bit moves up one place, and a new bit enters at the bottom (SI, serial in). After four clocks the four bits you put in are in the register, in order. It is the memory-based version of a queue, and it turns time into space.

::circuit{src="18-registers-and-counters/circuits/shift4.json" title="A 4-bit shift register" n="18.8" speed=1e-6 caption="Set SI (the switch on the left) to a bit and press CLK; then set the next bit and press again. Every press moves each bit one place to the right, and the bit you set enters at the left. After four presses of 1, 0, 1, 1 the four lamps show the bits you sent, the first one at the far end."}

Two things make shift registers useful.

- **Serial to parallel and back.** One wire, four clocks, and four bits are available at once (*serial in, parallel out*). With a multiplexer in front of each D input that chooses between the previous stage and a parallel load, the register does the reverse: load four bits at once and send them out one by one (*parallel in, serial out*). That is what a serial port does. Every UART and SPI interface in Chapter 24 has a shift register at its heart, and so does the 74HC595, the chip that gives a microcontroller eight more output pins from three wires.
- **A left shift is a multiplication.** If the register holds a binary number, shifting it one place to the left doubles it (the bit that falls off the end is the carry). The shifter of Chapter 14 does the same in one step with multiplexers; the shift register does it one place per clock.

A shift register also shows why hold time is not a formality. Every D input is connected straight to the previous Q, with *no gates at all* between them, so the data launched at an edge arrives at the next flip-flop after only a clock-to-Q delay. The edge must have finished being looked at (the hold time) before the new data arrives: t<sub>cq</sub> must be longer than t<sub>h</sub>, plus any clock skew. Flip-flops are designed with a hold time shorter than their clock-to-Q for exactly this reason. Chapter 17's *Break the clock* shows the failure: no gates, and a clock that reaches the second flip-flop 2 ns late.

## The LFSR: a counter that counts in a strange order

Put an XOR gate in the path of a shift register: the bit that enters at the bottom is not the input but the XOR of two or three of the bits already in the register (the **taps**). This is a :term[linear-feedback shift register]{id=lfsr}, an LFSR. It has no input and no counting logic, and it steps through a long sequence of values that looks random, without ever repeating until it has visited nearly all of them.

The right choice of taps gives a :term[maximal-length]{id=maximal-length-sequence} sequence. An n-bit register has 2<sup>n</sup> states; the all-zero state is a trap (zero XOR zero is zero, so the register stays there for ever), and with the right taps the other 2<sup>n</sup> − 1 form a single cycle. An 8-bit LFSR with taps at bits 7, 5, 4 and 3 visits all 255 non-zero bytes before it repeats. The out-of-place order is the point: it costs eight flip-flops and one three-XOR tree, where a counter of the same range needs an adder.

::lfsr-wheel{n="18.10" caption="Every state of the register, laid out on wheels: dots joined in the order the register visits them. Start with 4 bits and the maximal taps: one wheel of 15 states (the top of the wheel is the smallest state on it), and the all-zero state alone. Press Step, or Play, and follow the orange dot. Then press A poor choice, or click the taps to change them, and watch the states split into several short wheels. Try 8 bits: the maximal taps give a single wheel of 255 states."}

:::lab[Which taps?]
1. Choose 4 bits and *Maximal taps*: the feedback is x⁴ + x + 1, one wheel of 15 states, and the output stream is 15 bits long before it repeats. Count the ones in it: eight ones and seven zeros. Every maximal sequence has 2<sup>n−1</sup> ones and 2<sup>n−1</sup> − 1 zeros.
2. Click the tap on bit 1 to turn it on as well. The polynomial changes, and the single wheel splits. The number of states is the same, but they no longer form one cycle; where you start decides which cycle you are in, and you cannot get from one to another.
3. Choose 5 bits, then 6, 7 and 8. The number of tap sets that work (out of 2<sup>n−1</sup>) is small: 2, 2, 6, 6, 18, 16 for 3 to 8 bits. The maximal taps are the exponents of a *primitive polynomial* over the two-element field, and there is a table for every length.
:::

Where would a pseudo-random sequence that repeats be any use? Three places.

- **Testing and scrambling.** Chips test themselves by feeding an LFSR into a circuit and checking a checksum of the result. Data links XOR their data with an LFSR stream, so that long runs of zeros or ones (which the receiver could not keep in step with) are broken up. Reading the wheel, the register is only a cheap source of a sequence with no obvious pattern.
- **Error checking.** A CRC is the remainder of dividing the data by a polynomial, and the circuit that computes it is an LFSR.
- **Spread-spectrum radio.** Every GPS satellite transmits a pattern of 1 023 bits, made by two 10-bit LFSRs, at 1.023 million bits a second, so that it repeats every millisecond. A receiver finds out which satellite it hears and how far away it is by sliding its own copy of the pattern along until they match.

:::history{year=1967 title="Shift register sequences" people="Solomon W. Golomb"}
The mathematics of these sequences was worked out in the 1950s and 1960s, in the service of radar and radio. Solomon Golomb collected the theory in a book, *Shift Register Sequences*, published in 1967:cite[golomb1967]: which feedback connections give the longest cycles, why the sequences have exactly as many ones as they do, and why their autocorrelation (the match between a sequence and a shifted copy of itself) is so sharp.

The sharp autocorrelation is what GPS uses. The civilian C/A code of each satellite is a *Gold code*, named for Robert Gold, who described the family in 1967:cite[gold1967]: the XOR of the outputs of two 10-stage LFSRs, 1 023 chips long, sent at 1.023 million chips a second.:cite[is-gps-200]
:::

```quiz
q: 'A 16-bit synchronous counter uses a chain of half adders for its incrementer (a carry chain of 16 gates). Which change lets it run at a higher clock frequency, without changing what it counts?'
options:
  - text: Clock the flip-flops from each other, as in a ripple counter.
    why: 'That turns it into a ripple counter, and the count is then valid only after all sixteen delays have added up. It would look faster and give wrong numbers.'
  - text: Compute each carry directly with a wide AND of all the lower bits, instead of passing it along the chain.
    correct: true
    why: 'This is carry lookahead (Chapter 14). Each bit toggles when every lower bit is 1: one wide AND per bit, in parallel, so the delay of the logic no longer grows with the width. It costs gates, and wide ANDs are built from several levels, but the growth is logarithmic and not linear.'
  - text: Use a slower clock for the higher bits.
    why: 'The higher bits change at most half as often, but they are still sampled on the same clock edge and still need the carry in time. Two clocks would add the problems of Chapter 17 (crossing between clock domains) and solve none.'
```

## Build it for real

:::real{parts="74HC161, 74HC595, 8 LEDs, 8 × 330 Ω resistors, 555 timer and its parts from Chapter 17 (or a debounced push button), 5 V USB supply module, breadboard, jumper wires"}
**A 74HC161 counting on LEDs.** The 74HC161 is a 4-bit synchronous counter. Ground is pin 8 and +5 V pin 16. Tie pin 1 (master reset, active low), pin 9 (parallel enable, active low), pin 7 (CEP) and pin 10 (CET) to +5 V, so that the counter is always counting; the inputs D0–D3 (pins 3 to 6) can go to ground. Put an LED with a 330 Ω resistor from each output to ground: pin 14 is Q0, the least significant bit, pin 13 is Q1, pin 12 is Q2 and pin 11 is Q3. Pin 15 (TC, terminal count) goes high on the count of 15. Feed pin 2 with the clock: the 555 of Chapter 17 at about 1.5 Hz, or a push button through the debouncer of Chapter 17, and watch the four LEDs count in binary. Q0 blinks at half the clock, Q1 at a quarter, Q2 at an eighth and Q3 at a sixteenth: the octaves you heard. Hold pin 9 low across a clock edge and the D inputs are loaded into the counter; take pin 1 low and it clears at once, without waiting for a clock.

**A 74HC595 driving eight LEDs.** The 74HC595 is a serial-in, parallel-out shift register with an output latch. Ground is pin 8 and +5 V pin 16; tie pin 10 (clear, active low) to +5 V and pin 13 (output enable, active low) to ground. The serial data goes to pin 14 (SER), the shift clock to pin 11 (SRCLK) and the latch clock to pin 12 (RCLK). The eight outputs are pin 15 (QA), then pins 1 to 7 (QB to QH): an LED and a 330 Ω resistor on each. Set pin 14 to the first bit and give pin 11 a rising edge (with a debounced button, or two jumper wires); repeat for eight bits, then give pin 12 a rising edge: the eight bits appear at the outputs at once. Two shift registers are used inside: one that shifts, and one, the latch, that holds what the LEDs show, so that they do not flicker while the bits are shifted in. Pin 9 (QH′) is the last stage of the shift register itself: connect it to the SER of a second 74HC595 to get sixteen outputs from three wires.:cite[ti-sn74hc-family]
:::

## What’s next

A counter is the simplest **state machine**: a circuit with a memory whose next state depends only on its current state, and which goes round a loop. The LFSR is another, with a stranger loop. Chapter 19 makes it general. Any behaviour that unfolds in steps, such as a traffic light, a combination lock or a vending machine, can be written down as states and transitions and turned, mechanically, into flip-flops and gates. The registers of this chapter hold the state; the gates in front of them, Chapter 11's combinational logic, compute what comes next.
