## What each stage is worth

The emulator counts every instruction it executes, so we can measure what the passes buy. These are dynamic RISC-V instruction counts for four of the examples, as measured by the test-suite:

| program | -O0 | -O1 | -O2 | -O2, linear scan | -O2, 5 registers | -O2, no peephole |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| collatz | 233,729 | 145,807 | 147,886 | 181,752 | 151,173 | 152,850 |
| sieve | 3,573 | 2,246 | 2,042 | 2,439 | 2,287 | 2,043 |
| insertion sort | 1,081 | 724 | 708 | 748 | 788 | 711 |
| fib(20) | 361,253 | 361,254 | 361,254 | 361,254 | 328,416 | 372,199 |

The numbers are more interesting than a table of steady improvements:

- **mem2reg is the big win** — 1.4–1.6× fewer instructions — everywhere except `fib`. In `fib`, the value `n` must survive a recursive call no matter what. At -O0 it lives in a stack slot; at -O1 it lives in a callee-saved register, which the prologue saves and the epilogue restores. Both cost one store and one load per call.
- **-O2 is not always better than -O1.** CSE and if-conversion help the sieve and the sort, but not the Collatz loop. Optimisations are heuristics, and every production compiler has benchmarks that regress.
- **Linear scan costs 5–23%** here, mostly in extra moves around loops, where its interval approximation is weakest.
- **Five registers made `fib` faster.** With no callee-saved registers available, the allocator spills `n` instead. The spill code runs only where it is needed, whereas the -O2 prologue saves `s1` and `s2` even on the leaf path (`n < 2`), which half of all calls take. This is exactly the waste that *shrink-wrapping* (Chapter 14) removes.
- **Scheduling does not change these counts at all.** It reorders instructions without adding or removing any. Its benefit is in cycles on an in-order core, which the scheduling figure of Chapter 16 estimates.

## Things to build next

If you want to keep going, here are projects that fit naturally into kiln’s structure, roughly in order of effort:

1. **A new instruction pattern.** Add RISC-V `Zbb`’s `andn`/`orn` to the grammar in `target/riscv.ts`, with its encodings in `emit/rv64asm.ts` and its semantics in `sim/rv64.ts`. The tiling explorer will show when it fires.
2. **Machine LICM.** Hoist loop-invariant `li` and `lla` instructions into loop preheaders before register allocation, and measure the effect on dynamic instruction counts.
3. **Shrink-wrapping.** Move prologue saves off the paths that don’t need them.
4. **Live-range splitting in linear scan.** Split intervals at block boundaries instead of spilling them entirely (Wimmer and Mössenböck).
5. **Compressed instructions.** Emit RVC encodings for eligible instructions. This interacts with branch relaxation, because instructions become 2 or 4 bytes.
6. **A 32-bit integer type.** Add `i32` to KIR and legalise it on each target: `addw`/`sext.w` on RV64, `w` registers on AArch64, 32-bit operand size on x86 (which zero-extends!).
7. **DWARF CFI.** Emit `.eh_frame` so that `gdb` and profilers can unwind kiln’s frames without frame pointers.
8. **SSA-based register allocation.** Spill until MAXLIVE ≤ K, colour in dominance order on SSA, then destroy SSA afterwards.

The test-suite (`tests/`) differentially tests every example at many configurations. Keep it green and you can change anything with confidence.

## Further reading

- Keith Cooper and Linda Torczon, *Engineering a Compiler* (3rd ed., 2022): the best single textbook for backends; chapters on selection, scheduling and allocation.
- Andrew Appel, *Modern Compiler Implementation in ML/Java/C* (1998): iterated register coalescing, liveness and instruction selection, as implemented here.
- Steven Muchnick, *Advanced Compiler Design and Implementation* (1997): encyclopaedic on machine-level optimisation.
- Fabrice Rastello and Florent Bouchez Tichadou (eds.), *SSA-based Compiler Design* (2022): everything about SSA, including SSA-based register allocation and destruction.
- Jan Hubička et al., *System V AMD64 ABI*; the *RISC-V ELF psABI*; Arm’s *AAPCS64*: the real contracts.
- John Levine, *Linkers and Loaders* (2000), and Ian Lance Taylor’s 20-part blog series on linkers (2007).
- The LLVM source, especially `lib/CodeGen` and `lib/Target/RISCV`. It is large, but it is organised along exactly the lines of this course.
