## 1. Thesis

Hublin ends the chronological series by looking at what *Homo sapiens* did to its environment rather than at its anatomy. His argument is that human impact on the biosphere did not begin with industrialisation or the post-1945 "Great Acceleration". It goes back deep into the Pleistocene: "nous avons commencé doucement, mais … il y a longtemps" (paraphrased: we started slowly, but we started long ago). He builds the case in three layers. **Fauna:** humans are an unusual predator because they target very large prey and prime-age adults. They may have helped thin the East African large-carnivore guild after about 2 Ma. They are the main cause (around 80% in one model) of the late Quaternary megafaunal extinctions, which were most severe where *H. sapiens* arrived in "naïve" faunas (Australia about 50 ka, the Americas about 14–11 ka) and mildest in Africa and tropical Asia, where fauna and hominins had co-evolved. **Landscape:** deliberate fire reshaped vegetation in Australia (Lynch's Crater) and probably opened the forests of Last Glacial Maximum (LGM) western Europe. **Atmosphere:** Ruddiman's "early anthropogenic" hypothesis holds that Holocene agriculture (rice paddies, livestock, forest clearance) pushed CH₄, and perhaps CO₂, upward from about 5–7 ka. Hublin says the methane part is now widely accepted, citing Édouard Bard. He concludes that putting the start of the Anthropocene in 1950 is arbitrary, and that the "golden age" picture of Palaeolithic hunters living in balance with nature is a myth.

## 2. Timestamped outline (lecture)

**[0:00] Introduction: what the Anthropocene is**
- This is the last lecture of the year. Next year he will take thematic angles (brain, energy, diet). Today's subject is human–environment interaction. He introduces Geneste as the "more cheerful" counterpoint: how humans saw animals.
- The Anthropocene is a proposed epoch to follow the Holocene. Hublin calls the Holocene "about ten thousand years"; it is formally 11,700 yr. It follows a Pleistocene of more than 2 Myr (formally 2.58 Myr). The Holocene is the current interglacial that followed MIS 2.
- **Slide:** a sediment core from **west Greenland**, taken where a glacier had retreated. Glacial sediment is overlain by an anomalous organic layer, which illustrates the "**Great Acceleration**": plastic, concrete, aluminium ("new rocks"), carbon particles. The figure is probably from Waters et al. 2016, *Science* (verify).
- The convenient marker is the bomb spike. Trinity was fired on **16 July 1945**, and early tests ran at roughly "one every ten days". Their radionuclides are detectable in sediments.
- Geologists resisted strongly. Apart from human impact there is no stratigraphic or climatic reason to define a new epoch, and the start date is hard to fix (a theme he returns to at the end).
- **Slide:** a biomass bar chart. Ten thousand years ago wild vertebrates made up nearly all terrestrial vertebrate biomass. Today about **97%** is humans plus domestic animals (livestock outweigh humans) and only about **3%** is wild. The chart includes pessimistic projections for 2050.
- **Slide:** cumulative extinctions since the 16th–17th century, steepest for mammals and birds. The question is whether this is a **sixth mass extinction**. Comparisons: the end-Permian ("about half of families" lost) and the end-Cretaceous.

**[0:10] Humans as a peculiar predator; the deep-time carnivore decline**
- He attacks the myth of the Palaeolithic "golden age" of hunters who managed resources wisely.
- Two traits are almost unique among predators: (1) a taste for very large prey; (2) **preferential targeting of prime-age adults**, on land and later in aquatic settings.
- **Slide:** a **ternary mortality diagram** (juvenile / prime / old, after Mary Stiner). A living reindeer population sits at the "living-structure" centre, and natural attritional mortality is skewed to the very young and very old. **Neanderthal prey assemblages from SW France** plot at the prime-adult centre of gravity, and modern humans do the same. Removing breeders hits population dynamics much harder than culling the old or the young.
- The earliest predation signal is about **2 Ma**. Sites with hundreds or thousands of cut-marked and percussion-broken bones appear then. Earlier material "fits in a shoebox for all of Africa" (a recurring joke). He links this to *H. erectus* and the Acheulean.
- **Slide (Werdelin & Lewis):** East African carnivore richness at 3–3.5 Ma compared with about 1.5 Ma. Lost forms include a large **running bear** (probably *Agriotherium*), **giant hyenas** (*Pachycrocuta*) and several **sabre-toothed cats** (*Homotherium*, *Megantereon*, *Dinofelis*), which survived longer in Europe into the Middle Pleistocene. **Small carnivores (under about 20 kg) show no decline; large ones (over 20 kg) crash after 2 Ma.** Climate or prey turnover cannot explain this easily. Werdelin's reading is competition with a new predator, the hominins. Today's lions, cheetahs and leopards are only a remnant of the Pliocene guild.

**[0:20] Megafauna of Australia**
- The "extreme cases" are continents never before touched by hominins.
- **Slide:** reconstructions of the Sahul megafauna: giant kangaroos, **Diprotodon** (rhino-sized; transcribed "protodone"), marsupial carnivores convergent on placentals, giant birds (*Genyornis*) and reptiles (*Megalania*).
- He notes that the debate is emotionally charged ("Rousseauist" against pessimistic views of the First Australians and First Americans).
- First he asks whether humans and megafauna overlapped at all. **Warratyi** (Flinders Ranges, central Australia) has occupation at about **49–46 ka** with **Diprotodon** remains in its lowest levels (Hamm et al. 2016, *Nature*).
- **The natural-causes case (slide):** species richness declines in steps through the Pleistocene, with climate amplitude increasing after about 500 ka (larger glacial–interglacial swings). On this view, the 50 ka losses simply continue an existing trend. This is probably the Wroe et al. 2013 *PNAS* figure (verify).
- **Counter (slide: Lyons et al.):** body-mass histograms on a log₁₀ scale. Earlier Pleistocene extinctions struck all sizes, but the **~50 ka extinctions struck only the largest classes**. Extinctions after European colonisation struck a wide range of sizes ("guns shoot anything that moves").

**[0:27–0:35] Megafauna of the Americas**
- Most peopling happened after about 15–14 ka, on the post-glacial warming slope.
- **Slide (Faith & Surovell 2009):** **35 North American mammal genera** disappear between **13.8 and 11.4 ka**, a sharp "frontier" of last appearances.
- The natural-causes counter-argument is the dramatic end-glacial climate change.
- The human-cause argument is selectivity. Lyons et al. show that two groups were hit disproportionately: (1) **very large animals, especially giant xenarthrans** ("édentés": ground sloths, glyptodonts); (2) **carnivores**. Naïve prey, plus hunters with no technical limits, made the largest prey the most profitable. Carnivores declined through direct killing or competition, "an old story" (back to East Africa at 2 Ma).
- **Archaeological sites, as opposed to palaeontological ones:** two studies (probably Waguespack & Surovell 2003; Surovell & Waguespack 2008) compare predicted prey frequencies by body-size class, corrected for natural abundance ("many more mice than elephants"), with frequencies in Clovis assemblages. They find **over-representation of class 5 (proboscideans etc.)**.
- Mechanism: big-bodied species have slow life histories and low fecundity. If hunters take more prime adults than recruitment replaces, a threshold is crossed and decline becomes "autocatalytic".

**[0:35–0:45] Europe: co-evolved fauna, cave bear, global maps**
- In Europe, with a long hominin presence, losses are less dramatic. They begin with modern-human arrival at about 45 ka "or a bit more" and continue through and at the end of the Upper Palaeolithic.
- Anecdote: **a macaque** found with late Mousterian industry at a site near **Nuremberg**. This is probably Hunas (verify). There are no macaques in Germany after modern humans arrive.
- Upper Palaeolithic fauna had co-evolved with humans and responded less brutally. **Large carnivores** are again the group that ends in extinction.
- **Cave bear case study:** the late form appeared at about the same time as Neanderthals and coexisted with them through several glacial cycles, with occasional Neanderthal predation. People long thought it survived into the Magdalenian, because of cave-bear canines used as ornaments in later levels and bears in Upper Palaeolithic art. But brown and cave bear are hard to tell apart. Combined **aDNA and direct ¹⁴C dates** (Pacher & Stuart 2009; Stiller et al. 2010) showed an earlier extinction. *The transcript drops the conclusion here.* Geneste later quotes "about 25,000 years".
- **Slide (probably Sandom et al. 2014, *Proc. R. Soc. B*):** a world map of the proportion of megafaunal genera lost, in red where losses were high. Losses are high where humans arrived late and where Upper Palaeolithic population density was high, and **modest in Africa and tropical Asia**. This is counter-intuitive, since that is where *H. sapiens* lived longest. His explanation is **co-evolution**: those faunas were adapted to hominin predation.

**[0:50] Quantifying the cause: Bartlett et al.**
- **Slide (Bartlett et al. "2015", i.e. *Ecography* 2016, online 2015):** pie charts of megafaunal loss over the last 80 kyr. Up to **75–80%** was lost in some regions, especially the Americas and Australia, and much of Europe's megafauna went too. Africa kept its elephants and rhinos.
- In the model, which correlates extinctions with human arrival dates and with climate change, **climate alone explains about 20%** of extinctions. The remaining **80%** requires human arrival or humans combined with climate.

**[0:52] Fire and landscapes**
- Fire is underestimated in Palaeolithic studies. **Slide:** a global lightning-density map (NASA satellites, presumably): high in the tropics and Africa, significant in North America, low in Eurasia.
- **Slide (Scherjon et al. 2015, *Current Anthropology*):** ethnographically documented off-site fire use by foragers across biomes (rainforest, savanna, higher latitudes). Uses include driving game, burning savanna to collect killed and partly cooked animals, and **landscape management** (best documented in Australia): raising game productivity and opening forest to improve mobility.
- Controlled fire in Europe dates to about **400–500 ka** (Roebroeks & Villa 2011). Hafting with **heat-prepared birch-bark tar** shows mastery of fire.
- **Lynch's Crater** (NE Queensland): micro-charcoal appears abruptly at about **45–42 ka** and increases into the Holocene. At the same time sclerophyll vegetation increases and rainforest retreats. The open question is whether fire drove the vegetation change, or whether the loss of megaherbivores did (Rule et al. 2012, *Science*: the dung-fungus *Sporormiella* collapses before the charcoal rise).
- **Slide (Kaplan et al. 2016, *PLoS ONE*, published "a few weeks ago"):** model-predicted LGM forest cover in Europe compared with observed palaeo-records. Observed forest is much lower in **western Europe, the most densely populated area**. The conclusion is that Upper Palaeolithic burning produced a mosaic landscape, with supporting micro-charcoal at key sites.

**[1:02] The atmosphere and Ruddiman**
- **Slide:** an ice-core CO₂ curve following glacial cycles. Orbital forcing warms the southern oceans, which release CO₂ and amplify warming, so terminations are abrupt. Earlier interglacials sat around **~250 ppm** (more often quoted as 260–280 ppm); now it is **~400 ppm**, a level he says was last seen in the "Miocene". The usual analogue is the mid-Pliocene.
- **Ruddiman's early anthropogenic hypothesis:** in previous interglacials CO₂ and CH₄ fell steadily after the termination peak. In the Holocene they **rise from the mid-Holocene**, CH₄ from about **5 ka**. Hublin's explanations: forest burning (in Ruddiman this drives CO₂; the transcript links it to methane, possibly a slip), **Asian rice paddies** (CH₄), and **cattle** (CH₄). On this view farming may have stabilised the unusually stable Holocene climate.
- Climatologists criticised the idea harshly. A **2013 *Science* test** (Mitchell et al.) used high-resolution CH₄ from **Antarctic and Greenland ice**. An anthropogenic source should be northern-hemisphere and tropical and widen the **inter-polar gradient**, and that is what the study found.
- CO₂ remains disputed. For CH₄, his Collège de France colleague **Édouard Bard** ("Barr" in the transcript) told him "Ruddiman was probably right and we were wrong not to listen."

**[1:10] Conclusion**
- He accepts a late 20th-century step change (species loss, industrial products, radionuclides). But fixing the boundary there ignores impacts through the Holocene and, through extinctions, before it. He then hands over to Geneste, "very involved in the study of the Chauvet cave".

## 4. Hublin's positions and debates

- **Against the "ecologically noble savage" or Palaeolithic golden age.** He says this explicitly at [0:10]. He also frames the megafauna debate as partly ideological ("Rousseauist" against pessimistic).
- **Overkill and human-driven extinction:** he clearly takes sides. He presents the natural-causes argument fairly (Pleistocene stepwise decline in Australia; end-glacial upheaval in North America) and answers it with **selectivity** (size; xenarthrans; carnivores), timing tied to arrival, archaeological prey choice and Bartlett's 80/20 model. He allows "a combination of both factors in some cases". Opponents are not named; the school includes Wroe, Field, Grayson & Meltzer and Hocknull.
- **Co-evolution explains why Africa was spared.** He anticipates the learner's paradox ("why is the cradle the least affected?").
- **Hominins thinned the carnivore guild at 2 Ma.** He presents this as plausible, following Werdelin, and makes it a running thread: human–carnivore competition "usually ends badly for the other predators".
- **Sympathetic to Ruddiman**, with a careful split: the CO₂ claim is "very disputed" and the CH₄ claim is "probably right". He backs this with an authority anecdote (Bard).
- **Sceptical of a 1950 boundary.** He describes it as convenient (bombs) but conceptually arbitrary.
- **Memorable moves:** the "shoebox" of pre-2 Ma butchered bones; "evolution is above all a history of extinction"; Europeans with guns "shoot at everything that moves" against Pleistocene hunters choosing the giants; "many more mice than elephants"; macaques in Nuremberg; the reassurance that "something will happen after" the end of the course.

## 5. Concepts a learner must master (in prerequisite order)

1. **Geological time units** (era, period, epoch; GSSP): the Anthropocene is an epoch-level proposal and needs a golden spike.
2. **Pleistocene / Holocene, MIS stages, glacial–interglacial cycles**: Holocene = MIS 1; LGM ≈ 21 ka (MIS 2).
3. **Orbital forcing and CO₂ feedback**: insolation starts deglaciation and ocean CO₂ release amplifies it.
4. **Ice-core gas records** (ppm, ppb; Antarctica vs Greenland): these are the archives of past CO₂ and CH₄.
5. **Inter-polar CH₄ gradient**: the difference between Greenland and Antarctic methane shows where sources lie by hemisphere.
6. **Mass extinction / background extinction rate**: what "sixth extinction" means.
7. **Megafauna** (conventionally >44 kg; "class 5" >1,000 kg).
8. **Log body-mass distribution**: how size selectivity is shown.
9. **Life history / r–K continuum**: slow breeders cannot absorb harvesting of adults.
10. **Mortality profiles and ternary diagrams** (juvenile–prime–old; catastrophic vs attritional).
11. **Taphonomy: cut marks, percussion marks**: how we infer hunting or scavenging.
12. **Naïve prey / blitzkrieg (overkill) model** (P. S. Martin).
13. **Carnivore guild and competition**.
14. **Palaeoecological proxies: micro-charcoal, pollen, *Sporormiella***: how fire and herbivore presence are reconstructed.
15. **Radiocarbon and direct dating of bones; aDNA species ID**: how the cave bear's survival was tested.
16. **Early anthropogenic hypothesis** (Ruddiman).
17. **Great Acceleration / bomb spike** as stratigraphic markers.

## 6. Misconceptions

- "Human environmental impact began with the Industrial Revolution." This is the main target of the lecture.
- "Hunter-gatherers lived in harmony with nature."
- "Predators take the weak, old and young." Humans take prime adults.
- "If humans originated in Africa, Africa should have lost the most megafauna." The opposite is true, because of co-evolution.
- "Extinctions at the end of the Ice Age must be climate-driven because the timing coincides." In Australia the timing tracks arrival, not climate. Selectivity argues against random climate culling.
- "Late Pleistocene Europe was a treeless steppe purely because of climate." Fire may have contributed.
- "Bears in Magdalenian art prove cave bears were still alive." They are probably brown bears.
- Learners may also think the Anthropocene is an official epoch. It is not: it was rejected in 2024 (see §7).
- Learners may think 400 ppm CO₂ is a Miocene-like level. The mid-Pliocene (~3 Ma) is the standard analogue, so Hublin's "Miocene" is loose.

## 7. Since 2016

- **Anthropocene rejected.** The Anthropocene Working Group proposed **Crawford Lake (Ontario)** as GSSP with a base in about 1950 (2023). In March 2024 the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy voted it down, and the IUGS upheld the decision. *High confidence.* Cite: Witze 2024, *Nature* news (verify title); McCarthy et al. 2023, *The Anthropocene Review* (verify). Alternative framing: the Anthropocene as a diachronous "event" (Gibbard et al. 2022, *Episodes*; verify). The outcome vindicates Hublin's scepticism about a sharp 1950 line.
- **Biomass:** Bar-On, Phillips & Milo 2018, *PNAS* ("The biomass distribution on Earth"): livestock ~60%, humans ~36%, wild ~4% of mammal biomass. Greenspoon et al. 2023, *PNAS* ("The global biomass of wild mammals"). *High.*
- **Deep-time land use:** Ellis et al. 2021, *PNAS* ("People have shaped most of terrestrial nature for at least 12,000 years"); ArchaeoGLOBE Project 2019, *Science*. Both support the "early start" view. *High.*
- **Body-size downgrading:** Smith, Elliott Smith, Lyons & Payne 2018, *Science* ("Body size downgrading of mammals over the late Quaternary") extends Lyons et al. to the past 125 kyr and across continents. *High.*
- **Hominins and carnivores:** Faurby, Silvestro, Werdelin & Antonelli 2020, *Ecology Letters*: hominin brain expansion predicts East African carnivore extinctions (supports Werdelin). Faith, Rowan, Du & Koch 2018, *Science*: no evidence that hominins caused the Plio-Pleistocene megaherbivore decline. *High that both exist.*
- **Australia:** Saltré et al. 2016, *Nature Communications* (no climate signal in extinctions); van der Kaars et al. 2017, *Nature Communications* (humans rather than climate, Lake Mairs core); Clarkson et al. 2017, *Nature* (Madjedbebe ≥65 ka, contested); Hocknull et al. 2020, *Nature Communications* (environmental deterioration at South Walker Creek, for the climate side). *Medium–high.*
- **Americas:** White Sands footprints at 23–21 ka (Bennett et al. 2021, *Science*; Pigati et al. 2023, *Science*). These push human–megafauna overlap back about 10 kyr before the extinction pulse, which complicates simple blitzkrieg models. *High.* Ancient environmental DNA (Murchie et al. 2021, *Nature Communications*; Wang et al. 2021, *Nature*) suggests some mammoth and horse persisted later than the bones show and points to climate and vegetation roles. *Medium.*
- **Fire:** Thompson et al. 2021, *Science Advances*: anthropogenic fire reorganised the Malawi ecosystem from about 85 ka (verify details). *Medium.* The Kaplan et al. 2016 LGM fire hypothesis is still debated.
- **Methane and Ruddiman:** Ruddiman et al. 2016, *Reviews of Geophysics* ("Late Holocene climate: natural or anthropogenic?"; verify). The CH₄ result is broadly accepted and CO₂ still contested. *Medium.*
- **CO₂** passed about 420–425 ppm in 2023–25 (NOAA). *High.*
- **Cave bear:** Gretzinger et al. 2019, *Scientific Reports*: mitogenomes suggest human pressure contributed to decline (verify). *Medium.*

## 8. Interactive ideas (lecture)

1. **Mortality-profile ternary plot ("who does the predator kill?").** The learner places dots for prey assemblages: wolves, lions, hyenas, Neanderthals in SW France, Upper Palaeolithic hunters. They first *predict* where human hunters fall, then reveal the prime-dominated cluster. Data: published Stiner 1990 values; Discamps & Costamagno 2015 (open in papers).
2. **Predict, then reveal: body-size extinction histograms.** The learner sketches which log-mass bins they think were lost in Australia at about 50 ka, then compares with the actual pattern and with the historic European-era losses. Data: MOM database (Smith et al. 2003, *Ecology*, open) and PHYLACINE (Faurby et al. 2018, open).
3. **Overkill population simulator.** Sliders set harvest rate, age targeting (old/young vs prime) and species life history (mouse, deer, mammoth). The learner discovers the threshold collapse and why proboscideans fall first. No external data is needed, only literature life-history parameters.
4. **"How do we know?": arrival vs climate regression.** On a world map with continental human-arrival dates and % megafauna lost, the learner toggles predictors (arrival, climate change, both) and watches variance explained, reproducing the logic of Bartlett and Sandom. Data: Sandom et al. 2014 supplementary tables (open).
5. **Biomass waffle chart, 10 ka → today → 2050.** The learner guesses the wild-mammal share before the reveal (≈3–4%). Data: Bar-On 2018 and Greenspoon 2023 (open SI).
6. **Core detective: Lynch's Crater.** Stacked pollen, charcoal and *Sporormiella* curves. The learner orders the events and decides whether fire caused the vegetation shift or megafaunal loss did. Data: Rule et al. 2012 SI; Neotoma (check availability).
7. **Ruddiman's test.** Overlay the Holocene CH₄ curve on aligned earlier interglacials (EPICA/Vostok via NOAA NCEI, open). The learner predicts the "natural" trajectory, then sees the divergence after 5 ka, then toggles the Greenland–Antarctic gradient (Mitchell 2013 data, NOAA/USAP-DC).
8. **"Pick the golden spike."** Candidate boundaries: ~50 ka megafaunal extinction, 11.7 ka farming, 5 ka rice and methane, the 1610 Orbis spike (Lewis & Maslin 2015), 1800 industry, 1950 bomb spike. Each card shows the evidence and the stratigraphic usability. The learner votes, then the 2024 SQS outcome is revealed.
9. **The disappearing carnivore guild.** A timeline animation of East African carnivore taxa, filterable by body mass. The learner discovers that only species over 20 kg drop after 2 Ma. Data: Werdelin & Lewis 2013 (open-access *PLoS ONE* SI).

## 9. The seminar: Jean-Michel Geneste, "La représentation du monde animal dans l'art paléolithique"

**Speaker:** Jean-Michel Geneste (the transcript has "Jeuneste/Jeunesse"; the annuaire has "Michel Geneste"), conservateur général du patrimoine at the French Ministry of Culture, based at Les Eyzies-de-Tayac. He is former director of the Centre National de Préhistoire and scientific head of the Chauvet cave research team (2001–2018, succeeding Jean Clottes; dates to verify).

**Thesis.** "Art" is a convenient but anachronistic label. Palaeolithic visual production should be read as a multi-faceted **dialogue between humans and animals**, and studied **in context** (habitat, cave topography, technical chaînes opératoires, bones on the floor) rather than as isolated *objets d'art*. Hunter-gatherers lived *inside* the animal: they ate it, dwelt in its bones, wore its skins, made music with its bones and ornaments from its teeth. Figurative imagery after about 45 ka is the visible tip of a much older aesthetic engagement with natural materials. At Chauvet the bestiary, its placement and the manipulated bones together reveal intersubjective human–animal relations.

**Outline**
- **[0:00] Framing.** Art is polysemous: sign (semiology), socialisation of space and body, technical object (chaîne opératoire), social co-production and "intersubjectivity between humans and non-humans". Proto-aesthetics: **ochre** at Blombos (70–80 ka) and Neanderthal colorants (red ochre and black **manganese oxides**, SW France, 50–60 ka). **Marine shell beads** across Africa (he says ">200 ka"; this is overstated, see below). Work by **Francesco d'Errico and Christopher Henshilwood** ("Derrick et Enchilwood"). He notes the debate over whether figurative art is uniquely sapiens.
- **[0:10] Ornament and materials.** Engraved **ostrich eggshell** (~70 ka; Diepkloof). The aesthetics of Middle Palaeolithic tools (symmetry, long-distance raw materials). Perforated **carnivore teeth** and **shells** in the early Upper Palaeolithic. A large **conch** from a Magdalenian site (almost certainly the Marsoulas shell horn). A fossil-shell necklace from the **Don valley** (~15 ka, far from any coast). **Altamira:** *Patella vulgata* limpets used to grind and hold red ochre, from Breuil and Obermaier's 1924–25 excavations, restudied by **Cuenca-Solana** et al. ("Kwenka"). **Denisova layer 11** (~40 ka) ornaments, with the **green-stone bracelet**, whose attribution both Geneste and Hublin doubt. He then turns to living *with* animals: **mammoth-bone dwellings** of the Russian Plain and Ukraine (**Dobranichivka, Mezhyrich, Mezin**), 7–8 m across and semi-sunken.
- **[0:20] Living in the animal.** Mezin's red-ochre-painted mammoth bones (chevrons, the famous "orchestra"). Mezin's ivory **female/bird figurines**, which can be read as women or birds. The **eyed needle** appears at about **21 ka**, linked to LGM stress and tailored clothing for children (outside Europe it is older; see §7). Antler projectile points with microblade insets. **Horse hyoid pendants** from Cantabria to SW France, the "contours découpés" (classically **Middle Magdalenian**, not Solutrean as the transcript implies). **Bird-bone flutes** (swan, vulture, eagle). **Mal'ta** ivory flying-bird pendants (geese or swans). Ivory as material: beads from rods, statuettes, flexible bracelets. **Sungir** double burial (~30 ka, now ~34 ka cal) with straightened mammoth-ivory spears (~1.8 m). He presents experimental work by **Gennady Khlopachev and Evgeny Girya** (St Petersburg; "Gennagirya"), in the tradition of **Gerasimov and Semenov**: a 2 m, 35 kg tusk is grooved with flint and its strips are straightened.
- **[0:30] Ivory and Chauvet's bears.** Permafrost-soaked ivory is ductile ("you could tie a bow"), then hardens and gains its lustre. A La Madeleine ivory spear-thrower hook (~10 cm) still shows flint facets as if carved in soft wood. **Chauvet:** first use about **36 ka**, with abundant cave-bear bones (the cave bear died out about 25 ka, citing Pacher & Stuart). One **brown bear** skull is identified by frontal morphology. **Slide:** species present as bones (black) against species depicted (red), showing a bone spike for bear but a different set of depicted species. Techniques include finger flutings on soft walls, scraped mammoths (~6 × 2 m), body-contact images, sketchy black outlines over a positive hand, and stump-shaded charcoal. Early Upper Palaeolithic art emphasises **faces and gazes**. **Bears are never alone (always 2–3), mostly red, and never have eyes**, the only species treated this way. They are placed in **low recesses** (the "Diverticule des ours").
- **[0:40] Manipulated bones and the bestiary.** Bones moved, with negative imprints left behind. An accessibility map shows bear images in low, hard-to-reach zones where bears hibernated. **Two wolf atlases** were found placed together, although there are no other wolf bones, only many wolf tracks. Chauvet inventory: **494 graphic entities**, 12 negative hands, 20 dot groups; lions, mammoths and rhinos lead; **17 bears**, aurochs, musk ox, an owl. He asks whether the skull placements and bone manipulations are themselves "art". He argues that compositional complexity grows from entrance to back; the End Chamber panels hold 30–40 figures. The **"Venus and Sorcerer"** pendant combines a female lower body, a bison and a lion, compared with **Willendorf** and **Laussel**. The **Hohlenstein-Stadel Lion-Man** (~40 ka). **Lascaux:** the bird-headed man in the **Shaft scene** (the only explicit hunting/death scene), the "**Unicorn**" (~21 ka), a horse whose forequarters are replaced by dots. The **Trois-Frères "sorcerer"**. **Tuc d'Audoubert** clay bison and the "Salle des talons" (heel prints; "étalon" in the transcript). Superposition and palimpsests: reindeer, then mammoths, then scraping.
- **[0:50] Missing.** From context it probably continued with the history of works over time and a Magdalenian habitation site.
- **[1:00] Magdalenian regionalism and the hidden.** A habitation site whose sculpted frieze shows **ibex and life-size female bodies**. This is almost certainly **Roc-aux-Sorciers (Angles-sur-l'Anglin)**, transcribed "angle sur angle". **Engraved horse incisors** with pubic triangles cut on the enamel fold occur only there and at **La Marche (Lussac-les-Châteaux)**. La Marche also has engraved plaquettes with dozens of "animalised" human profiles. A wolverine, a bear head facing a phallus (La Madeleine?), and three-dimensional bison spear-throwers in antler and ivory show a shared tradition in how eyes are rendered. Fine naturalistic engravings from **Teyjat** ("Teja"), which remind him of the Saharan Neolithic. The **Raymonden (Chancelade)** pendant shows a bison carcass with a fish-bone spine and human figures as ribs, read as mythic narrative close to oral tradition. Public art: Chauvet **intervisibility** studies give 15–20, up to 30, viewers for the big panels, against 2–3 for discreet ones. **Marsoulas** reconstruction (bison, horses and abstract signs). The **Lascaux Apse** palimpsest. Some objects may have been decorated "*not to be seen*", such as a Cantabrian Magdalenian fish-decorated object. **Ethnographic analogy:** **Kwakwaka'wakw** ("Quatiols") harpoon heads in British Columbia, whose thunderbird and orca designs are hidden under resin. Museums in Vancouver and Victoria keep them covered, and access requires the families' permission.

**Interactive ideas (seminar)**
1. **Menu vs bestiary (predict, then reveal).** The learner predicts which species dominate Chauvet's walls, then the bone assemblage (black) and the depictions (red) are overlaid. Data: Chauvet monographs; other caves via published counts.
2. **Where would you paint the bear?** On a Chauvet accessibility map, the learner places species and then sees the real placements (bears in low recesses; complexity increasing towards the back).
3. **Intervisibility calculator:** how many people can see this panel? The learner sees how audience size shapes "public" vs "secret" art.
4. **Ivory chaîne opératoire sequencer:** order the steps from a 35 kg tusk to a 1.8 m Sungir spear, with time estimates (after Khlopachev & Girya).
5. **Palimpsest peeler:** drag layers off a superposed panel (Lascaux Apse style) to reconstruct the order of works.
6. **Body-part matching game:** match ornaments and objects (hyoid contour, perforated canine, swan-bone flute, Mal'ta bird, limpet ochre cup) to their source animal and part.
7. **"Made not to be seen" discussion card,** contrasting Kwakwaka'wakw hidden harpoon art with the Chauvet bear recess.

**How it complements the lecture:** Hublin shows humans *acting on* animals (predation, extinction, fire). Geneste shows humans *thinking with* animals (dwelling, clothing, music, myth). The cave bear links them. Hublin dates its extinction with aDNA and ¹⁴C, and Geneste shows the same animal manipulated and depicted at Chauvet shortly before it vanished. A good course pairing is "the hunter's gaze": prey choice and prey imagery.
