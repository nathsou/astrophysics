## 1. Thesis

Hublin opens by admitting he will "pose more questions than answers". The peopling of the Americas is the last and most contested stage of the sapiens dispersal. He finds it paradoxical that North America, with its dense and well-funded research community, still cannot settle the archaeology of its own territory. His argument has three parts. First, the long-held "Clovis-first" orthodoxy, in which Alaskan hunters walked down an ice-free corridor at about 13.5 ka and founded Clovis, has collapsed. The corridor was not biologically viable until about 12.6 ka, after Clovis was already present. Monte Verde II (Chile, about 14.5 ka cal) is now almost universally accepted. A Pacific coastal route open from about 15 ka is therefore the most reasonable entry. Second, most claims of a much older "pre-Clovis" presence (18–30 ka or more) share one pattern: an undisputed site with something doubtful beneath it in the stratigraphy, whether intrusive artefacts, doubtful dates, geofacts or natural fire. These claims deserve scepticism, and the exotic routes (Solutrean across the Atlantic, transpacific voyages) are close to folklore. Third, genetics now gives a provisional synthesis. Native Americans descend mainly from one wave that left Siberia no earlier than about 23 ka. That founding population already carried 14–40% ancestry from an Upper Palaeolithic population of Siberia related to western Eurasians (the Mal'ta boy). Later, smaller gene flows came from Asia (Inuit-related) and possibly from southern Asia or Australasia. This genetic picture explains the "non-Amerindian" skull shapes (Luzia, Kennewick) without multiple colonisations. His conclusion: the peopling was pre-Clovis, "but probably not as old as some would like".

## 2. Timestamped outline (lecture)

**[0:00] Introduction: Beringia**
- The topic is highly polemical. The consensus route runs from north-east Asia to Alaska across the Bering Strait.
- He shows a map of Beringia at the LGM (about 21 ka), when the land bridge joined eastern Siberia and Alaska. Much of the surrounding land was ice-covered, so Beringia's habitability is uncertain.
- An animation of sea-level rise (it fails, then works) runs through 18 ka, 17 ka and about 14 ka. Conditions improve after the LGM. Travel is easy between about 15 and 10 ka, and the sea separates the continents at about 10 ka.
- Earliest generally accepted Alaskan sites: about 14–15 ka cal, with hints of older presence. He reminds the audience that radiocarbon years and calibrated years differ by about 2,000 years in this period.
- He sets out the recurring pattern: well-dated upper levels with unproblematic assemblages, and deeper, contested levels. The deeper finds are often not lithic assemblages but "traces" such as fire or modified bone.

**[~0:05] The Siberian side: Yana**
- Yana RHS (Yana River, Arctic Siberia), about 31 ka cal. Humans were on the Arctic Ocean shore before the LGM peak, whereas until recently people thought they arrived no earlier than about 14 ka.
- This does not prove humans were in America at 31 ka, but "keep an open mind".

**[~0:07] North American ice sheets**
- A slide shows ice thicknesses over modern skylines: more than 3,000 m over Montreal and about 900 m over Chicago, with Boston and Toronto also shown.
- The ice built up visibly from about 40 ka and grew until the LGM. There are three centres: the Laurentide (largest), the Cordilleran (on the Pacific ranges) and Greenland.
- The Laurentide and Cordilleran sheets merged and separated repeatedly during the Late Pleistocene. When they separated, a corridor opened between them.

**[0:10] Corridors and megafauna**
- The interior ice-free corridor (Laurentide/Cordillera) is placed at about 14–13.5 ka cal. A coastal "passage" between the Pacific and the Cordilleran ice became usable earlier, at about 15 ka cal.
- South of the ice lay a continent with no earlier hominin history and a megafauna that recalls Australia: proboscideans (mammoths, mastodons), large cats, bears and bison, camels, and giant xenarthrans. He shows a Megatherium silhouette next to a human.
- The overkill-versus-climate debate mirrors the Australian one, but here the extinctions date to about 10–11 ka, against about 40–45 ka in Australia. He defers this to the next lecture (L9, Anthropocene).

**[~0:13] Clovis and the point traditions**
- Point-based complexes were recognised from the 1920s. The points are bifacial and pressure-retouched, with a concave base and a "flute" (a long basal removal) for hafting.
- Clovis is named after Clovis, New Mexico. Sites occur across the Plains from Montana to New Mexico and further east. Toolkits also include end-scrapers and burins. The people were mobile, carried non-local raw materials, and hunted proboscideans at butchery sites (mammoth, mastodon) as well as other game.
- Dates are 13,200–12,600 cal BP ("give or take a century or two"), a short-lived complex centred on about 13 ka.
- Later complexes: Folsom, Plainview, Dalton, Cumberland and others, with point typology tracked almost century by century.
- Folsom points are smaller and more deeply fluted. He shows the historic find of a Folsom point lodged between bison ribs, which proved that humans hunted extinct fauna.

**[0:20] The Clovis-first orthodoxy and its critics**
- Alaskan industries after about 14 ka are the Nenana complex (small triangular points, i.e. Chindadn points, plus bifaces) and the Denali complex (the transcript has "Nedali"). There is no true Clovis in Alaska, but many archaeologists see continuity.
- The orthodox model runs as follows: the corridor opens at about 14–13.5 ka, Alaskan hunters pass through, Clovis appears, industries diversify, and humans move rapidly overland into South America.
- Pre-Clovis claims keep surfacing, sometimes at 30 or even 60 ka. If any were genuine, the corridor model would fail, because before about 14 ka there was no land route south.
- **Debra L. Friedkin site (Texas)**: a Clovis level overlies the "Buttermilk Creek Complex", OSL-dated to between 13.2 ka and more than 15 ka (Waters et al. 2011, *Science*). A 2012 *J. Archaeol. Sci.* critique questioned the OSL precision. More importantly, it argued that the lower artefacts are technologically Clovis and had moved down from the level above. Hublin calls this "absolutely typical" of the pre-Clovis debate.
- **Monte Verde (Chile)**: a complex of sites (MV-I, MV-II and neighbours). MV-II is a sealed level at about 14.5 ka cal in a boggy, peaty setting with preserved organics (structures, cordage, hide), transported stone and few classic tools.

**[0:30] South America; the corridor DNA study; the coastal route**
- MV-II is now "almost accepted by all", and he shows an indisputable retouched point. South American industries are highly regionalised (Brazil, Chile, Peru) and show no continuity with Clovis. His reading is rapid dispersal into disconnected favourable patches followed by cultural drift. Human remains give the same impression.
- **"The last nail in the Clovis coffin"**: Danish geneticists sampled lake sediments in British Columbia on the corridor route and reconstructed the environment from sedimentary aDNA. At about 13 ka the corridor was a near-desert with little to eat. Bison and other animal and plant DNA appear only from about 12.6–12.5 ka. Even if Clovis is the oldest complex, its makers cannot have arrived through the corridor (Pedersen et al. 2016, *Nature*).
- The coastal route, usable by about 15 ka, therefore becomes the reasonable option. If the coast opened at 14.7–15 ka, there is some room for modestly pre-Clovis sites.
- Supporting evidence: Monte Verde lies on the Pacific side and requires a fast southward move. Human remains on Santa Rosa Island (Channel Islands, opposite Santa Barbara), directly radiocarbon-dated to about 13 ka, imply watercraft at Clovis time.
- He lists the alternative routes: the corridor, the coast (from Beringia, or from much further west in Asia), Europe, and transpacific voyages.

**[~0:37–0:40] The Solutrean hypothesis**
- Some see Solutrean laurel- and willow-leaf points (France and northern Spain) in Clovis bifaces, which would mean colonists reaching the eastern USA from Europe.
- Cactus Hill (Virginia) has disputed levels claimed at about 18 ka. Mitochondrial haplogroup X2a among Native Americans is linked to European X lineages.
- Getting there would need an Atlantic crossing by boat or a walk along the pack-ice edge from south-west France via southern Greenland and Labrador. Hublin says it is "often cited as something a bit folkloric".

**[0:40] Older pre-Clovis claims; Pedra Furada and capuchins**
- At Monte Verde, MV-I and Chinchihuapi Creek 1 (transcript: "Chin Chinwapu Creek") have recently published objects dated to about 18.5 ka. He shows them because they are unspectacular and draw scepticism.
- The recurring question: if humans were present at 18–30 ka, why are sites so rare and population density so low?
- **Pedra Furada (Piauí, Brazil)**: levels at 11–6 ka are undisputed and come with spectacular rock art. Older levels are contested. He shows a photo of cobble-rich deposits. Level C7 is dated to about 23 ka, and dates go back to about 30 ka. The case for humans rests on "worked" cobbles and hearths. Critics call them geofacts (sort hundreds of cobbles and some will look knapped) and natural fires.
- **Capuchins**: "a few weeks ago" a *Nature* paper showed wild capuchins in Brazil breaking cobbles against cobbles. They lick the siliceous powder and in doing so produce flakes with real striking platforms (Proffitt et al. 2016, October). He shows a quartzite block with conchoidal removals. Cobbles falling from the cliffs above the sites were already one alternative explanation; capuchins add another.

**[~0:46] Human remains: "Paleoamericans"**
- No remains anywhere near 30 ka; the dated remains are mostly younger than 12 ka.
- Some early skulls differ from recent Native Americans. "Luzia" (Brazil) has been described as resembling Africans, Australians or South-East Asians. This led to two-wave models in which a second, non-Siberian source was added.

**[0:50] Drift versus second wave; Lapa do Santo; Kennewick**
- The counter-explanation is genetic drift: small, isolated populations randomly fix odd traits, the same logic he applied to lithic regionalisation.
- A recent genetic paper did find a Brazilian Amerindian affinity with Australasians and South-East Asians (Skoglund et al. 2015).
- **Lapa do Santo (Lagoa Santa, Brazil)** is a site he knows well: a doctoral student from his department works there (André Strauss) and his department dated it. Occupation reaches back about 12.7 ka. Among the burials is the oldest known decapitation in the Americas: a skull with cervical vertebrae and cut marks, buried with two amputated hands placed head-to-toe in a pit.
- **Kennewick Man (Washington State), about 9 ka**: local tribes claimed him as an ancestor for reburial, which made the study highly charged. Early craniometric work judged him "not very Amerindian". Suggested affinities included Europeans and later circum-Pacific groups such as the Ainu. Hublin's point: every time "non-Amerindian" traits are identified, people invent extra waves and complex routes.

**[~0:55] Genetics I: Mal'ta**
- Mal'ta, near Lake Baikal, has Gravettian-like assemblages, bone and ivory objects and "Venus" figurines. A boy's burial with ornaments, beads and ochre was found in the 1920s.
- His genome (2014) is close to western Eurasians in mtDNA, Y chromosome and autosomes. A nearby site confirmed this (Afontova Gora). This points to an Upper Palaeolithic expansion of western-Eurasian-related people into Siberia.
- Second surprise: Native Americans carry 14–40% of their ancestry from the Mal'ta-related population, mixed with an East Asian component close to modern Chinese.

**[1:00] Genetics II: Anzick, Kennewick, synthesis**
- The Mal'ta link "explains" haplogroup X in the Americas and the European-looking traits claimed for Kennewick. (Note for learners: the Mal'ta boy's own mtDNA is U, not X, so this connection is looser than he implies.)
- **Anzick-1 (Montana)**: an infant with a Clovis assemblage. The genome sits near the split between northern and southern Native American branches and already carries Mal'ta-related admixture, so the admixture predates about 12.6 ka. The northern/southern divergence predates Clovis.
- **Kennewick genome**: firmly Native American, closest to one of the claimant tribes (the Colville), which ended the controversy, and the remains were returned. It also shows later East Asian gene flow into Native Americans after Kennewick's time, which explains why ancient skulls look "less Amerindian" than present-day people.
- **Final synthesis figure** (very likely Raghavan et al. 2015, *Science*):
  - one main founding wave, entering eastern Beringia no earlier than about 23 ka (the divergence from Siberians), possibly as late as about 18 ka;
  - inside the Americas, two branches: one found in both North and South America, one only in North America;
  - later gene flows: one from Siberia, perhaps linked to the ancestors of the Inuit in the last few millennia, and a small one from much further south in Asia (Australasian-related), possibly via the coast.
- He calls the conclusion provisional.

## 4. Hublin's positions and debates

- **Against Clovis-first**, which he calls "l'orthodoxie" and which he says is still held by many American archaeologists. He accepts modest pre-Clovis (about 15–14.5 ka) via the coast.
- **Sceptical of deep pre-Clovis** (Pedra Furada, Cactus Hill, Monte Verde I/CH-I, sites claimed at 30–60 ka). His methodological critique has three parts: the stratigraphic pattern (good site, doubtful level beneath), intrusion of artefacts, and equifinality of "traces" (fire, broken cobbles, bone damage). He adds the demographic argument: why would such early populations stay invisible? (White Sands since 2021 has put this argument under real strain.)
- **Dismissive of the Solutrean hypothesis** (associated with Dennis Stanford and Bruce Bradley, whom he does not name) and of transpacific models.
- **Prefers drift and admixture over multiple waves** to explain Luzia and Kennewick morphology. This is a form-versus-genes argument: craniometric "affinities" are unreliable guides to ancestry. Without naming them, he opposes Walter Neves's two-component model and the "Ainu-like" readings of Kennewick (Chatters; Owsley and Jantz).
- **Neutral on overkill versus climate**, but he caricatures the climate camp as "les défenseurs des aborigènes", a sign he leans towards human impact (developed in L9).
- **Rhetorical moves**:
  - The opening paradox: the world's best-staffed archaeology cannot settle its own continent.
  - The skyscraper ice-thickness slide.
  - The Folsom point stuck in bison ribs.
  - The capuchin twist as a lesson in equifinality ("intentionally broken, but by a monkey").
  - "The last nail in the coffin" for the corridor aDNA.
  - "Les archéologues sont des gens créatifs et pleins d'imagination" (ironic, about exotic routes).
  - The Kennewick story as science meeting indigenous rights, where genetics vindicated the tribes.
  - The punchline that Native Americans share genetic ancestry with Europeans.
- **Personal links**: his Leipzig department dated Lapa do Santo, and the doctoral student is from his lab.

## 5. Concepts to master (prerequisite order)

1. **Glacial/interglacial cycles and the LGM**: about 26–19 ka, the most recent maximum of ice volume, with sea level about 120 m lower.
2. **Eustatic sea-level change and land bridges**: lower seas expose continental shelves (Beringia, Sahul, already covered in L6–7).
3. **Ice sheets (Laurentide, Cordilleran) and the ice-free corridor**: the gap between the two ice masses, and the difference between the physical opening and ecological viability.
4. **Radiocarbon and calibration**: raw 14C ages underestimate calendar age by about 2 kyr at 13–15 ka, so always check "cal BP".
5. **OSL dating**: the time since quartz grains were last exposed to light; useful beyond the radiocarbon range, but with larger errors.
6. **Stratigraphic integrity and post-depositional movement**: artefacts can migrate between layers (bioturbation, trampling).
7. **Geofacts and equifinality**: natural processes (and animals) can mimic human flaking and fire.
8. **Bifacial fluted points, pressure flaking, hafting**: the Clovis and Folsom typology.
9. **Archaeological "complex" or "culture" naming**: named after type sites; a technological category, not a people.
10. **Megafauna and overkill**.
11. **Genetic drift and founder effects**: small isolated groups fix random traits, in morphology as in culture.
12. **Haplogroups (mtDNA, Y) versus autosomal ancestry**.
13. **Admixture proportions and population splits (aDNA)**: e.g. 14–40% Mal'ta-related, divergence no earlier than 23 ka.
14. **Ancient North Eurasians (ANE)**: the Mal'ta-related population (the term postdates the lecture slightly in popular use).
15. **Beringian standstill hypothesis**: a pause of several thousand years in Beringia between the split from Siberians and entry into the Americas (implied by his "they stayed there perhaps a few thousand years").
16. **NAGPRA and the ethics of ancient remains** (Kennewick).

## 6. Misconceptions

- "Humans walked across the Bering land bridge and straight down an ice-free corridor." The corridor opened late and was barren until about 12.6 ka.
- "Clovis people were the first Americans." Monte Verde II predates Clovis.
- "The Bering land bridge was a narrow bridge." It was a subcontinent (Beringia) and possibly a refugium.
- "The oldest date wins." Calibrated against uncalibrated, stratigraphy, and artefact authenticity all matter.
- "A broken cobble or a hearth proves humans." There are geofacts, natural fires and capuchins.
- "Skulls that don't look like modern Native Americans mean a different migration." This can be drift, or later gene flow altering modern populations (the Kennewick genome).
- "Native Americans are simply transplanted East Asians." They carry a substantial ANE (western-Eurasian-related) component.
- "Haplogroup X proves a European (Solutrean) origin." X2a is explained by an Asian or Beringian source; no European signal.
- "The Inuit are descendants of the first Americans." They come from a separate, much later wave (annuaire).
- Possible learner confusion: Hublin says the coast was usable "around 15 ka" and the corridor at "14–13.5 ka". The *physical* corridor and the *habitable* corridor (12.6 ka) are different things.

## 8. Interactive ideas (lecture)

1. **Beringia sea-level slider (predict, then reveal).** The learner drags time from 30 ka to 8 ka and watches sea level, the land bridge, and the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice (coast open, corridor open, corridor viable). First they predict the date at which a walker could reach Texas by each route. Data: open ice-sheet reconstructions (Dalton et al. 2020 North American ice margins, open in the *Quaternary Science Reviews* supplement) and sea-level curves (verify licence). Overlay site pins (Yana, Swan Point, Monte Verde, Friedkin, White Sands, Clovis) so the learner sees which ones are "impossible" under each route.
2. **Is it a site? (how do we know?).** For each case (Friedkin, Monte Verde II, Pedra Furada C7, CH-I, White Sands), the learner gets an evidence card: dating method, stratigraphic context, artefact type, critique. They score it on a rubric (dating secure? context sealed? artefacts unambiguous? replicated?), then compare their score with the field's verdict and its 2026 status.
3. **Geofact, human or capuchin?** A blind image sort of flaked cobbles: knapped by humans, broken by capuchins (Proffitt 2016 has open figures), or naturally fractured. Reveal the source and the diagnostic features (bulbs, platform preparation, patterned removals). This teaches equifinality.
4. **Corridor menu from sedimentary DNA.** A timeline of DNA taxa recovered from the BC lake cores (steppe plants, bison, mammoth, elk, fish); based on Pedersen et al. 2016 figures. The learner decides at which point a hunter-gatherer band could survive the trip, then compares with Clovis dates.
5. **Calibration trap.** The learner is given a raw 14C date (e.g. 11,000 14C BP for Clovis) and must place it against Monte Verde's 14.5 ka cal. Then they calibrate it with an embedded IntCal20 curve (open data) and watch the order of events change.
6. **Build the Native American family tree.** The learner drags populations (Mal'ta MA-1, Han Chinese, Ancient Beringian USR1, Anzick-1, Kennewick, Karitiana/Surui, Athabascan, Inuit) onto a tree with admixture arrows, then checks against the Raghavan 2015 / Moreno-Mayar 2018 graph. A slider for "% ANE" shows how 14–40% arises.
7. **Skull versus genome (predict, then reveal).** Show the Kennewick craniometric "affinity" results (Ainu, Polynesian, European) and ask the learner to predict the genome's answer. Reveal the Colville affinity. Discuss drift and why morphology misleads at population level.
8. **Clovis point anatomy.** A 3D or annotated point (fluting, pressure flakes, concave base) next to Folsom and a Solutrean laurel leaf. The learner identifies the differences that undercut the Solutrean claim (fluting is unique to the Americas; Solutrean is about 5,000 years older and an ocean away).
9. **Why so few sites? A demography toy.** Population density and duration set the expected number of discovered sites, which shows why a sparse early population could be archaeologically invisible. This tests Hublin's "why didn't they show up earlier?" argument against White Sands.

## 9. The seminar: Nicolas Zwyns (UC Davis), "La dispersion de l'homme moderne en Eurasie septentrionale"

The transcript spells his name "Zwins" and the annuaire "Zwynns"; the correct spelling is **Nicolas Zwyns**. He is a Belgian archaeologist at UC Davis who did his PhD in Hublin's Leipzig department and works on the Initial Upper Palaeolithic of Mongolia and Siberia (Tolbor). It was his name day (6 December, St Nicolas). His slides were in English and he spoke in French.

**Thesis.** The northern route through the Eurasian steppe belt was used repeatedly: by Neanderthals reaching the Altai, by Denisovans, and by modern humans. In contrast to the poorly documented southern route, it is rich in fossils, archaeology and DNA. Modern humans with the Initial Upper Palaeolithic (IUP), a distinctive and complex blade technology that can be traced like Clovis, spread from the Altai to Baikal, Mongolia and northern China at about 45 ka during a warm interstadial (GI-12). This pioneer wave failed: Ust'-Ishim left no descendants. A second wave with the Early Upper Palaeolithic at about 40–35 ka (around GI-8; Tianyuan) succeeded and gave rise to East Asians. Climate-driven pulses of expansion and local extinction, rather than the LGM, may be the key threshold. This sets up the Arctic and Beringian presence (Sopochnaya Karga 45 ka, Yana 31 ka) that precedes the Americas.
