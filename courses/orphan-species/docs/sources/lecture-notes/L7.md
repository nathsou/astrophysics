## 1. Thesis of the lecture

Hublin treats Australia (Sahul) as the first case in the series of a **landmass that no hominin had ever lived on**. *Homo sapiens* had to colonise it by sea, and the arrival immediately transformed an isolated, endemic, very dry continent. He argues four things:
- Colonisation happened **around 45–50 ka** ("plus près de 50 000 que de 40 000"). It required real seafaring (at least about 80 km of open water even at lowest sea level), and people spread fast, reaching the arid centre within a few millennia (Warratyi).
- The arrival coincides with **new fire regimes, vegetation change and megafaunal extinction**. Hublin leans towards a human role in that extinction.
- The first Australians had a culturally sophisticated package from the start: ochre, bone tools, edge-ground axes tens of millennia before the European Neolithic, burial with ochre, the world's oldest known cremation, and rock art. He thinks this package was carried out of Africa rather than reinvented locally.
- Australian fossils were long the **flagship evidence for multiregionalism** (Weidenreich, Thorne, Wolpoff). That evidence falls apart once you take into account dating problems, contaminated ancient DNA, **artificial cranial deformation** (Peter Brown) and the full range of variation in late Pleistocene samples. What really sets Sahul populations apart biologically is not *H. erectus* ancestry but the **highest Denisovan (plus Neanderthal) admixture of any living people**.

He ends with the Holocene: population growth, the dingo, and an epilogue on the much later Austronesian expansion across the Pacific.

## 2. Timestamped outline (lecture)

**[0:00] Framing: a new kind of colonisation**
- Recap of L6: moderns at high latitudes about 45 ka (western Siberia, eastern Europe possibly 48–45 ka).
- Australia, like the Americas later, had no long hominin history. A continent "inhabité par l'homme" for the first time.
- The colonisation of **Sahul** (Australia + New Guinea + Tasmania, transcribed "Saoul") was necessarily maritime. Even at Last Glacial Maximum (LGM) lowstand, open sea separated it from the Indonesian islands.
- Two candidate routes, likely both used. (a) The southern chain: Java, Bali, Lombok ("Bangkok" in the transcript), down to Timor, with at least about 80 km of salt water. (b) A northern chain from Sulawesi ("Célèbes") to New Guinea.
- Probable slide: globe centred on Australia showing its oceanic isolation, then a Sunda/Sahul palaeogeographic map.

**[0:05] Deep-time isolation and endemism**
- Gondwana break-up in the Mesozoic. South America, Antarctica and Australia separated, and marsupials and monotremes flourished in the south. Antarctica's drift was "funeste" for its mammals. South America lost many marsupials after the American interchange.
- Pleistocene megafauna occupying placental-like niches. **Diprotodon** (rhinoceros-sized). A "small lion"-sized carnivore (*Thylacoleo*). Kangaroos about 2 m tall. A giant varanid he gives as "7–8 m" (*Varanus priscus*/"Megalania"). Giant flightless birds (*Genyornis*). The thylacine, recently extinct.
- Sets up the question for the rest of the lecture: did human predation cause the megafaunal extinction?

**[0:10] Flattest, driest inhabited continent; water and fire**
- About a quarter of Australia is desert (Fitzsimmons later says 50%). Tropical north, temperate south. The LGM was even more arid than today.
- A "very recent study" (= **Bird, O'Grady & Ulm 2016, PNAS**) found that most archaeological sites lie close to water. Hypothesised routes skirt the desert along the west coast, loop around to the south, then move inland.
- Aboriginal **firestick farming**. The chicken-and-egg question: did burning change vegetation and doom the fauna, or did removing herbivores change the vegetation and fuel the fires?
- He shows a core (almost certainly **Lynch's Crater**, Queensland). Micro-charcoal appears and rises from about 43–41 ka, and the vegetation shifts to sclerophyll taxa.
- **Tasmania**: he thinks it was isolated at first arrival (about 50 ka) and joined later. The vegetation change there differs, and *Protemnodon anak* (giant kangaroo) survived later than on the mainland (cf. Turney et al. 2008, PNAS).
- Methodological point: finding megafauna *in association with* the earliest human traces is the key argument against "they were already extinct when people arrived".

**[0:17] Where did the Australians come from? Genetics, 2016**
- Model 1: an early, separate southern-route dispersal along the northern Indian Ocean rim, with Eurasians splitting later. He attributes it to "l'équipe de Villarslève" = Eske Willerslev's group, **Malaspinas et al. 2016, Nature**. In that paper Australo-Papuans split from the Eurasian lineage early, then a Denisovan introgression follows.
- Model 2, published in the same *Nature* issue: "Malik" = **Mallick et al. 2016** (Simons Genome Diversity Project). Here Papuans and Australians fall *within* an Asian clade, so Asia was peopled first and Sahul colonised from East Asia.
- Reconciliation: perhaps an older modern lineage was absorbed by later arrivals from Asia (this is close to **Pagani et al. 2016**'s "≥2% xOoA" signal, also in that issue; Hublin does not name it).

**[0:22] Homo floresiensis and the Liang Bua re-dating**
- On the way, moderns met Denisovans and also a surviving *H. erectus* offshoot. Hublin sees *H. floresiensis* as a late, dwarfed *erectus*. Remains come from **Liang Bua** ("Liangbois"), Flores.
- The original dates went down to about 18 ka, which he and others found implausible: moderns would have had to leave "ces petits hobbits" alone for tens of millennia.
- **Sutikna et al. 2016 (Nature)**: complex stratigraphy, with erosional channels later filled by younger sediment. The young charcoal came from the infill. The youngest dates for the *floresiensis*-bearing deposits are about 46 ka in his reading (the paper says skeletal remains about 100–60 ka and artefacts to about 50 ka). So any modern-caused demise falls around 45–50 ka.

**[0:25] Population history from radiocarbon dates as data**
- He shows a summed-probability curve of ¹⁴C dates from archaeological sites (very likely **A. N. Williams 2013, Proc. R. Soc. B**). Population stayed low for a long time and dipped around the LGM (aridity). Growth took off around 14 ka in his spoken account and especially in the Holocene. The annuaire says strong growth from about 5 ka.

**[0:27] How old is human Australia? Sites**
- The debate history: long thought to be under 25 ka, then claims of 60 ka and more. Today's consensus is 40–50 ka, nearer 50.
- Map of sites over 40 ka: several in the far north; **Huon Peninsula** ("Yonne"), New Guinea, opposite New Britain; western Australia; and a cluster in the south (the **Willandra Lakes**, red dot). He hands over to Fitzsimmons here.
- **Malakunanja II** (Arnhem Land rock shelter): OSL combined with ¹⁴C gives dates beyond 50 ka. Contested, but he finds the dataset increasingly convincing.
- Lithics look "frustes" (flakes) partly because of the raw materials (quartzite, silcrete), yet the associated technology is in some respects more advanced than the European Upper Palaeolithic.

**[0:31] Warratyi: the arid interior by about 49–46 ka**
- "Waraki" = **Warratyi** rock shelter, Flinders Ranges (**Hamm et al. 2016, Nature**, published "a few weeks" before the lecture). Central-ish and arid, 49–46 ka.
- Finds: ochre, worked gypsum (pigment), a bone tool, backed/geometric microliths. Each is about 10 kyr older than previously thought.
- In the lowest levels, artefacts and ochre are associated with **Diprotodon** remains, which supports a human role in its extinction soon after.

**[0:34] Ground-edge axes and rock art**
- Pecking and grinding to make axes. In Europe "pierre polie = Néolithique", but in Australia a ground-axe fragment dated beyond 40 ka has just been published (= **Hiscock et al. 2016, Australian Archaeology**, Carpenter's Gap 1, Kimberley, about 44–49 ka). He expects confirmation.
- Rock art: dating is hard. **U-series on calcite crusts** overlying hand stencils at **Sulawesi** (Maros, **Aubert et al. 2014, Nature**) gives about 40 ka for the oldest, plus figurative animals. This is comparable to Chauvet and Aurignacian figurative art.
- His inference: either independent invention or, more probably, the art tradition was part of the moderns' baggage at or before the exit from Africa, and preservation explains the gap.

**[0:39] Human remains and multiregionalism**
- He shows **Weidenreich (1943)**: a late *H. erectus* from **Ngandong** (Solo River, Java) beside an Aboriginal skull whose face was painted out with gouache so that only the vault shows.
- Wolpoff is still a defender. **Alan Thorne** (whom Hublin knew; died 2012) championed continuity from Javanese *erectus* to Aboriginal Australians.
- Willandra Lakes: dry lakes with crescentic dunes (lunettes). He shows Fitzsimmons' lake-level curve with sites and fossils placed on it.
- **Mungo 3 (LM3)**: he gives about 42–43 ka; others claimed about 60 ka. **Mungo 1 (LM1)**: he gives about 25 ka. **WLH 50** ("Willandra Lake Promenade 50" in the transcript = Willandra Lakes Hominid 50): age unresolved.
- **LM1**: young, probably female, **the oldest known cremation used as a funerary practice**.
- **LM3**: near-complete burial covered in red ochre, found in the 1970s. Remains returned to Aboriginal custodians. In Europe at that time there were hardly any burials; he cites Kostenki ("l'Origination" = Aurignacien), which is much younger.
- **LM3 mtDNA** (Adcock et al. 2001) seemed to show a lineage older than all living ones, which Thorne and Wolpoff used for multiregionalism. It is now attributed to poor preservation or contamination.

**[0:46] Thorne's two-population (gracile and robust) model, and Brown's critique**
- Gracile, Asian-like forms: LM1, **Keilor** ("Keylor", terminal Pleistocene), compared with **Liujiang** ("Thorne de Yudjiang" is probably "crâne de Liujiang", age uncertain).
- Robust forms with supposed Javanese *erectus* ancestry: WLH 50 (flat frontal, thick vault, strong postorbital constriction, occipital torus), **Kow Swamp** ("Co-Swamp"/"Cauchemps"), **Nacurrie** ("Nakuri"). He recalls Weidenreich's comparison with Ngandong/Sambungmacan ("Sangbumachan").
- Dating problems: ¹⁴C on bone risks contamination, and OSL dates the sediment rather than the bone. A burial cuts into older sediment, so OSL ages of the fill are maxima. Probable age is terminal Pleistocene, during the waning of the LGM.
- **Peter Brown** ("Brown"/"Brandt") used metric analysis. The flat frontals are **artificial cranial deformation** from infant head-binding, known in South America and Europe and also historically in Sahul (photo: New Britain, early 20th century).
- Anatomy of the deformation: it flattens the frontal but makes the parietal *more* convex and flattens the occipital. That is the *opposite* of the *erectus* condition, where the occipital is short and strongly angled.
- The **Coobool Creek** series ("Cool Bull Creek") spans Kow Swamp to Mungo 1 variability, so there is no need for two source populations.
- Synthesis: one colonisation about 50 ka with an effective population of a few thousand. Then cultural modification, drift and founder effects in contracted regional populations, and possibly adaptation to LGM aridity.

**[0:54] Archaic ancestry and the Holocene**
- He rejects Wolpoff and Thorne, but Sahul populations do carry archaic DNA: **Denisovan** plus Neanderthal. They have the highest combined share of any living group. Probable slide: bar chart, Australians and Papuans with green (Denisovan) and red (Neanderthal) bars, as in Sankararaman et al. 2016 (Current Biology).
- Holocene: population growth and contacts with Southeast Asia. Dingo introduced by people. He says the dingo did not exterminate the (Tasmanian) thylacine, which was shot, but introduced placentals competed with marsupials.

**[1:00] Epilogue: the Austronesian expansion**
- He gives about 3,000 years ago for the start "from Taiwan" as a consequence of the Chinese Neolithic. Austronesian languages from Madagascar to Rapa Nui. Map in centuries: Hawai'i and Easter Island about 1,000 years ago.
- **Lapita** red pottery. The old model was a pause in Melanesia with mixing, then eastward expansion. Two "few weeks old" studies (one is certainly **Skoglund et al. 2016, Nature**: Lapita-era individuals from Vanuatu and Tonga with almost no Papuan ancestry) show a rapid, unmixed spread, with Papuan admixture arriving later.

## 4. Hublin's positions and debates

- **Chronology: moderate "short-long" position.** He rejects both the old under-25 ka view and claims of 60+ ka. He is sceptical of the oldest dates "comme toujours", yet warming to Malakunanja's over-50 ka. Within a year Clarkson et al. (2017) pushed this very site to about 65 ka. That is worth pointing out to learners as a live test of his stated consensus.
- **Megafauna: humans largely responsible.** "Dans beaucoup de cas il semble que ce soit bien l'homme". He accepts that some taxa went extinct before humans arrived. He frames the question as a matter of association (Warratyi Diprotodon) and refugia (Tasmania). He leaves open the order of fire versus herbivore loss.
- **Against multiregionalism.** He is explicit: "en tout cas moi je la rejette". He dismantles it in four steps: (1) dating of the robust crania is weak and they are late, not early; (2) the LM3 aDNA is contaminated; (3) the flat frontals are cranial deformation (Brown); (4) the variation fits within one population (Coobool Creek). He is courteous to Thorne ("que j'ai eu le plaisir de connaître").
- **Genetic routes.** He presents Malaspinas vs Mallick as contradictory and proposes absorption of an earlier wave as a bridge. He is balanced and does not commit.
- **Floresiensis.** He feels vindicated: "on avait raison d'avoir des doutes". He uses ecological plausibility ("laissé tranquilles ces petits hobbits") as an argument.
- **Art as Out-of-Africa baggage.** He prefers single origin plus loss through poor preservation over independent invention, while admitting this "restera peut-être ouverte pour toujours".
- **Rhetorical moves.** Primary-school memory ("pierre polie = Néolithique… j'espère qu'on l'apprend encore") to overturn a schoolbook equation. The Weidenreich gouache image as an example of how a figure can build a theory. "Que d'eau, que d'eau, que d'eau" for isolation. The "hobbits" quip. A caution on the ethics and data loss of repatriation ("regardez bien cette photo"). The political history (Thorne, the races) is touched on lightly.
- **Repeated theme across the series**: burial disturbs sediment, so OSL dates of grave fill are maxima. This links to the LM3, Kow Swamp and Skhul/Qafzeh dating debates.

## 5. Concepts a learner must master (prerequisite order)

1. **Glacial/interglacial cycles and sea level**: lowstand about −120 m at the LGM (about 26–19 ka). Exposes the continental shelves.
2. **Sunda vs Sahul vs Wallacea**: the two Pleistocene shelves and the island zone between them, which never had a land bridge.
3. **Wallace and Weber (also Lydekker) lines**: biogeographic boundaries tracking deep water.
4. **Endemism and Gondwana**: isolation yields marsupial and monotreme faunas.
5. **Megafauna**: animals over about 45 kg. Late Quaternary extinction debate (overkill vs climate vs fire/habitat).
6. **Proxies in cores**: pollen (vegetation), micro-charcoal (fire), *Sporormiella* dung fungus (herbivore biomass).
7. **Radiocarbon limits**: about 50 ka ceiling, contamination of old samples, calibrated vs uncalibrated ages.
8. **Luminescence (OSL/TL) dating**: time since sediment was last exposed to light. Age = equivalent dose ÷ dose rate. Single-grain analysis and partial bleaching.
9. **U-series on carbonate crusts**: gives *minimum* ages for underlying art.
10. **Stratigraphy and unconformities, intrusive features (graves, channels)**: why dates can be misattributed (Liang Bua, burial fills).
11. **Summed-probability "dates as data"** demography and its taphonomic bias.
12. **Multiregional vs Recent African Origin** (from L1). Regional continuity traits.
13. **Artificial cranial deformation**: fronto-occipital binding and its morphological signature.
14. **Effective population size, founder effect, drift.**
15. **Archaic introgression**: Denisovan vs Neanderthal ancestry and how percentages are estimated.
16. **Austronesian expansion, Lapita**: linguistic plus archaeological plus genetic triangulation.

## 6. Misconceptions

- "Australia was reached by a land bridge at glacial lowstand." No: Wallacea always required sea crossings (about 80–90 km at minimum).
- "Aboriginal people arrived only a few thousand years ago." This was the 1960s official view (seminar), overturned by Mungo.
- "Ground stone = Neolithic = farming." Australian ground-edge axes predate this by about 40 kyr.
- "Simple-looking stone tools mean simple people." Raw material constrains form, and the bone, pigment, art and seafaring evidence says otherwise.
- "Robust Australian crania show *H. erectus* ancestry." Deformation and in-population variation explain them.
- "Ancient DNA results are self-validating." The LM3 mtDNA case shows contamination.
- "The dingo killed the thylacine." Mainland extinction (about 3.2 ka) remains debated (dingo, human intensification, climate). The Tasmanian population, where there were no dingoes, was destroyed by bounty hunting (last captive died 1936). Hublin compresses this.
- "Megafaunal extinction = one event, one cause." It may have been staggered and regionally variable.
- "OSL dates the bone/artefact." It dates the sediment's last light exposure.
- "Polynesians are a Melanesian–Asian mix from the start." The earliest Lapita people had little Papuan ancestry.

## 7. Since 2016 (relevant to this lecture)

- **Madjedbebe (= Malakunanja II) at about 65 ka.** Clarkson et al. 2017, *Nature* 547:306–310. Critiques: O'Connell et al. 2018, *PNAS* ("When did Homo sapiens first reach Southeast Asia and Sahul?") argue ≤50 ka. Confidence that the debate is unresolved: high.
- **Neanderthal admixture dating** constrains Sahul arrival. Iasi et al. 2024, *Science* (about 47 ka pulse) and Sümer et al. 2025, *Nature* (Ranis/Zlatý kůň genomes, 45–49 ka). If Australo-Papuans carry the same pulse, their ancestors left the Eurasian pool after about 47 ka. That favours Hublin's "nearer 50" and is in tension with 65 ka. High confidence the papers exist; the interpretation is debated. A 2025 mtDNA study arguing for the "long chronology" (about 60 ka, *Science Advances*, Richards/Soares group) exists to my knowledge; **verify**.
- **Sulawesi and Borneo art pushed back**: Aubert et al. 2018 *Nature* (Borneo ≥40 ka); Aubert et al. 2019 *Nature* (hunting scene ≥43.9 ka); Brumm et al. 2021 *Science Advances* (Leang Tedongnge pig ≥45.5 ka); Oktaviana et al. 2024 *Nature* (Leang Karampuang narrative scene ≥51.2 ka). A 2026 *Nature* report of a hand stencil ≥67.8 ka on Muna Island (Oktaviana, Aubert, Brumm et al.): **verify**. This strengthens Hublin's "art in the baggage" claim, and the timing sits awkwardly with ≤50 ka arrival models.
- **Wallacean hominins**: Mata Menge (Flores) fossils about 700 ka (van den Bergh et al. 2016 *Nature*, June 2016, so just before the lecture). Small body size already at 700 ka (Kaifu et al. 2024, *Nature Communications*). *Homo luzonensis* (Détroit et al. 2019, *Nature*). Early Pleistocene tools on Sulawesi ≥1.04 Ma at Calio (Hakim et al. 2025, *Nature*; medium-high confidence). Archaic hominins did cross some Wallacean straits, which complicates "only sapiens crossed".
- **Denisovans**: multiple distinct Denisovan sources in Papuans (Jacobs et al. 2019, *Cell*). The Harbin cranium identified as Denisovan by proteomics/mtDNA (Fu et al. 2025, *Science* and *Cell*) finally gives a Denisovan face for the "who did they meet?" question.
- **LM3 aDNA**: Heupink et al. 2016, *PNAS* (published Jun 2016) showed the 2001 sequence was contamination. Hublin's caution is confirmed. **Repatriation**: LM3 returned to the Willandra in Nov 2017. Reburial of the Mungo ancestors in the national park was agreed in the early 2020s; details **verify**.
- **Warratyi** dates and associations were critiqued (e.g. Hiscock and colleagues, 2017 commentary; **verify** exact citation).
- **Peopling models**: Bradshaw et al. 2019, *Nature Ecology & Evolution* (minimum founding population about 1,300–1,550). Crabtree et al. 2021, *Nature Human Behaviour* ("superhighways" across Sahul). Both develop Bird et al. 2016's water logic (good widget data).
- **Megafauna**: Saltré et al. 2016 *Nature Communications* (no climate signal at extinction); Saltré et al. 2019 *Nature Communications* (staggered SE Australian extinctions); Hocknull et al. 2020 *Nature Communications* (local pre-human extinctions in tropical Queensland). Still contested.
- **Dingo**: earliest reliable dates about 3,300 cal BP (Balme, O'Connor & Fallon 2018, *Scientific Reports*, Madura Cave). High.
- **Pacific**: Skoglund et al. 2016 *Nature*; Lipson et al. 2018 *Nature Communications*; Posth et al. 2018 *Nature Ecology & Evolution* (Papuan ancestry arrived in Vanuatu after Lapita while the language persisted). High.

## 8. Interactive ideas (lecture)

1. **Sea-level slider over Sunda/Sahul (predict, then reveal).** The learner lowers sea level from 0 to −130 m and is first asked "is there a land bridge to Australia at the LGM?". The reveal shows a narrowing gap that never closes. A ruler tool measures the widest gap on each route. A "visibility" toggle shows whether the next island is visible from a hilltop (earth-curvature calculation), contrasting the Wallace and Weber lines. Data: GEBCO bathymetry (open) plus an open sea-level curve (Spratt & Lisiecki 2016, NOAA).
2. **Route-finding with water (how do we know?).** The learner draws a path from the Kimberley or Arnhem Land to Lake Mungo. The widget counts days beyond the water-carrying range and compares the result with Crabtree et al. 2021 superhighways. Data: Geoscience Australia surface-water layers, open DEM.
3. **Chicken or egg in the Lynch's Crater core.** Three proxy curves (dung fungus, charcoal, sclerophyll pollen) start hidden. The learner predicts the order, reveals them one at a time, and decides between "fire first" and "herbivore loss first". Data: Rule et al. 2012 supplement / NOAA Paleo (check availability).
4. **Liang Bua stratigraphy puzzle.** A section with an erosional channel filled by younger sediment. The learner assigns ¹⁴C/OSL samples to strata. Misassigning reproduces the "18 ka hobbit". Built from published Sutikna 2016 figures (schematic).
5. **Dating-method matchmaker.** Cards (LM3 grave fill, Sulawesi stencil under calcite, Kow Swamp bone, Malakunanja sand, Lynch's charcoal) to be matched to ¹⁴C, OSL, U-series or none. Feedback explains minimum vs maximum ages (U-series crust = minimum; burial fill = maximum).
6. **"When did people reach Australia?" timeline of claims.** A scatter of published estimates from 1960s to 2025 (official view a few kyr; Mungo 1969–74; Malakunanja 1990; Bowler 2003; Madjedbebe 2017; admixture 2024–25). The learner places a slider for their own estimate before seeing the spread and the methods behind each.
7. **Skull deformation morpher.** The learner applies a fronto-occipital binding slider to a modern cranium outline and tracks frontal angle, parietal curvature and occipital angle against *H. erectus* (Ngandong) values. Discovery: deformation mimics only one *erectus* trait and inverts the others. Brown vs Thorne in one picture. Data: schematic, or MorphoSource surface scans where licence allows.
8. **Dates-as-data population curve.** The learner plots summed ¹⁴C probabilities from **AustArch** (Williams et al. 2014, open via Archaeology Data Service), then toggles a taphonomic correction and a site-bin option. Watch the LGM dip and the Holocene rise grow or shrink.
9. **Archaic ancestry bar chart (predict, then reveal).** The learner estimates Denisovan % for Europeans, East Asians, Papuans and Philippine Ayta, then sees the real values (Sankararaman 2016; Larena et al. 2021 *Current Biology* for Ayta). Open data: SGDP.

## 9. The seminar: Kathryn Fitzsimmons, "Ce qui embellit le désert, c'est qu'il cache un puits quelque part"

**Speaker**: Kathryn E. Fitzsimmons (transcribed "Catherine Fitzsimons"). Australian geochronologist and geomorphologist, luminescence specialist, then in Hublin's Department of Human Evolution, MPI-EVA Leipzig (leaving "dans quelques jours"; she moved to an independent group at the MPI for Chemistry, Mainz, and later a chair in Germany: **verify**). Trained under Jim Bowler (master's). The title quotes Saint-Exupéry's *Le Petit Prince*. "Professor Oblan" in the transcript = Hublin.

**Thesis.** Lake Mungo is a literal and metaphorical "well": water and habitat in the driest inhabited continent, and fertile ground for ritual (LM3). Only *systematic integration of geological and archaeological evidence*, mapped on foot at landscape scale and dated by luminescence, reveals how people responded to environmental change. Her headline findings: (1) the descent into the LGM was **non-linear**, with a short-lived **mega-lake about 24 ka**; (2) people coped flexibly, still reaching a mega-lake island and foraging stranded fish on drying lake beds.

**Outline**
- **[0:00] Hublin's introduction.** OSL explained (dates sand and feldspar sediments, used for nearly every Australian site). Fitzsimmons as physicist and geographer.
- **[0:02] Context.** Two dispersals from Africa, but only *H. sapiens* reached Sahul. Wallace Line (Bali–Lombok, tens of km, Lombok visible from Bali) vs **Weber Line** ("Viva line"; about 100 km, no land visible). Wallace was Darwin's correspondent. - **[0:21] Lunette geomorphology.** The downwind (eastern) dune is the best archive in a sediment-poor desert. Three states: (1) **full lake**: waves, beach, clean rounded quartz sand lunette; (2) **drying/saline**: exposed clay-and-salt bed, efflorescence makes sand-sized clay pellets and a clay-pellet dune; (3) **post-15 ka dry**: soils (accretionary or in situ). Archaeology is partly eroded and becomes palimpsests. A geologist's caveat: lake *filling* records rainfall about 1,000 km east in the highlands, while people respond to the *local* lake.

- **[0:33] Transects.** Unit thickness varies north to south, which lets her reconstruct wind regimes and synoptic circulation, a step towards quantitative climate input. Microscopy confirms rounded clean quartz for full phases, clay-rich "brownie gunk" for drying and the Arumpo unit.
- **[0:35] Luminescence explained.** ¹⁴C is unsuitable (oxidising, little organic preservation, about 40–50 ka limit, while the record is ≥100 ka). Trapped electrons in crystal defects form a "battery" that light releases as photons. Range about 1 year to about 1 Ma, 5–10% uncertainty. Applications: glacial, cave, colluvial, fans, faults, dunes, shorelines. Mungo has about 50% very bright quartz grains vs about 20% at Lake George (headwaters), "much better than Europe", and is well bleached, so single-grain dating works. (Her physics wording is loose. She says photons are released on sunlight exposure; in fact the signal accumulates from radiation during burial and is read in the lab by light stimulation, while sunlight resets it. Correct this in course text.)
- **[0:38] Chronology.** About 60(?)–42 ka full lake (Lower Mungo unit, LM3). About 42–30 ka oscillating with freshwater pulses. About 30–24 ka little deposition (dry or low), but people were present (palimpsest). **About 24 ka short mega-lake**, so the slide into the LGM was non-linear. About 24–15 ka final drying with pulses. **About 15 ka Willandra Creek stops**: she proposes a tectonic cause (a cm-scale fault offset diverts the creek about 50 km east). After that, reworking of the lunette, with the last about 2 kyr very dry. Wind shifts from southern to northerly to westerly deposition around the LGM. Consistent with large Murray–Darling palaeochannels at the same time.
- **[0:42] Archaeology integrated.** Oldest tools about 45 ka (Lower Mungo). LM3 about 42 ka. **LM1 "around the mega-lake period"** (about 24 ka; this contradicts Bowler et al. 2003, about 40 ka, and is close to Hublin's 25 ka). Pedestrian surveys at 1 m spacing with flags by artefact class. Midden geochemistry for palaeosalinity. GIS integration of stone tools, shell, hearths ("thousands") and bone. Hearths with **terrestrial** fauna (kangaroo, bettong) vs **lacustrine** (fish, shellfish) sort systematically by unit. **Fish and shellfish foraging peaks during LGM drying phases**: stranded fish are easy, so "we're quite lazy people". Footprints link to that foraging.
- **[0:47] Synthesis.** Before 15 ka, human–environment interactions were driven by lake level; after 15 ka by local climate. Did a full lake around LM3 time free up time for ritual? (Open question.) Drying phases with flood pulses were *more* productive than full lakes. Mega-lake: did people reuse boats? No archaeological evidence for boats in Australia between about 50 and 24 ka (coastal evidence drowned). Dense footprint trails on a dry LGM lake floor show desert conditions did not deter people.
- **[0:50] Conclusion.** Mungo was home for ≥42 kyr. Integration is the method. (No Q&A in the transcript.)

**Interactive ideas (seminar)**
1. **Flood the lunette (predict, then reveal).** The learner raises the water level on a Mungo DEM from 70 m to 75 m. Predict: "what happens to the eastern dune?" The island appears. Then place the archaeological finds on it and ask: boats, swimming, or a long walk round? The widget computes the detour distance. Data: NSW LiDAR (ELVIS portal, open) for the Mungo area.
2. **Sediment detective (how do we know?).** Microscope-image cards (rounded quartz, clay pellets, soil) go into a 1 m section. The learner infers lake state for each layer, then adds OSL ages to build the lake-level curve.
3. **OSL "battery" simulator.** Sliders for dose rate and burial time, a "sunlight" button to reset, and partial-bleaching toggles that show the single-grain age distribution spread. This fixes the speaker's loose physics. Bright-grain proportion (Mungo vs Europe) changes the precision.
4. **Counter-intuitive foraging.** Predict whether fish hearths are more common when the lake is full or drying, then reveal the hearth counts by unit.
5. **Two archives, two clocks.** A map linking Mungo lake level to highland rainfall 1,000 km east vs local climate. The learner decides which record answers a "climate" question and which a "human habitat" question.

**How it complements the lecture.** Hublin gives the continental picture and the fossil and multiregional debate. Fitzsimmons zooms into the single most important southern locality he defers to her (LM1, LM3, WLH 50 ages on her curve). She supplies the **method**, luminescence and geomorphology, behind numbers he quotes, and turns "people adapted to aridity" into concrete behaviour: island access, opportunistic fishing, and continued presence through the LGM. Both independently use Lynch's Crater, Bird et al. 2016's water map and the 42 ka Mungo date. They differ on how firmly humans drove megafaunal extinction (she says there is "no direct evidence") and on the desert fraction (a quarter vs 50%).
