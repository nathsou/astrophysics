Book VII begins the arithmetic of the Elements, which continues through Books VIII and IX. Its objects are **numbers**: multitudes of units ([[7.def.2]]), that is, $2, 3, 4, \dots$ The unit $1$ is not a number, and zero does not exist. Euclid draws numbers as line segments, and the figures here draw them as rods marked off in units. But nothing in the book depends on geometry. The arguments use only addition, subtraction, and "measuring": $a$ *measures* $b$ when $b$ is $a$ taken some number of times, that is, $a \mid b$.

The book is self-contained. It does not use Book V's theory of proportion for magnitudes. Instead it defines proportion for numbers from scratch ([[7.def.20]]), in terms of "part" (a divisor) and "parts" (a proper fraction). The propositions fall into a few groups:

- **The Euclidean algorithm** ([[7.1]], [[7.2]], [[7.3]]): repeated subtraction finds the greatest common divisor, or shows there is none but the unit. It is the tool behind almost everything that follows.
- **Fractions and proportion** ([[7.4]]–[[7.19]]): every smaller number is a part or parts of a larger one, and the rules of proportion (alternation, ex aequali, the product of the extremes equals the product of the means, [[7.19]]) are rebuilt for numbers. Multiplication is shown to be commutative ([[7.16]]).
- **Least numbers in a ratio** ([[7.20]]–[[7.22]], [[7.33]]): fractions in lowest terms, and why they are exactly the pairs prime to one another.
- **Coprimality and primes** ([[7.23]]–[[7.32]]), culminating in **Euclid's lemma** ([[7.30]]): a prime that divides a product divides one of the factors. This is the heart of unique factorization, which Euclid never states as such. Next, every number has a prime divisor ([[7.31]]), by an argument of infinite descent.
- **Least common multiples** ([[7.34]]–[[7.39]]), ending with the least number that has given fractional parts, a common denominator.

Book VIII studies continued proportions of numbers (geometric sequences), and Book IX draws the famous conclusions: there are infinitely many primes ([[9.20]]), and even perfect numbers can be built from primes of the form $2^n - 1$ ([[9.36]]).
