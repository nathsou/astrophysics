---
title: Coprimality by repeated subtraction
---
**Statement.** Take two unequal numbers $a > b$ and keep subtracting the smaller from the larger. If the last thing left is the unit $1$, then $a$ and $b$ are prime to one another: $\gcd(a, b) = 1$.

In the figure, @CD is taken from @AB as many times as it fits and leaves @AF; @AF is taken from @CD and leaves @CG; @CG is taken from @AF and leaves the unit @AH. With the default sliders that is $19 = 2\cdot 7 + 5$, $7 = 1 \cdot 5 + 2$, $5 = 2 \cdot 2 + 1$.

**Proof.** Suppose some number $e \ge 2$ (the dashed @E) divides both $a$ and $b$. Everything rests on one rule:
$$e \mid x \ \text{ and } \ e \mid y \implies e \mid x - ky.$$
So $e$ divides $AF = AB - k\cdot CD$, then $CG = CD - k \cdot AF$, then $AH = AF - k \cdot CG = 1$. A number cannot divide the unit, which is smaller than it. So no such $e$ exists, and $a, b$ are prime to one another ([[7.def.12]]).

The remainders go down at every step, and the proof walks down with them: whatever divides the pair before a step divides the pair after it.

:::gap Why is there a separate proposition for the unit?
For Euclid, the unit is not a number ([[7.def.2]]), so "the greatest common measure of 19 and 7" does not exist. The algorithm therefore has two outcomes that he treats in two propositions: it ends at the unit (this one), or it ends at a number that divides the one before it ([[7.2]]). In modern terms both are the same statement, $\gcd = $ the last non-zero remainder, and the case $\gcd = 1$ needs no special treatment.

The proof also takes for granted that the process stops. It does, because the remainders form a strictly decreasing sequence of positive integers. That is the well-ordering of $\mathbb{N}$, which Euclid uses openly only in [[7.31]].
:::

:::code The invariant
Write the loop state as the pair $(x, y)$. Each step replaces it by $(x - y, y)$ or $(x, y - x)$. The invariant is not a number but a set:
$$\operatorname{CD}(x, y) = \{\, e : e \mid x \text{ and } e \mid y \,\}$$
is the same before and after each step. At the end the pair is $(1, 1)$, whose only common divisor is $1$. So that was true at the start as well. The widget in [[7.2]] runs the loop on any pair of numbers.
:::

**In the figure.** The sliders do not set @AB and @CD directly: they set the remainder @CG and how many times each number is taken. The figure then builds @AB and @CD backwards, so that the last remainder is always a unit, as the hypothesis requires.
