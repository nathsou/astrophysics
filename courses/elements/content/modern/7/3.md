---
title: Greatest common divisor of three numbers
---
**Statement.** Given three numbers $a, b, c$ that have a common divisor other than the unit, find $\gcd(a, b, c)$. The answer is
$$\gcd(a, b, c) = \gcd(\gcd(a, b), c).$$

**Proof.** Let $d = \gcd(a, b)$ (@D), found by [[7.2]].

- *If $d \mid c$*, then $d$ is a common divisor of all three. Any common divisor of $a, b, c$ divides $a$ and $b$, so it divides $d$ by the porism of [[7.2]], and cannot be greater than $d$.
- *If $d \nmid c$* (the figure's case), let $e = \gcd(d, c)$ (@E). It exists: anything dividing $a, b, c$ divides $d$ and $c$, so $d$ and $c$ are not prime to one another. Then $e$ divides $d$, hence $a$ and $b$, and it divides $c$. A greater common divisor @F of $a, b, c$ would divide $d$ (porism) and $c$, hence $e$ (porism again), which is impossible.

The porism does all the work: "every common divisor divides the gcd" is what lets the two-number gcd be reused.

:::code A fold
The proposition is the step case of a fold. Euclid stops at three numbers, but the argument works for any list:
```ts
const gcdAll = (xs: number[]) => xs.reduce(gcd); // gcd(gcd(gcd(a, b), c), d) …
```
This only works because $\gcd$ is associative, and that in turn rests on the porism.
:::

**In the figure.** The sliders build $A = D k$, $B = D(k+1)$, and $C = E c$, with $D = E m$ and $c$ prime to $m$. This makes @D the gcd of @A and @B, and guarantees that it never divides @C.
