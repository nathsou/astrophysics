---
title: Adding multiples
---
**Statement.** If $a = mc$ and $d = mf$, and $b = nc$ and $e = nf$, then $a + b$ and $d + e$ are the same multiple of $c$ and $f$:
$$mc + nc = (m + n)\,c.$$
In the figure, @AB $= m\,C$, @BG $= n\,C$, @DE $= m\,F$, @EH $= n\,F$, and the claim is that @AG and @DH are both $(m + n)$ times their unit.

**Proof.** @AB contains $m$ copies of @C and @BG contains $n$; so @AG contains $m + n$ copies. The same count works for @DH and @F. Only the counts matter, and they are the same on both sides.

This is distributivity again, now on the other side: $(m + n)c = mc + nc$. With [[5.1]] and [[5.3]] it gives the arithmetic of integer multiples that the rest of the book needs.
