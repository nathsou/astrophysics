Book V is a self-contained theory of ratio and proportion for **magnitudes**: lengths, areas, volumes, angles, times, anything that can be added and compared with others of its kind. It uses no geometry at all, and no figure in it is more than a row of segments. Its purpose is to make the similarity theory of Book VI, and the areas and volumes of Book XII, valid for magnitudes with no common measure, such as the side and the diagonal of a square.

Before this theory, a proportion $a : b = c : d$ was understood through a common unit: $a$ and $b$ were multiples of one measure, so the ratio was a ratio of whole numbers. That breaks as soon as the ratio is irrational. An ancient scholium credits the new theory to Eudoxus of Cnidus. Its core is one definition, [[5.def.5]]: two ratios are equal when they compare in the same way with every fraction $n/m$. This is essentially the definition of a real number by a Dedekind cut, about 2,200 years before Dedekind. [[5.def.4]] adds the Archimedean property, which rules out infinitely small magnitudes.

The 25 propositions then derive, from these definitions alone, the rules we now treat as ordinary algebra of fractions:

- [[5.1]]–[[5.6]]: arithmetic of integer multiples, $m(a + b) = ma + mb$ and $n(ma) = (nm)a$;
- [[5.7]]–[[5.10]]: ratios respect order, and [[5.11]] proves that "same ratio" is transitive;
- [[5.12]]–[[5.19]]: the manipulations of a proportion: alternation ([[5.16]]), separation ([[5.17]]), composition ([[5.18]]) and subtraction ([[5.19]]);
- [[5.20]]–[[5.23]]: compounding ratios *ex aequali*, the multiplication of ratios ([[5.22]]);
- [[5.24]]–[[5.25]]: addition of ratios with equal consequents, and an inequality.

Read with a modern eye, Book V builds the positive real numbers and their field operations out of magnitudes, while never treating a ratio as a number. It also shows where Euclid's assumptions lie: the existence of a fourth proportional, used without proof in [[5.18]], and the Archimedean property, used in [[5.8]].
