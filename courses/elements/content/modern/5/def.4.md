---
title: Having a ratio (Archimedean property)
---
**Definition.** Magnitudes $a$ and $b$ *have a ratio* to one another when some multiple of each exceeds the other:
$$\exists\, n \ge 1:\ na > b \qquad\text{and}\qquad \exists\, m \ge 1:\ mb > a.$$

This excludes infinitely small and infinitely large magnitudes. Euclid has an example: the "horn angle" between a circle and its tangent is smaller than every rectilineal angle ([[3.16]]), so no multiple of it exceeds a right angle, and it has no ratio to one.

The condition is now called the *Archimedean property*, after Archimedes, who stated it as an assumption. Euclid uses it as a fact, not just a definition: in [[5.8]] he takes a multiple of a small magnitude that exceeds a given one, and the lemma [[10.1]] rests on the same step. Without it the test of [[5.def.5]] would not see infinitesimal differences. In an ordered field with an infinitesimal $\varepsilon > 0$, compare the ratios $(1 + \varepsilon) : 1$ and $(1 + 2\varepsilon) : 1$. For every $m, n$, both $m(1 + \varepsilon)$ and $m(1 + 2\varepsilon)$ exceed $n$ when $m \ge n$ and fall short of it when $m < n$. Def. 5 would declare them the same ratio, and [[5.9]], which concludes $1 + \varepsilon = 1 + 2\varepsilon$, would be false.
