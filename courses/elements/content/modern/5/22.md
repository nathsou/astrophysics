---
title: Ex aequali (multiplication of ratios)
---
**Statement.** If $a : b = d : e$ and $b : c = e : f$, then
$$a : c = d : f,$$
and likewise for any number of terms: from $a_1 : a_2 = b_1 : b_2$, …, $a_{k-1} : a_k = b_{k-1} : b_k$ follows $a_1 : a_k = b_1 : b_k$.

For fractions, $\frac ac = \frac ab \cdot \frac bc$. The proposition says that this product depends only on the two ratios, not on the magnitudes chosen to represent them. It is the well-definedness of multiplication of ratios.

**Proof.** Take equimultiples @G, @H $= mA, mD$, then @K, @L $= nB, nE$ and @M, @N $= pC, pF$. By [[5.4]], $G : K = H : L$ and $K : M = L : N$. So the three magnitudes $G, K, M$ and the three $H, L, N$ satisfy the hypotheses of [[5.20]], which gives: $G \gtrless M$ exactly when $H \gtrless N$. Since $G, H$ are equimultiples of $A, D$ and $M, N$ of $C, F$, this is [[5.def.5]] for $A : C = D : F$. More terms follow by applying the result repeatedly.

:::code A monoid of ratios
Think of a ratio as a type `Ratio` with an equality from [[5.def.5]]. Ex aequali defines `compose(a:b, b:c) = a:c` and proves it respects equality: equal inputs give equal outputs. Composition is associative, and $a : a$ is the identity. Book VI ([[6.23]]) calls the result a *compound ratio*, and uses it for the ratio of two parallelograms. What Euclid lacks is a way to compose two ratios that do not share a middle term: that needs a fourth proportional, the assumption discussed in [[5.18]].
:::
