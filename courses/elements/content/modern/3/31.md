---
title: Thales’ theorem
---
**Statement.** Let @BC be a diameter of a circle with centre @E.
1. The angle in the semicircle is right: $\angle BAC = 90°$ for every @A on the circle.
2. The angle in a segment greater than a semicircle is acute: $\angle ABC < 90°$.
3. The angle in a segment less than a semicircle is obtuse: $\angle ADC > 90°$.
4. The angle *of* the greater segment (between the arc $ABC$ and the chord @AC) is greater than a right angle, and the angle of the less segment is less.

**Proof.** Join @AE. The triangles $ABE$ and $ACE$ are isosceles, so
$$\angle BAE = \angle ABE, \qquad \angle CAE = \angle ACE \quad ([[1.5]]).$$
Adding, $\angle BAC = \angle ABC + \angle ACB$. Extend @BA to @F. The exterior angle $\angle FAC$ also equals $\angle ABC + \angle ACB$ ([[1.32]]). So the adjacent angles $\angle BAC$ and $\angle FAC$ are equal, and each is right ([[1.def.10]]).

The rest follows. In the triangle $ABC$, $\angle ABC < 180° - 90°$ ([[1.17]]), so it is acute. $ABCD$ is a cyclic quadrilateral, so $\angle ADC = 180° - \angle ABC$ ([[3.22]]), which is obtuse. Part 4 compares mixed angles with the right angle $\angle BAC$ and its supplement $\angle CAF$, one containing and the other contained in them.

It is also a special case of [[3.20]]: the angle at the centre on the arc $BC$ is a straight angle, $180°$, and the inscribed angle is half of it. Euclid does not argue this way because his angles are always less than two right angles.

**In the figure.** Drag @A around the semicircle: the angle at @A stays right. Drag @D along the lesser arc: its angle is obtuse, and $\angle ABC + \angle ADC = 180°$. Drag @B to turn the diameter.

:::code A dot product that vanishes
Put the centre at the origin, $B = -u$, $C = u$ and $A = a$ with $|a| = |u| = r$. Then
$$(B - A)\cdot(C - A) = (-u - a)\cdot(u - a) = |a|^2 - |u|^2 = 0.$$
The vectors $AB$ and $AC$ are orthogonal, for every $A$ on the circle. This is the shortest proof there is, and it makes the converse just as clear: $(B-A)\cdot(C-A) = 0$ says $|A - M|^2 = |u|^2$ with $M$ the midpoint of $BC$, so $A$ is on the circle with diameter $BC$.
:::

:::history Thales
Later Greek tradition credits Thales of Miletus (6th century BC) with this theorem; Diogenes Laertius reports, citing Pamphila, that Thales was the first to inscribe a right-angled triangle in a circle. The attribution is late and cannot be checked. In German the theorem is still called *Satz des Thales*.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
The converse (the circle on a hypotenuse passes through the right angle) is the standard way to construct right angles and tangents. I.47's figure uses this proposition to keep its triangle right-angled while you drag. Thales' theorem is the case $\theta = 90°$ of the arcs that [[3.33]] constructs: the locus of points from which a segment is seen at a right angle is the circle on it as diameter.
:::
