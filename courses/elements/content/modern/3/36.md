---
title: Tangent–secant theorem
---
**Statement.** From a point @D outside a circle, let a line cut the circle at @C and @A, and let @DB touch it at @B. Then
$$AD \cdot DC = DB^2.$$

**Proof (the case drawn: @AC not through the centre @E).** Drop the perpendicular @EF to @AC; it bisects @AC ([[3.3]]). The segment @AC is bisected at @F and extended to @D, so by [[2.6]]
$$AD \cdot DC + FC^2 = FD^2.$$
Add $FE^2$ and use Pythagoras ([[1.47]]) twice: $AD \cdot DC + EC^2 = ED^2$. Now $EC = EB$, and the angle at @B is right ([[3.18]]), so $ED^2 = EB^2 + BD^2$. Subtract $EB^2$: $AD \cdot DC = DB^2$.

Euclid first proves the case where the line passes through the centre; there the midpoint of @AC is the centre itself and the second use of Pythagoras is not needed.

So, as in [[3.35]], the product is independent of the line:
$$AD \cdot DC = ED^2 - r^2 = DB^2,$$
the power of @D with respect to the circle (see the note on [[3.35]]).

**In the figure.** Drag @D, or @A to turn the secant. The product $AD \cdot DC$ and the square $DB^2$ stay equal.

:::code A similarity proof
With [[3.32]] there is a proof with no Book II at all: the tangent–chord angle $\angle DBC$ equals the inscribed angle $\angle DAB$, so the triangles $DBC$ and $DAB$ are similar (they share the angle at $D$). Hence $DB : DA = DC : DB$ ([[6.4]]), which is $DB^2 = DA \cdot DC$. Euclid cannot use it here, since similar triangles wait until Book VI.
:::
