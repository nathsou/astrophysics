---
title: Constructing a segment admitting a given angle
---
**Statement.** On a given segment @AB, construct a segment of a circle that admits a given angle @C (every angle in it equals $\angle C$).

**Construction (acute angle, Heath's first figure).** Construct $\angle BAD = \angle C$ at @A ([[1.23]]). Draw @AE perpendicular to @AD, and the perpendicular bisector @FG of @AB, meeting @AE at @G. The circle with centre @G through @A passes through @B (by SAS, $GA = GB$), and @AE is a diameter.

**Proof.** @AD is perpendicular to the diameter @AE at its end, so it touches the circle ([[3.16]], porism). By the tangent–chord theorem ([[3.32]]) the angle between the tangent @AD and the chord @AB equals the angle in the alternate segment, $\angle AEB$. So the segment $AEB$ admits $\angle C$.

The three cases differ only in where the centre falls. For a right angle, @G falls on @F and the segment is a semicircle ([[3.31]]). For an obtuse angle, the centre @G lies on the same side of @AB as @D, and the segment admitting the angle is the lesser one, $AHB$.

In modern language this is the **inscribed angle locus**: the points $P$ on one side of @AB with $\angle APB = \theta$ form an arc of a circle of radius $\frac{AB}{2\sin\theta}$. [[3.23]] says it is unique.

**In the figure.** Move the slider to change the given angle @C and watch the case change: $\angle AEB = \angle C$ while the angle is acute, $\angle AHB = \angle C$ once it is obtuse. Drag @A and @B.
