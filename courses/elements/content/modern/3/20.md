---
title: Inscribed angle theorem
---
**Statement.** In a circle with centre @E, let the angle @angle(BEC) at the centre and the angle @angle(BAC) at the circumference stand on the same arc $BC$. Then
$$\angle BEC = 2\,\angle BAC.$$

**Proof.** Draw the diameter through @A, to @F.
- The triangle $EAB$ is isosceles ($EA = EB$), so $\angle EAB = \angle EBA$ ([[1.5]]).
- The exterior angle @angle(BEF) of that triangle equals the sum of the two opposite interior angles ([[1.32]]), so $\angle BEF = 2\angle EAB$.
- Likewise $\angle FEC = 2\angle EAC$.
- Adding: $\angle BEC = 2\angle BAC$.

If the centre lies outside the angle, as for @D, draw the diameter @DG and *subtract* instead of adding: $\angle GEC - \angle GEB = 2(\angle EDC - \angle EDB)$, which is $\angle BEC = 2\angle BDC$.

The idea is to split the angle along the diameter through its vertex. Each half is the base angle of an isosceles triangle whose apex is the centre, and an exterior angle is twice a base angle.

**In the figure.** Drag @A and @D along the arc. The two inscribed angles stay equal to each other and to half of $\angle BEC$. @A shows the first case (centre inside the angle), @D the second. Drag @B or @C to change the arc.

:::gap Cases Euclid leaves out
Euclid proves the two cases in his figure: the centre inside the angle, and the centre outside it. He omits the case where the centre is on one arm, which is the simplest (one step of the proof). More seriously, he only considers arcs less than a semicircle, because his angles are always less than two right angles. If @A were on the minor arc, the angle at the centre standing on the major arc would be a reflex angle, which Euclid never uses. The theorem is true there too, as $360° - \angle BEC = 2\angle BAC$, and this is what makes [[3.22]] work. Euclid has to prove [[3.22]] by a different route.
:::

:::code An invariant under motion
Read the figure as a program with a loop: move $A$ along the arc; the value $\angle BAC$ does not change. With complex numbers, $A$, $B$, $C$ on the unit circle, the direction of $B - A$ relative to $C - A$ is
$$\arg\frac{C - A}{B - A},$$
and the inscribed angle theorem says this is constant as $A$ moves along one arc (it jumps by $\pi$ when $A$ crosses to the other). Four points are concyclic exactly when the cross-ratio $\frac{(C-A)(D-B)}{(B-A)(D-C)}$ is real, the usual algebraic test for points on a circle.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
Almost all of the rest of Book III follows: angles in the same segment are equal ([[3.21]]), opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary ([[3.22]]), the angle in a semicircle is right ([[3.31]]), and the tangent–chord angle ([[3.32]]). Read backwards, the theorem says that the points from which a segment $BC$ is seen at a fixed angle form two arcs, the locus that [[3.33]] constructs. In Book IV it is the tool for inscribing regular polygons.
:::
