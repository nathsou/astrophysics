---
title: Cyclic quadrilaterals
---
**Statement.** If a quadrilateral $ABCD$ is inscribed in a circle, its opposite angles add up to two right angles:
$$\angle ABC + \angle ADC = 180°, \qquad \angle BAD + \angle BCD = 180°.$$

**Proof.** Draw the diagonals. In the triangle $ABC$,
$$\angle CAB + \angle ABC + \angle BCA = 180° \quad ([[1.32]]).$$
By [[3.21]], $\angle CAB = \angle BDC$ (both stand on the arc $BC$) and $\angle BCA = \angle BDA$ (both stand on $AB$). So $\angle CAB + \angle BCA = \angle BDC + \angle BDA = \angle ADC$. Substituting, $\angle ABC + \angle ADC = 180°$.

With reflex central angles the proof is shorter: the angles at $B$ and $D$ stand on the two arcs $ADC$ and $ABC$, whose central angles add up to $360°$, so the inscribed angles add up to $180°$. Euclid avoids reflex angles and goes through the triangle instead.

**In the figure.** Drag the four vertices around the circle (they must stay in order). Both sums stay at $180°$.

:::leads Where it leads
The converse is also true, though Euclid does not state it: a convex quadrilateral whose opposite angles are supplementary can be inscribed in a circle. It is the standard test for four points to be concyclic. Ptolemy's theorem, $AC \cdot BD = AB \cdot CD + AD \cdot BC$ for a cyclic quadrilateral, is the metric counterpart.
:::
