---
title: The tangent and the horn angle
---
**Statement.** Let @AB be a diameter of a circle with centre @D.
1. The line @AE perpendicular to @AB at @A lies outside the circle (except at @A).
2. No straight line through @A fits between @AE and the circle.
3. So the angle between the diameter and the arc (the "angle of the semicircle") is greater than every acute angle, and the angle between the arc and the tangent (the "horn angle", or angle of contact) is less than every acute angle.

**Porism.** The perpendicular to a diameter at its end touches the circle. This is the tangent construction the rest of the book uses.

**Proof.**
1. If the perpendicular met the circle again at @C, the triangle $DAC$ would be isosceles, so $\angle DCA = \angle DAC$ would be a right angle too ([[1.5]]), and a triangle cannot have two right angles ([[1.17]]).
2. Take any other line @FA through @A, and drop the perpendicular @DG to it. In the triangle $DGA$ the right angle at @G is the largest, so $DA > DG$ ([[1.19]]). So @G is inside the circle, and the line @FA enters it. Nothing lies between the tangent and the circle.
3. An acute angle between the tangent and a line @FA would give a line between the tangent and the arc, which (2) rules out.

**In the figure.** Drag @F as close to the tangent as you like. The foot @G stays inside the circle ($DG < DA$), so @FA always cuts it.

:::gap A magnitude that breaks the Archimedean axiom
If the horn angle is a magnitude, it is a strange one. It is greater than zero (the arc and the tangent are different curves), yet any multiple of it is still less than any acute angle, since the tangent has no line between itself and the circle. That violates the Archimedean property of [[5.def.4]]: two magnitudes have a ratio only if some multiple of each exceeds the other. So horn angles and rectilineal angles have no ratio.

Modern analysis settles it by measuring the angle between two curves at a common point as the angle between their tangent lines. The horn angle is then exactly $0$. The quantity that tells the curves apart is curvature, a second-order quantity: near $A$ the circle departs from its tangent by about $x^2/2r$, which is smaller than any linear $x\tan\theta$ for small $x$. That is exactly what the widget's zoom shows. Ordered fields with infinitesimals (non-Archimedean fields) give a home to such magnitudes.
:::

:::history The quarrel over the angle of contact
The horn angle was discussed by ancient commentators, and became the subject of a long dispute in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Jacques Peletier held that the angle of contact is not an angle at all, while Christopher Clavius defended it as a genuine, though incomparably small, magnitude. John Wallis later wrote a treatise on the angle of contact (1656), siding in substance with Peletier.
:::
