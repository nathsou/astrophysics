---
title: A chord lies inside its circle
---
**Statement.** If @A and @B are on a circle, every point of the segment $AB$ between them lies inside the circle.

**Proof.** Suppose instead that the line from @A to @B falls outside, like the dashed curve through @E. Let @D be the centre ([[3.1]]), and join @DA, @DB, and the line from @D through @F on the circle to @E. The triangle $DAB$ is isosceles, so $\angle DAE = \angle DBE$ ([[1.5]]). In the triangle $DAE$ the exterior angle $\angle DEB$ exceeds $\angle DAE$ ([[1.16]]), hence exceeds $\angle DBE$. The greater angle is opposite the greater side ([[1.19]]), so $DB > DE$. But $DB = DF$, so $DF > DE$, although @F lies between @D and @E. Contradiction. The case where the line lies on the circle is handled the same way.

In modern terms this is the **convexity of the disc**. If $|a|, |b| \le r$ then $|(1-t)a + tb| \le (1-t)|a| + t|b| \le r$, strictly for $0 < t < 1$ when $a \ne b$.

:::gap The picture of an impossible object
Euclid asks us to imagine a *straight* line @AB lying outside the circle, which is impossible to draw. His proof really shows something precise: any point $E$ of the segment $AB$ satisfies $DE < DA$. Hilbert-style treatments prove exactly that, using the fact that in the isosceles triangle the foot of the perpendicular from $D$ is between $A$ and $B$.
:::
