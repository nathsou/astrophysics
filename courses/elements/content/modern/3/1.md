---
title: Finding the centre of a circle
---
**Statement.** Given a circle, construct its centre.

**Construction.** Draw any chord @AB and bisect it at @D ([[1.10]]). Erect the perpendicular to @AB at @D ([[1.11]]) and extend it both ways until it meets the circle at @C and @E. The midpoint @F of @CE is the centre.

**Why it works.** Euclid argues by contradiction. Suppose some point @G off the line @CE were the centre. Then $GA = GB$ (radii), $AD = DB$, and $GD$ is common, so the triangles $ADG$ and $BDG$ are congruent by SSS ([[1.8]]). Their angles at $D$ are equal and adjacent, hence both right ([[1.def.10]]). So both $\angle GDB$ and $\angle FDB$ are right, and one is part of the other, which is impossible. Hence the centre lies on $CE$. A point of $CE$ equidistant from $C$ and $E$ can only be its midpoint.

In modern language: the centre is equidistant from $A$ and $B$, so it lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$. That line is a diameter, and the centre is its midpoint.

:::gap Two small gaps
The argument shows that the centre is not off the line @CE. It does not say why the centre, once on @CE, must be its midpoint @F; this needs the remark that $C$ and $E$ are on the circle, so the centre is equidistant from them. 
The construction also assumes that the perpendicular meets the circle twice, a continuity fact of the kind discussed under [[1.1]].
:::

**In the figure.** Drag @A and @B round the circle: whatever chord you choose, @F lands on the same point. Drag @G: the readout shows that $GA \ne GB$ unless @G is on @CE.

**Porism.** A line that bisects a chord at right angles passes through the centre. This is the form in which [[3.9]] and [[3.10]] use the proposition.
