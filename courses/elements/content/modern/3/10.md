---
title: Two circles meet in at most two points
---
**Statement.** Two distinct circles have at most two points in common.

**Proof.** Suppose the circles $ABC$ and $DEF$ had more than two points in common, say @B, @G, @H (Heath's text also lists @F). Bisect the chords @BH and @BG at @K and @L, and draw the perpendiculars there. By the porism of [[3.1]], the centre of $ABC$ lies on both perpendiculars @AC and @NO, so it is their intersection @P. But the same reasoning applies word for word to the other circle, since @BH and @BG are chords of it too. So @P is the centre of both circles, which cut each other; this contradicts [[3.5]].

The real content: three non-collinear points determine one circle, whose centre is the intersection of two perpendicular bisectors. Two circles with three points in common have the same centre and radius, so they are the same circle.

:::gap The figure of an impossible thing
The figure cannot show two circles cutting in four points, so the dashed curve $DEF$ is an oval: it does meet the circle four times, and its "centre" @P is equidistant from those four points. The readout shows that $PE \ne PB$: $DEF$ is not a circle. Euclid's proof tacitly assumes that three points of a circle are not collinear, so that the perpendicular bisectors meet.
:::

In algebra: subtract the equations $x^2 + y^2 + a_1x + b_1y + c_1 = 0$ and $x^2 + y^2 + a_2x + b_2y + c_2 = 0$. What remains is linear, a line (the radical axis), and a line meets a circle at most twice.
