---
title: Equal angles stand on equal arcs
---
**Statement.** In equal circles $ABC$ and $DEF$ (centres @G, @H), if the angles at the centres $\angle BGC = \angle EHF$, or the angles at the circumferences $\angle BAC = \angle EDF$, are equal, then the arcs they stand on are equal: $\text{arc } BKC = \text{arc } ELF$.

**Proof.** The triangles $BGC$ and $EHF$ have equal radii as sides and equal included angles, so $BC = EF$ by SAS ([[1.4]]). Equal angles at @A and @D make the segments $BAC$ and $EDF$ similar ([[3.def.11]]), and they stand on equal chords, so they are equal ([[3.24]]). Subtract them from the equal whole circles: what remains, the arcs $BKC$ and $ELF$, are equal.

In modern terms: arc length is $r\theta$ for a central angle $\theta$ in radians. Equal radii and equal central angles give equal arcs. Euclid has no measure of arc length; he treats an arc as known once the region it bounds is known, which is why he subtracts segments from whole circles.

**In the figure.** The slider sets the common radius. @F is placed so that $\angle EHF = \angle BGC$; drag @B, @C, @E, @A, @D and watch the arcs and chords stay equal.

The next three propositions complete a set of four equivalences in equal circles: equal central angles, equal inscribed angles, equal arcs, and (for arcs of the same kind) equal chords all imply one another ([[3.27]], [[3.28]], [[3.29]]).
