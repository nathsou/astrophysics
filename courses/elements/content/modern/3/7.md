---
title: Lines from a point on a diameter
---
**Statement.** Let @F be a point on the diameter @AD of a circle with centre @E, other than @E, with @F nearer to @D. Among the segments from @F to the circle:
1. @FA (through the centre) is the longest and @FD the shortest;
2. of two others, the one closer to @FA is longer: $FB > FC > FG$;
3. equal segments come in pairs, one on each side of the diameter: for each @G there is exactly one other point @H with $FH = FG$.

**Proof.**
- $FB < BE + EF = AE + EF = AF$ by the triangle inequality ([[1.20]]).
- In the triangles $BEF$ and $CEF$, two sides are equal ($EB = EC$, $EF$ common), and the included angle at @E is greater for @B. So $BF > CF$ ([[1.24]], the "hinge" theorem).
- $GF + FE > EG = ED$, so subtracting $FE$ gives $GF > FD$.
- Copy the angle $\angle GEF$ on the other side of the diameter to get @H. Then $FG = FH$ by SAS ([[1.4]]). A third point @K with $FK = FG$ would contradict the strict monotonicity of the second step.

In coordinates, with $E$ at the origin, $F = (f, 0)$ and $P = r(\cos t, \sin t)$:
$$|FP|^2 = r^2 + f^2 - 2rf\cos t,$$
a strictly monotonic function of the angle $t \in [0, \pi]$. That one formula contains the whole proposition.

**In the figure.** Drag @F along the diameter, and @B, @C, @G around the circle. The chain of inequalities holds, and $FG = FH$. Drag @K: $FK$ can match $FG$ only when @K lands on @G or @H.
