---
title: Tangent–chord angle
---
**Statement.** If @EF touches a circle at @B and @BD is a chord, the angles between the tangent and the chord equal the angles in the alternate segments:
$$\angle FBD = \angle BAD, \qquad \angle EBD = \angle BCD,$$
where @A is in the segment on the other side of @BD from @F, and @C in the segment on the other side from @E.

**Proof.** Take @A at the end of the diameter through @B. This is allowed because all angles in a segment are equal ([[3.21]]), and it simplifies everything. The perpendicular to the tangent at @B passes through the centre ([[3.19]]), so @BA is a diameter and $\angle ADB = 90°$ ([[3.31]]). In the triangle $ABD$ the other two angles add up to one right angle ([[1.32]]): $\angle BAD + \angle ABD = 90° = \angle ABF$. Subtract $\angle ABD$: $\angle DBF = \angle BAD$.

For the other angle, $ABCD$ is a cyclic quadrilateral, so $\angle BAD + \angle BCD = 180° = \angle DBF + \angle DBE$ ([[3.22]], [[1.13]]). Subtract the equal angles $\angle BAD = \angle DBF$: $\angle DBE = \angle BCD$.

It is the limiting case of [[3.21]]: as a point of the circle slides towards @B, the chord from it to @B turns into the tangent, and the inscribed angle keeps its value.

**In the figure.** Drag @D and @B around the circle and @C along its arc. Both pairs of angles stay equal.

:::leads Where it leads
[[3.33]] and [[3.34]] use this to construct segments admitting a given angle, and [[4.2]] uses it to inscribe a triangle of given shape in a circle. It also gives a short proof of [[3.36]]: the triangles $DBC$ and $DAB$ are similar.
