Book III is about the circle: its chords, its tangents, the angles that stand on its arcs, and the lines through a point. Book I gave the straight line and the triangle; Book III uses them to find out what a circle forces on the lines drawn in it.

The book opens with a construction that later ones rely on, finding the centre of a given circle ([[3.1]]). A long run of propositions follows, most of them reductios, which pin down how circles and lines can meet. A chord lies inside its circle ([[3.2]]). Two circles meet in at most two points ([[3.10]]). Touching circles have their centres in line with the point of contact ([[3.11]], [[3.12]]). These are the facts a modern reader takes from the picture. Euclid proves them, because nothing in his postulates says what a circle looks like.

Tangents come next. The perpendicular to a radius at its end touches the circle, and no straight line fits between it and the circle ([[3.16]], with its strange "horn angle"). [[3.17]] constructs a tangent from an outside point.

The centre of the book is the theory of angles in a circle. The angle at the centre is double the angle at the circumference ([[3.20]]). So angles in the same segment are equal ([[3.21]]), opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add to two right angles ([[3.22]]), and the angle in a semicircle is right ([[3.31]], Thales' theorem). The tangent–chord angle equals the angle in the alternate segment ([[3.32]]).

The book ends with the *power of a point*: for chords through a point inside a circle, the products of the segments are equal ([[3.35]]), and from a point outside, the product for a secant equals the square of the tangent ([[3.36]], with its converse [[3.37]]). These use Book II's identities, and they lead to Book IV's constructions of regular polygons in circles.

Look for the live figures: in [[3.20]], [[3.21]] and [[3.31]] you can drag a point round the circle and watch the angle stay fixed.
