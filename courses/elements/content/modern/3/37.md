---
title: Converse of the tangent–secant theorem
---
**Statement.** From a point @D outside a circle, let one line cut the circle at @C and @A, and another meet it at @B. If $AD \cdot DC = DB^2$, then @DB touches the circle.

**Proof.** Draw the tangent @DE ([[3.17]]) and let @F be the centre. By [[3.36]], $AD \cdot DC = DE^2$, so $DE^2 = DB^2$ and $DE = DB$. The triangles $DEF$ and $DBF$ have three sides equal ($FE = FB$ are radii, $FD$ common), so $\angle DBF = \angle DEF$ ([[1.8]]), which is right ([[3.18]]). A line perpendicular to a radius at its end is a tangent ([[3.16]], porism).

In terms of power: the only points of the circle at distance $\sqrt{\operatorname{pow}(D)}$ from @D are the two points of contact, since $|DB|^2 = |DF|^2 - r^2$ holds exactly when the angle at @B is right.

**In the figure.** @B is placed on the circle at distance $\sqrt{AD \cdot DC}$ from @D, which is the hypothesis. Drag @D or @A: the angle at @B stays right, and $DB = DE$.
