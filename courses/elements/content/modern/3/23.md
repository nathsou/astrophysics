---
title: One similar segment per side
---
**Statement.** On a given segment @AB, on one side of it, there cannot be two different segments of circles that are similar (admit equal angles).

**Proof.** Suppose the segments $ACB$ and $ADB$ were similar and different, one inside the other. Draw a line from @A cutting the inner arc at @C and the outer at @D, and join @CB and @DB. Similarity means $\angle ACB = \angle ADB$ ([[3.def.11]]). But $\angle ACB$ is an exterior angle of the triangle $CDB$, so it is greater than the interior angle $\angle ADB$ ([[1.16]]). Contradiction.

So the arc on @AB admitting a given angle, on a given side, is unique. [[3.33]] constructs it.

:::gap The two arcs might cross
Euclid assumes that one segment lies inside the other, so that a line from @A meets the inner arc first. Two arcs through @A and @B could a priori cross, but then the circles would meet in three points, which [[3.10]] forbids. Euclid does not say this.
:::

**In the figure.** Drag @D along the outer (dashed) arc: $\angle ACB$ always exceeds $\angle ADB$, so the segments admit different angles.
