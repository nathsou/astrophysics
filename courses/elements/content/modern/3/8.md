---
title: Lines from a point outside a circle
---
**Statement.** From a point @D outside a circle with centre @M, draw lines that cut the circle. Of their far ends (on the "concave" side), @A on the line through the centre is farthest, and the nearer a line is to @DA, the farther its far end: $DA > DE > DF > DC$. Of their near ends (on the "convex" side), @G on @DA is nearest, and the nearer a line is to @DG, the nearer its near end: $DG < DK < DL < DH$. Equal lines from @D to the circle come in pairs, one on each side of @DA.

**Proof.** This is [[3.7]] with the point outside instead of inside, and it uses the same tools.
- Far side: $DE < DM + ME = DM + MA = DA$ ([[1.20]]), and $DE > DF$ by the hinge theorem ([[1.24]]) applied to the triangles $EMD$ and $FMD$.
- Near side: $DK + KM > DM = DG + GM$, so $DK > DG$. For $DK < DL$ Euclid uses [[1.21]]: two lines drawn inside a triangle from the ends of a side have a smaller sum than the other two sides. Applied to the triangle $MLD$ with @K inside it, $MK + KD < ML + LD$, and $MK = ML$.
- The pair: copy $\angle KMD$ on the other side of @MD to get @B, so $DB = DK$ by SAS. A third point @N with $DN = DK$ would contradict the monotonicity.

With the centre at the origin and $D = (d, 0)$, a point of the circle at angle $t$ from $DM$'s direction has $|DP|^2 = d^2 + r^2 - 2dr\cos t$, again monotonic in $t$. The far and near sides are simply the two ranges of $t$ on either side of the tangent points.

**In the figure.** Drag @D, and @E, @F, @C along the far arc; the near points @K, @L, @H follow. Drag @N: it matches $DK$ only at @K or @B.
