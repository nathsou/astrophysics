---
title: A square product comes from similar plane numbers
---
**Statement.** If $AB$ is a square, then $A$ and $B$ are similar plane numbers. This is the converse of [[9.1]].

**Proof.** Let @D $= A^2$. Then $A : B = D : C$ ([[7.17]]). @D and @C are both squares, so they are similar plane numbers, and one mean falls between them ([[8.18]]). The same number of means falls between @A and @B ([[8.8]]), and two numbers with a mean between them are similar plane numbers ([[8.20]]).

In modern terms, $AB$ is a square exactly when $A = k a^2$ and $B = k b^2$ for some $k$. Then $A = (ka)\cdot a$ and $B = (kb)\cdot b$ have proportional sides. The figure is parametrized this way.

:::gap Primes are not plane numbers
Euclid's plane numbers have two sides, each a number, so each side is at least $2$ (the unit is not a number). Take $A = 2$ and $B = 8$: the product $16$ is a square, but $2$ is prime, so it is not a plane number at all. The gap is really in [[8.20]]. For $A = 2$, $B = 8$ its proof takes $1$ and $2$ as the least numbers in the ratio $2 : 4$, and the unit is not a number. The theorem is true once $A$ and $B$ are both composite, or if "plane number" is allowed a side equal to $1$.
:::
