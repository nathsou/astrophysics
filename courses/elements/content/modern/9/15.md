---
title: Sums of terms in a least continued proportion
---
**Statement.** Let @A, @B, @C be the least numbers in their continued ratio. Then the sum of any two of them is coprime to the third.

Such numbers are $A = x^2$, $B = xy$, $C = y^2$ with $\gcd(x, y) = 1$ ([[8.2]]). In the figure $x =$ @DE and $y =$ @EF.

**Proof.** $DE$ and $EF$ are coprime ([[7.22]]), so their sum @DF is coprime to each of them ([[7.28]]).

- $A + B = x^2 + xy = x(x + y)$. Both $x$ and $x + y$ are coprime to $y$, so their product is coprime to $y$, and hence to $y^2 = C$ ([[7.24]], [[7.25]]).
- $B + C = y(x + y)$ is coprime to $A$ in the same way.
- $(x + y)^2$ is coprime to $xy$. Now $(x + y)^2 = x^2 + y^2 + 2xy$. Subtracting $xy$ twice does not change the common divisors with $xy$, so $A + C = x^2 + y^2$ is coprime to $B$.

Euclid gets the identities $x(x+y) = x^2 + xy$ and $(x+y)^2 = x^2 + 2xy + y^2$ from Book II ([[2.3]], [[2.4]]), which proves them for lines. Here they are applied to numbers, although Book VII never shows that the geometric results carry over.
