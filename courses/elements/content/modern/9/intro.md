Book IX finishes the arithmetic that began in Book VII. It has no definitions of its own: it works with the vocabulary of [[7.def.1]]–[[7.def.22]] (prime, square, cube, plane and solid numbers, even and odd, perfect) and with Book VIII's continued proportions, that is, geometric sequences. The book falls into four groups.

- **Squares and cubes** ([[9.1]]–[[9.10]]). Products and powers of squares and cubes, and which terms of a sequence $1, a, a^2, a^3, \dots$ are squares or cubes. In modern terms these are statements about exponents. Euclid proves them with the mean proportionals of Book VIII, because he has no exponent notation.
- **Primes and divisors** ([[9.11]]–[[9.20]]). A prime dividing $a^n$ divides $a$ ([[9.12]]), the only divisors of $p^n$ are powers of $p$ ([[9.13]]), and a product of distinct primes has no other prime divisors ([[9.14]]). That last one is the nearest Euclid comes to unique factorization. Then comes the most famous theorem of Greek arithmetic, [[9.20]]: there are more primes than any given finite list.
- **Even and odd** ([[9.21]]–[[9.34]]). Parity rules for sums, differences and products, and a classification of even numbers by how many times 2 divides them. These are short, and probably older than Euclid.
- **Two finales.** [[9.35]] sums a geometric series with a telescoping argument, and [[9.36]] uses that sum to build perfect numbers from primes of the form $2^n - 1$: $6$, $28$, $496$, $8128$, …

The book is written for numbers drawn as lines, and its proofs cite Books VII and VIII heavily. In the figures here, numbers are rods of units. The squares-and-cubes propositions use a logarithmic scale instead, cut at the prime factors. On that scale a product is its factors laid end to end, and a geometric sequence is a staircase of equal steps.
