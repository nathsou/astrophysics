---
title: A product of distinct primes has no other prime factors
---
**Statement.** Let @A be the least number divisible by the primes @B, @C, @D. Then no other prime divides $A$.

For distinct primes the least number they divide is their product, so in modern terms: *the only primes dividing $p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k$ are $p_1, \dots, p_k$.*

**Proof.** Suppose a prime @E, different from $B, C, D$, divides $A$, and write $A = E \cdot F$. Each of $B, C, D$ is a prime dividing the product $E \cdot F$, so it divides $E$ or $F$ ([[7.30]], Euclid's lemma). It cannot divide $E$, a prime different from it. So $B, C, D$ all divide @F. But $F < A$, and $A$ was the *least* number divisible by $B, C, D$. Contradiction.

In the figure, $A$ is drawn on a logarithmic scale as $B$, $C$, $D$ laid end to end. @E is the least prime not on the list, and the dashed @F $= A/E$ is not a whole number.

**What this is, and what it is not.** Put it this way: if $p_1 \cdots p_k = q_1 \cdots q_m$ with distinct primes on each side, then every $q_j$ divides the left side, so by IX.14 it is some $p_i$, and conversely. The two sets of primes are equal. This is **uniqueness of factorization for squarefree numbers**, and with [[9.13]] (prime powers) it covers the two extreme cases. Euclid does not state or prove:

- **Existence**: that every number *is* a product of primes. [[7.31]] and [[7.32]] give one prime divisor. Repeating the step to obtain a full factorization is not in the Elements.
- **Uniqueness with repeated factors**: that the *exponents* are determined as well as the primes, that is, $\prod p_i^{a_i} = \prod p_i^{b_i}$ implies $a_i = b_i$ for every $i$. IX.14 speaks only of the least number that given primes divide, in which each prime occurs once.

The key tool for the general theorem is already here: [[7.30]] used inside a minimality argument. The fundamental theorem of arithmetic (every integer $> 1$ is a product of primes in exactly one way, up to order) was first stated and proved in full generality by Gauss, in the *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801).

:::code Unique factorization as a property test
```ts
// IX.14, as a property: the primes dividing lcm(ps) are exactly ps.
function primesDividing(n: number): number[] {
  const out: number[] = [];
  for (let p = 2; p * p <= n; p++) {
    if (n % p === 0) out.push(p);
    while (n % p === 0) n /= p;
  }
  if (n > 1) out.push(n);
  return out;
}
// for distinct primes ps (sorted): primesDividing(ps.reduce((a, b) => a * b)) equals ps
```
Trial division *computes* a factorization, which settles existence. Uniqueness is the claim that any other way of splitting $n$ into primes gives the same multiset. That is exactly what Euclid's lemma guarantees: a prime $p$ dividing $q_1 q_2 \cdots q_m$ must be one of the $q_j$.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
Unique factorization is not automatic. In the numbers $a + b\sqrt{-5}$ (with $a, b$ integers), $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$, and none of these four factors can be split further. There Euclid's lemma fails: $2$ divides the product $(1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$ but neither factor. Repairing this in the nineteenth century led Kummer and Dedekind to the theory of ideals.
:::
