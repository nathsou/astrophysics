---
title: The divisors of a prime power
---
**Statement.** If $p$ is prime, the only divisors of $p^n$ are $1, p, p^2, \dots, p^n$.

**Proof.** Take the figure's sequence @A, @B, @C, @D $= p, p^2, p^3, p^4$, and suppose that $E \mid D$ with $E$ not among $A, B, C$.

- $E$ is not prime: a prime dividing $D$ divides $A = p$ ([[9.12]]), so it would be $p$ itself.
- So $E$ is composite, and every prime dividing it divides $D$, so it is $p$ ([[7.31]], [[9.12]]). Hence $p \mid E$.
- Write $D = E \cdot F$. The quotient @F is not a power of $p$ either, since otherwise $E$ would be one ([[9.11]]). The same argument gives $p \mid F$.
- $E \cdot F = A \cdot C$ gives $A : E = F : C$ ([[7.19]]). Since $A \mid E$, $F \mid C$, say $C = F \cdot G$. Repeat one level down with $G$ and $H$. At the bottom, $H \cdot G = A^2$ gives $H \mid A$, and $A$ is prime, which is absurd.

The dashed rods @E, @F, @G, @H are these impossible numbers. On the logarithmic scale they fall between the steps.

**Comment.** This is unique factorization for prime powers: a number whose only prime factor is $p$ is a power of $p$. Together with [[9.14]], it is as close as Euclid comes to the fundamental theorem of arithmetic.
