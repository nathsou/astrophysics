---
title: A prime dividing a power divides its base
---
**Statement.** If a prime $p$ divides $a^n$, then $p$ divides $a$. So $a$ and $a^n$ have the same prime divisors.

**Proof.** Let the prime @E measure @D $= a^4$ (the figure's sequence is @A, @B, @C, @D $= a, a^2, a^3, a^4$), and suppose $E \nmid A$. Then $E$ and $A$ are coprime ([[7.29]]). Euclid then walks down the sequence:

- $E \cdot F = D = A \cdot C$, so $A : E = F : C$ ([[7.19]]). $A, E$ are coprime, hence least in their ratio ([[7.21]]), so $E \mid C$ ([[7.20]]).
- The same argument with $E \cdot G = C = A \cdot B$ gives $E \mid B$.
- And with $E \cdot H = B = A \cdot A$ it gives $E \mid A$.

The last step contradicts the assumption, so $E \mid A$.

**Comment.** Each step is [[7.30]] (Euclid's lemma) proved again from scratch: a prime dividing $A \cdot X$ and not $A$ divides $X$. Once that lemma is available, the modern proof is a one-line induction on $n$.

The reductio is set up in a roundabout way. The assumption $E \nmid A$ is first turned into "$E$ and $A$ are coprime". The contradiction then shows that they are not coprime, and a prime that shares a factor with $A$ divides $A$.
