---
title: Euclid's perfect numbers
---
**Statement.** If $1 + 2 + 4 + \dots + 2^{n-1} = 2^n - 1$ is prime, then
$$N = 2^{n-1}(2^n - 1)$$
is *perfect*: it equals the sum of its parts (its divisors less than itself, [[7.def.22]]). For $n = 2, 3, 5, 7$ this gives $6$, $28$, $496$, $8128$.

**Proof.** The figure is Heath's case $n = 5$: the unit and @A, @B, @C, @D $= 2, 4, 8, 16$ add up to the prime @E $= 31$, and @FG $= E \cdot D = 496$.

1. *FG is the sum of the listed numbers.* Double @E repeatedly: $E$, @HK, @L, @M $= 31, 62, 124, 248$. The next double is $FG$, because $A : D = E : M$ gives $FG = E \cdot D = A \cdot M = 2M$. So $E, HK, L, M, FG$ are in double proportion. By [[9.35]], the excess of the second over the first ($NK = E$) is to the first as the excess of the last ($OG$) is to the sum of all before it. So $OG = M + L + HK + E$. The rest of $FG$ is @FO $= E = 1 + A + B + C + D$. Hence
$$FG = 1 + A + B + C + D + E + HK + L + M,$$
and each of these divides $FG$.
2. *There are no other divisors.* Suppose @P divides $FG$ and is none of these, with $FG = P \cdot Q$. From $E \cdot D = P \cdot Q$, $E : Q = P : D$ ([[7.19]]). The only divisors of $D = 2^{n-1}$ are $1, A, B, C$ ([[9.13]]), so $P \nmid D$, and therefore $E \nmid Q$. $E$ is prime, so $E$ and @Q are coprime and least in their ratio, and $Q \mid D$ ([[7.20]], [[7.21]]). So $Q$ is a power of 2, say $Q = B$. Then $B \cdot L = D \cdot E = Q \cdot P$ forces $P = L$, which is on the list. Contradiction.

So the parts of $FG$ are exactly the unit, the doubles of the unit up to $D$, and $E$ and its doubles up to $M$. Their sum is $FG$.

:::code The modern proof in two lines
The divisor-sum function $\sigma$ is multiplicative, and $\sigma(p) = p + 1$ for a prime $p$. With $p = 2^n - 1$:
$$\sigma(N) = \sigma(2^{n-1})\,\sigma(p) = (2^n - 1)\cdot 2^n = 2N.$$
"Perfect" means $\sigma(N) = 2N$: the parts add up to $N$, and $N$ itself adds another $N$.
```ts
const sigma = (n: number) => { let s = 0; for (let d = 1; d <= n; d++) if (n % d === 0) s += d; return s; };
const perfect = (n: number) => sigma(n) === 2 * n;
[2, 3, 5, 7].map((k) => 2 ** (k - 1) * (2 ** k - 1)).every(perfect); // 6, 28, 496, 8128: true
```
:::

:::leads Where it leads
*Euler's converse.* Euler proved that every **even** perfect number has Euclid's form $2^{n-1}(2^n - 1)$ with $2^n - 1$ prime. Even perfect numbers therefore correspond exactly to primes of the form $2^n - 1$, now called *Mersenne primes*. $2^n - 1$ can be prime only if $n$ is prime, but that is not enough: $2^{11} - 1 = 2047 = 23 \cdot 89$. A little over fifty Mersenne primes are known, most of them found by distributed computer searches. Whether there are infinitely many is open.

*Odd perfect numbers.* No odd perfect number is known, and no one has proved that none exists. This is one of the oldest open problems in mathematics. Computer searches and theoretical constraints show that an odd perfect number would have to be enormous, with well over a thousand digits, and would need many distinct prime factors.
:::

:::history The first perfect numbers
The four perfect numbers $6$, $28$, $496$, $8128$ were known in antiquity. IX.36 is the last proposition of the arithmetical books, and the definition of a perfect number ([[7.def.22]]) is used nowhere else in the Elements. The arithmetic of Books VII–IX thus ends with this construction.
:::
