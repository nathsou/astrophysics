---
title: Rational and irrational lines
---
**Definition.** Fix one line $\rho$ and call it *rational*. A line is also called rational when it is commensurable with $\rho$ in length **or in square only**, and *irrational* otherwise.

In modern terms, with $\rho = 1$: a line $x$ is rational in Euclid's sense when $x^2$ is a rational number. So $2$, $\tfrac35$, $\sqrt 2$ and $\sqrt{3/7}$ are all "rational", while $\sqrt[4]{2}$ and $1 + \sqrt2$ are "irrational". The word does not mean what it means today. The modern *rational* corresponds to Euclid's "rational and commensurable in length with $\rho$".

The choice is natural for Euclid, whose basic operation is to square a line. A line whose square can be measured by $\rho^2$ is under control: the whole of Book X is about the lines that are not. The irrational lines he studies are all built from these rational ones by one more square root, as in $\sqrt[4]{2}$ or $\sqrt{\sqrt3 + \sqrt2}$.

The definition is relative to $\rho$, and Euclid can choose any line as $\rho$. The phrase "a rational line" in a proposition means "a line commensurable in square with the one we fixed". [[10.10]] shows that lines incommensurable with $\rho$ in length only, and in square too, exist.
