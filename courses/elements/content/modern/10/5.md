---
title: Commensurable means a ratio of numbers
---
**Statement.** If $a, b$ are commensurable, then $a : b = m : n$ for some whole numbers $m, n$.

**Proof.** Let @C measure @A $m$ times and @B $n$ times, and let the numbers @D, @E be $m$ and $n$. Then $C : A = 1 : m$ in the sense of numbers ([[7.def.20]]), so inversely $A : C = D : 1$. Likewise $C : B = 1 : E$. Composing ex aequali ([[5.22]]), $A : B = D : E$.

The point of the proof is the bridge between two theories of proportion: the numerical one of Book VII and Eudoxus' one of Book V. Euclid passes silently from one to the other, which is harmless here: for commensurable magnitudes the two definitions agree.
