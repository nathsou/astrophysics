---
title: Commensurable with one, incommensurable with the other
---
**Statement.** If $a, b$ are commensurable and $a$ is incommensurable with $c$, then $b$ is incommensurable with $c$.

**Proof.** Otherwise $a$ would be commensurable with $c$ through $b$ ([[10.12]]). In modern terms: if $b/c \in \mathbb{Q}$ then $a/c = (a/b)(b/c) \in \mathbb{Q}$. In the figure, @B $= \tfrac pq A$ and @C $= \sqrt n\, A$, so $B : C = p : q\sqrt n$, never a ratio of numbers.

**Lemma.** Given lines $AB > c$, find the line $x$ with $AB^2 = c^2 + x^2$: draw the semicircle on @AB, fit @AD equal to $c$ in it ([[4.1]]), and join @DB. The angle at $D$ is right ([[3.31]]), so $x = DB$ by [[1.47]]. Conversely the hypotenuse of a right angle with sides $AD, DB$ gives $\sqrt{AD^2 + DB^2}$. Book X uses this constantly in the form "the square on $a$ exceeds the square on $b$ by the square on $x$", that is $x = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2}$.
