---
title: First binomial
---
Take the rational line set out as the unit, $\rho = 1$. A binomial ([[10.36]]) is $x + y$ with $x > y$ rational lines commensurable in square only: $X = x^2$ and $Y = y^2$ are rational numbers, and $X/Y$ is not the square of a rational. The binomial is **first** when

- $\sqrt{X - Y}$ is commensurable with $x$, that is, $1 - Y/X$ is the square of a rational, and
- the greater term $x$ is commensurable with $\rho$, that is, $x$ is itself a rational number.

Example: $3 + \sqrt5$, since $\sqrt{9 - 5} = 2$ and $2/3$ is rational.

**Comment.** The six definitions ask two independent questions about a binomial $x + y$:

| | $\sqrt{X-Y}$ commensurable with $x$ | $\sqrt{X-Y}$ incommensurable with $x$ |
|---|---|---|
| $x$ commensurable with $\rho$ | first: $3 + \sqrt5$ | fourth: $2 + \sqrt2$ |
| $y$ commensurable with $\rho$ | second: $3\sqrt2 + 4$ | fifth: $\sqrt2 + 1$ |
| neither | third: $3\sqrt2 + \sqrt{10}$ | sixth: $\sqrt3 + \sqrt2$ |

The first question decides whether $\sqrt{x + y}$ can be split into two lines commensurable in square ([[10.17]], [[10.18]]); the second decides what those lines are. [[10.54]]–[[10.59]] show that the six orders give exactly the six additive irrationals of [[10.36]]–[[10.41]].
