---
title: A binomial splits into its terms in only one way
---
**Statement.** If a binomial is cut at @C into its terms, it cannot be cut into rational terms commensurable in square only at any other point. In algebra: if $a, b, c, d$ are positive rationals with $\sqrt{a/b}$ and $\sqrt{c/d}$ irrational, then
$$\sqrt a + \sqrt b = \sqrt c + \sqrt d \implies \{a, b\} = \{c, d\}.$$

**Proof (Euclid's).** Suppose @AB is also cut at @D into rational terms @AD, @DB commensurable in square only. The point $D$ is not the mirror image of $C$ in the midpoint: that would give the same two terms in the other order. So $C$ and $D$ are at different distances from the midpoint.

Square the whole line both ways ([[2.4]]):
$$AC^2 + CB^2 + 2\,AC\cdot CB \;=\; AB^2 \;=\; AD^2 + DB^2 + 2\,AD\cdot DB,$$
and rearrange:
$$(AC^2 + CB^2) - (AD^2 + DB^2) \;=\; 2\,AD\cdot DB - 2\,AC\cdot CB.$$
The left side is a difference of two rational areas, so it is rational. It is not zero, because the cut farther from the middle gives the larger sum of squares (the lemma after [[10.41]]). On the right, each rectangle is contained by rational lines commensurable in square only, so it is medial ([[10.21]]). But one medial area never exceeds another by a rational area ([[10.26]]). So there is no second point $D$.

In numbers, [[10.26]] is a two-line argument. If $\sqrt p - \sqrt q = r$ with $r \ne 0$ rational and $p, q$ rational, then $p = q + r^2 + 2r\sqrt q$, so $\sqrt q$ is rational after all.

**In the figure.** The sliders set $AC^2 = a$ and $CB^2 = b$. Drag @D: the identity above holds in every position, since it is only algebra. What fails is the arithmetic. For $AD$, $DB$ to be rational terms, the left side would have to be a nonzero rational and the right side a difference of two medial areas, which [[10.26]] forbids.

:::code Well-defined classification
Book X sorts lines into types by how they split: a line is a binomial of the fourth order, a major, and so on. For that to be a property of the *line*, and not of one way of cutting it, the cut must be unique. X.42–47 prove exactly that, one proposition per additive irrational. In programming terms, `order(x)` is computed from a representation, and these propositions show that every representation gives the same answer.

The figures of Book X compute with the same fact. Their lengths are stored exactly, as sums $c_1\sqrt{n_1} + c_2\sqrt{n_2} + \dots$ with rational $c_i$ and square-free $n_i$. Because the square roots of distinct square-free integers are linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$ (a standard theorem of algebra), two such sums are equal only if their coefficients agree, and a ratio is rational only if the coefficient vectors are proportional. So "commensurable" becomes an exact test, not a floating-point guess.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
In modern language, every element of the field $\mathbb Q(\sqrt d)$ is $r + s\sqrt d$ in exactly one way, because $\{1, \sqrt d\}$ is a basis. X.42 is the analogous statement for two roots. Uniqueness makes the *conjugate* $\sqrt a - \sqrt b$ well defined: that is the apotome of [[10.73]], whose own uniqueness is [[10.79]]. Multiplying a binomial by its conjugate gives a rational number, the idea behind [[10.112]] and behind rationalizing denominators in school algebra.
:::
