---
title: Rational and irrational areas
---
**Definition.** The square $\rho^2$ on the rational line is *rational*, and so is every area commensurable with it: an area is rational when it is a rational multiple of $\rho^2$. Other areas are *irrational*, and so is the line whose square equals an irrational area (the side itself, if the area is a square).

With $\rho = 1$: an area $A$ is rational when $A \in \mathbb{Q}$, and a line $x$ is irrational when $x^2 \notin \mathbb{Q}$. Areas have no "in square only" clause, so for areas Euclid's word agrees with the modern one.

The first irrational area in the book is the rectangle $\sqrt a \cdot \sqrt b = \sqrt{ab}$ on two rational lines commensurable in square only; the side of the equal square, $\sqrt[4]{ab}$, is the *medial* line ([[10.21]]). A rectangle is turned into a square by [[2.14]], so "the line whose square equals the area" always exists.
