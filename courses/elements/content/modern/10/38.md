---
title: The second bimedial line
---
**Statement.** If two medial lines, commensurable in square only and containing a medial rectangle, are added, the whole is irrational. It is called the **second bimedial**.

With $\rho = 1$ (the pairs of [[10.28]]):
$$\text{second bimedial} = \sqrt[4]{k} + \frac{\sqrt\lambda}{\sqrt[4]k},$$
with $k, \lambda$ and $k\lambda$ not squares, for example $\sqrt[4]2 + \sqrt3/\sqrt[4]2$. The terms are medial, their product $\sqrt\lambda$ is medial.

**Proof.** Now both parts of $AC^2 = (AB^2 + BC^2) + 2\,AB \cdot BC$ are medial, so the squaring argument of [[10.36]] alone does not decide. Euclid applies $AC^2$ to a rational line @DE as the rectangle @figure(DF): the part $AB^2 + BC^2$ as @figure(EH) with breadth @DH, and $2\,AB \cdot BC$ as @figure(HF) with breadth @HG. Both breadths are rational and incommensurable with $DE$ ([[10.22]]); they are incommensurable with each other because the two areas are. So $DH, HG$ are rational and commensurable in square only, and $DG = DH + HG$ is a binomial ([[10.36]]). A rectangle of a rational and a binomial line is irrational, so $AC$ is irrational.

In the figure, $DH = (k + \lambda)/\sqrt k$ and $HG = 2\sqrt\lambda$, and $DG$ is a binomial of the third order.
