---
title: The medial line
---
**Statement.** If $AB, BC$ are rational lines commensurable in square only, the rectangle $AB \cdot BC$ is irrational, and so is the side of the square equal to it. That side is called **medial**.

With $\rho = 1$: $AB = \sqrt p$, $BC = \sqrt q$ with $p, q$ rational and $pq$ not a square. The rectangle is $\sqrt{pq}$, irrational, and the medial line is
$$\sqrt[4]{pq}.$$
The medial is the mean proportional between two rational lines commensurable in square only, which is where its name (*mésē*, "middle") comes from.

**Proof.** As in [[10.19]]: the square @figure(AD) on @AB and the rectangle @figure(AC) have the same height, so $AD : AC = BD : BC = AB : BC$ ([[6.1]]). This time $AB, BC$ are incommensurable in length, so the areas are incommensurable ([[10.11]]). $AD$ is rational, so $AC$ is irrational, and so is the side of the equal square ([[10.def1.4]]).

**Why this is new.** Everything before this has lengths whose squares are rational. The medial line $\sqrt[4]{pq}$ has an irrational square, so it is not "rational" in any of Euclid's senses: it is the first of the thirteen irrational lines of the book. In the figure, the rectangle $AC = \sqrt6$ (for $p = 3$, $q = 2$) and the medial side $\sqrt[4]{6} \approx 1.565$.

**Lemma.** For lines $FE, EG$: $FE : EG = FE^2 : FE \cdot EG$. The figure draws it on the same square: $F, E$ are $A, B$, and the dashed rectangle @figure(GD) is $DE \cdot EG$ with $EG = BC$. This is [[6.1]] once more, and it lets Euclid turn a ratio of lines into a ratio of areas and back.

:::leads Where it leads
Medial lines and areas are the raw material of the rest of the book: [[10.22]]–[[10.26]] work out how they behave, and five of the twelve compound irrationals are built from medial lines ([[10.37]], [[10.38]], [[10.41]] and their apotome partners). In field terms, a medial line generates $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{pq})$, of degree 4 over $\mathbb{Q}$: the first time Book X leaves the quadratic fields $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)$.
:::
