---
title: The binomial
---
**Statement.** If two rational lines, commensurable in square only, are added, the whole is irrational. It is called a **binomial** (*ek dyo onomatōn*, "from two names").

With $\rho = 1$:
$$\text{binomial} = \sqrt a + \sqrt b, \qquad a, b \in \mathbb{Q}_{>0},\ \ a/b \text{ not the square of a rational}.$$
For example $1 + \sqrt 2$, $\sqrt 5 + \sqrt 2$, $3 + \sqrt 5$. The condition on $a/b$ is exactly "commensurable in square only": if $a/b$ were a square, $\sqrt a + \sqrt b$ would be a rational multiple of $\sqrt b$, hence rational in Euclid's sense.

**Proof.** Let @AB and @BC be the two lines, so @AC is their sum. By [[2.4]] (the square below the line in the figure),
$$AC^2 = \underbrace{AB^2 + BC^2}_{a + b} + \underbrace{2\,AB \cdot BC}_{2\sqrt{ab}}.$$
- $AB : BC$ is irrational, and $AB : BC = AB \cdot BC : BC^2$, so the rectangle $AB \cdot BC$ is incommensurable with $BC^2$ ([[10.11]]).
- $AB^2 + BC^2$ is commensurable with $BC^2$ (both rational), and $2\,AB \cdot BC$ with $AB \cdot BC$. So $2\,AB\cdot BC$ is incommensurable with $AB^2 + BC^2$ ([[10.13]]).
- Adding two incommensurable areas gives an area incommensurable with each ([[10.16]]). So $AC^2$ is incommensurable with the rational $AB^2 + BC^2$, and is irrational. So is $AC$ ([[10.def1.4]]).

In algebra: $(\sqrt a + \sqrt b)^2 = (a + b) + 2\sqrt{ab}$, a rational plus a non-zero irrational, which is irrational.

**Orders.** The binomial is one kind of line, but Euclid sorts binomials into six orders ([[10.def2.1]]–[[10.def2.6]]) by two tests on the greater term $\alpha = \sqrt a$ and the lesser $\beta = \sqrt b$: is $\sqrt{a - b}$ commensurable with $\alpha$ in length, and is $\alpha$ or $\beta$ commensurable with $\rho$ in length? $3 + \sqrt5$ is of the first order ($\sqrt{9 - 5} = 2$, and $3$ is rational); $1 + \sqrt 2$ of the fifth; $\sqrt3 + \sqrt2$ of the sixth. The figure shows the order for the current $a, b$. The orders do not matter for the binomial itself, but they decide which of the six additive lines is the square root of a binomial ([[10.54]]–[[10.59]]). The classifier in the [introduction](#/book/10) computes them.

:::code A binomial is a pair
Euclid's binomial is really an element of the field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)$ written in the basis $\{1, \sqrt d\}$, or more generally a pair of "names" $(\alpha, \beta)$ with $\alpha^2, \beta^2 \in \mathbb{Q}$:
```ts
type Binomial = { a: Rational; b: Rational }; // √a + √b, a > b > 0, a/b not a rational square
const square = ({ a, b }: Binomial) => ({ rational: a + b, medialPart: 4 * a * b }); // (a+b) + √(4ab)
```
[[10.42]] proves the representation is unique, so the pair $(a, b)$ is well defined: a binomial determines its two names. That is the statement that $1$ and $\sqrt d$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb{Q}$.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
X.36 is the first of six propositions, [[10.36]]–[[10.41]], each adding two lines and proving the sum irrational, with the same squaring argument. Its mirror is [[10.73]], the *apotome* $\sqrt a - \sqrt b$. [[10.112]] shows that $1/(\sqrt a + \sqrt b)$ is an apotome: rationalizing the denominator, $(\sqrt a - \sqrt b)/(a - b)$. In modern algebra the binomials and apotomes are exactly the irrational elements of the real quadratic fields $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)$ with both coordinates non-zero, measured against $\rho = 1$ or against $\rho = \sqrt r$.
:::
