---
title: The quadratic with a commensurable root difference
---
**Statement.** Let $a > c$ be lines, and cut $a$ into two parts $x + y = a$ with $xy = (c/2)^2$ (a rectangle applied to $a$, falling short by a square). Then $x, y$ are commensurable in length exactly when $\sqrt{a^2 - c^2}$ is commensurable in length with $a$.

In algebra: $x, y$ are the roots of $t^2 - at + c^2/4 = 0$, and
$$x - y = \sqrt{a^2 - c^2}.$$
So the roots are in a rational ratio exactly when the "discriminant" $\sqrt{a^2 - c^2}$ is commensurable with $a$.

**Proof.** In the figure $a$ is @BC, cut at @D into $BD = x$ and $DC = y$; the shaded rectangle is $BD \cdot DC$ and the dashed square completes it. Bisect $BC$ at @E and make $EF = ED$, so $BF = DC$ and $DF = x - y$. By [[2.5]], $BD \cdot DC + ED^2 = EC^2$. Multiply by 4: $A^2 + DF^2 = BC^2$, where @A is the line $c$. So $DF = \sqrt{a^2 - c^2}$.

If $BD, DC$ are commensurable, so are $BC$ and $DC$ ([[10.15]]), hence $BC$ with $BF + DC = 2DC$ and with the remainder $DF$ ([[10.12]], [[10.15]]). The converse runs the same steps backwards.

This criterion is how Euclid tests the condition "the square on the greater exceeds the square on the less by the square on a line commensurable with the greater", which defines the first three orders of binomials ([[10.def2.1]]).
