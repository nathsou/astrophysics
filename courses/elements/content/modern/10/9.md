---
title: Irrationality of square roots (Theaetetus)
---
**Statement.** For lines $a, b$:
$$a, b \text{ commensurable in length} \iff a^2 : b^2 = m^2 : n^2 \text{ for some whole numbers } m, n.$$
Equivalently, $a^2 : b^2 = p : q$ (whole numbers) gives commensurable sides exactly when $p : q$, in lowest terms, is a ratio of two square numbers. **Porism:** lines commensurable in length are commensurable in square, but not conversely.

With $b = 1$ and $a^2 = n$: $\sqrt n$ is rational if and only if $n$ is a perfect square. More generally $\sqrt{p/q}$ is rational only when $p$ and $q$ (in lowest terms) are both squares.

**Proof.** If @A and @B are commensurable, $A : B = C : D$ for numbers @C, @D ([[10.5]]). Squares of similar figures are in the duplicate ratio of their sides ([[6.20]], porism), and so are square numbers ([[8.11]]). So $A^2 : B^2 = C^2 : D^2$, a ratio of squares. Conversely, if $A^2 : B^2 = C^2 : D^2$, then taking the "half" of each duplicate ratio gives $A : B = C : D$, and [[10.6]] makes them commensurable. The two negative statements are the contrapositives.

The number theory is in the lemma: numbers in the ratio of a square to a square are *similar plane numbers* ([[8.26]] and its converse), $p = k m^2$ and $q = k n^2$. So the real content is arithmetic: if $p/q = (m/n)^2$ in lowest terms then $p, q$ are squares, which is the uniqueness of prime factorization in disguise.

**In the figure,** choose $p : q$ for the ratio of the squares. For $9 : 4$ the sides are in the ratio $3 : 2$ and @C, @D are those numbers. For $2 : 1$ they cannot exist (dashed): the figure tests commensurability numerically, independently of the squares, and the two tests always agree.

:::code Deciding rationality of √(p/q)
```ts
const isSquare = (n: number) => Number.isInteger(Math.sqrt(n));
const gcd = (a: number, b: number): number => (b ? gcd(b, a % b) : a);

// Is √(p/q) rational? By X.9: exactly when p/q in lowest terms is a ratio of squares.
function sqrtIsRational(p: number, q: number) {
  const g = gcd(p, q);
  return isSquare(p / g) && isSquare(q / g);
}
```
This is the decision procedure the whole of Book X rests on. Every "commensurable" or "incommensurable" there reduces, in the end, to checking whether some ratio of whole numbers is a ratio of squares.
:::

:::history Theodorus and Theaetetus
In Plato's *Theaetetus* (147d–148b), the young Theaetetus recalls that Theodorus drew figures showing that the sides of squares of area $3, 5, \dots$ up to $17$ are incommensurable with the unit, one case at a time. Theaetetus and a friend then looked for a general statement, and divided numbers into "square" and "oblong" ones: the side of an oblong number is commensurable with the unit only in square. That is the content of X.9, and an ancient scholium on this proposition credits it to Theaetetus. How Theodorus handled each case, and why he stopped at 17, is not known.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
The modern proof is one line: if $\sqrt n = m/k$ in lowest terms then $n k^2 = m^2$, and comparing the exponent of each prime shows that $n$ is a square. The same argument proves that $\sqrt[r]{n}$ is irrational unless $n$ is an $r$-th power, and it generalizes to the statement that $\mathbb{Z}$ is *integrally closed*: a rational root of a monic integer polynomial is an integer. Book X uses X.9 in almost every proposition, through [[10.10]] and [[10.29]]–[[10.30]], to produce lines commensurable in square only.
:::
