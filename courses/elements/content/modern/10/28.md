---
title: Medial lines containing a medial rectangle
---
**Statement.** To find medial lines, commensurable in square only, whose rectangle is medial.

With $\rho = 1$ and three rationals $A = \sqrt a$, $B = \sqrt b$, $C = \sqrt c$, commensurable in square only: $D = \sqrt[4]{ab}$ and $E = DC/B$ are medial, and $DE = AC = \sqrt{ac}$ is medial. In the figure, $A, B, C = \sqrt2, \sqrt3, \sqrt5$.

**Proof.** @D is the mean proportional between @A and @B, so medial ([[10.21]]). Choose @E with $B : C = D : E$ ([[6.12]]): $D, E$ are commensurable in square only ([[10.11]]) and $E$ is medial ([[10.23]]). Alternating and using $B : D = D : A$ gives $D : A = C : E$, so $DE = AC$ ([[6.16]]), a medial area ([[10.21]]).

These pairs are the terms of the *second bimedial* line ([[10.38]]).

**Lemma 1** (a Pythagorean triple recipe). If $AB$ and $BC$ are similar plane numbers of the same parity, say $AB = mp^2$ and $BC = mq^2$, then with $D$ the midpoint of $CA$,
$$AB \cdot BC + CD^2 = BD^2,$$
([[2.6]]) and $AB \cdot BC = (mpq)^2$ is a square. So $(mpq)^2 + \big(\tfrac{m(p^2 - q^2)}2\big)^2 = \big(\tfrac{m(p^2 + q^2)}2\big)^2$: two squares whose sum is a square. On the number line in the figure, $B$ is at 0 and $A$ at $AB$.

**Lemma 2.** Two squares whose sum is not a square: move from $D$ one unit towards $C$, to $E$. Then $AB \cdot BC + CE^2$ lies strictly between two consecutive squares $BE^2$ and $BD^2$, so it is not a square. Euclid shows it equals neither $BE^2$ nor any $BF^2$ in between, using the auxiliary points $G$, $F$, $H$. His argument is longer than it needs to be, since whole numbers strictly between $(n-1)^2$ and $n^2$ are not squares.

These lemmas supply the numbers used in [[10.29]] and [[10.30]].
