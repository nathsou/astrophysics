---
title: Infinitely many irrationals from a medial
---
**Statement.** From one medial line arise infinitely many irrational lines, each of a kind different from all before it. With $\rho = 1$ and the medial line $A = \sqrt[4]{k}$ ($k$ a non-square), the lines are
$$A = \sqrt[4]{k}, \qquad C = \sqrt[8]{k}, \qquad D = \sqrt[16]{k}, \qquad \dots, \qquad \sqrt[2^{n}]{k}, \dots$$
each the mean proportional between $\rho$ and the one before.

**Proof (Euclid's).** Set out a rational line @B and let $C^2 = B \cdot A$.

- @C is irrational: a rectangle on a rational and an irrational line is irrational ([[10.20]] read backwards), and the side of an irrational area is irrational ([[10.def1.4]]).
- @C is none of the thirteen. The square of each of the thirteen, applied to a rational line, has as breadth a rational line (for the medial) or a binomial or an apotome ([[10.22]], [[10.60]]–[[10.65]], [[10.97]]–[[10.102]]). The square of $C$ applied to $B$ has breadth $A$, a medial line, which is none of these.

Then let $D^2 = B \cdot C$. The same argument shows that @D is irrational and new: its square applied to $B$ has breadth $C$, which is not the breadth of any earlier line. And so on without end.

:::gap What "none the same" needs
Euclid's test for sameness is the breadth: lines of the same kind have squares whose breadths on a rational line are of the same kind. For the thirteen lines this was established in [[10.60]]–[[10.65]], [[10.97]]–[[10.102]] and [[10.111]]. For the new ones the chain runs: the breadth of $C$ is the medial $A$; the breadth of $D$ is $C$, which is new; the breadth of the next line is $D$; and so on. The induction is left to the reader: "similarly, if this arrangement proceeds ad infinitum". The text also does not say what "the same" should mean for kinds that have no definition; the natural reading is that no new line is commensurable with a line of an earlier kind.

Degrees make the claim precise. For $k = 2$ the polynomial $x^{2^n} - 2$ is irreducible (Eisenstein's criterion at $2$), so $\sqrt[2^n]{2}$ has degree $2^n$ over $\mathbb Q$, and so does every rational multiple of it. Each of the thirteen lines is the square root of a number in a field $\mathbb Q(\sqrt X, \sqrt Y)$ of degree at most $4$, so its degree is at most $8$. From $D = \sqrt[16]{2}$ on, every line of the sequence differs from the thirteen, and from every other line of the sequence, by degree alone.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
X.115 is the book's glimpse of an infinite hierarchy. Every line in Book X is reached from $\rho$ by square roots, so it is constructible with ruler and compass; and a constructible number lies in a tower of fields
$$\mathbb Q \subset K_1 \subset K_2 \subset \dots \subset K_n,$$
each of degree $2$ over the one before. Book X works on the lowest floors of that tower. The sequence $\sqrt[4]{k}, \sqrt[8]{k}, \sqrt[16]{k}, \dots$ climbs it forever. Wantzel's theorem (1837) turns the picture around: a constructible number has degree a power of $2$, so $\sqrt[3]{2}$, of degree $3$, is not constructible, and the cube cannot be doubled with ruler and compass. Numbers like $\pi$ are outside every such tower altogether.
:::
