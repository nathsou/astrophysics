---
title: The side of the sum of two medial areas
---
**Statement.** Let $x = AB$ and $y = BC$ be incommensurable in square, with $x^2 + y^2$ medial, $xy$ medial, and $x^2 + y^2$ incommensurable with $xy$ (such lines exist by [[10.35]]). Then $x + y$ is irrational. It is the sixth and last additive irrational, the **side of the sum of two medial areas**: its square $(x^2 + y^2) + 2xy$ is exactly that.

Example, with $\rho = 1$: $x^2 = \tfrac{\sqrt5 + \sqrt3}{2}$, $y^2 = \tfrac{\sqrt5 - \sqrt3}{2}$. Then $x^2 + y^2 = \sqrt5$, $xy = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$, and $(x + y)^2 = \sqrt5 + \sqrt2$. This is the default of the figure.

**Proof.** Apply both medial areas to the rational line @DE: @DF $= x^2 + y^2$ with breadth @DG, and @GH $= 2xy$ with breadth @GK. A medial area on a rational line has a rational breadth incommensurable in length with that line ([[10.22]]), so $DG$ and $GK$ are rational. Their ratio is the ratio of the two areas ([[6.1]]), which is irrational, so they are commensurable in square only and @DK is a binomial ([[10.36]]). The rectangle $DH = AC^2$ is then irrational, and so is its side @AC.

**Lemma.** Heath's appended lemma prepares [[10.42]]–[[10.47]]: if a line is cut at two points, the cut farther from the middle gives the larger sum of squares. In modern form, for $a + b$ fixed, $a^2 + b^2 = \tfrac12\big((a+b)^2 + (a-b)^2\big)$ grows with $|a - b|$. The lemma reuses the letters $A$–$E$ for a different line; the figure draws only the proposition.
