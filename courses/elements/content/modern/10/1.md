---
title: The exhaustion lemma
---
**Statement.** Let $a > c > 0$ be magnitudes of one kind. Take away from $a$ more than half of it, from the remainder more than half of the remainder, and so on. Then at some step the remainder is less than $c$. In symbols: if $r_0 = a$ and $0 \le r_{k+1} < \tfrac12 r_k$, then $r_n < c$ for some $n$. The closing sentence adds that "exactly half" works too.

**Proof.** By the Archimedean property ([[5.def.4]]), some multiple of @C exceeds @AB: in the figure $DE = 3C > AB$, with @DE cut into @DF, @FG, @GE equal to $C$. Now remove parts from both, step for step: from @AB more than half (@BH, then @HK), from @DE one copy of $C$ at a time (@GE, then @FG). Removing one of $n$ equal parts takes away at most half, so:

- $DE > AB$; from $DE$ we remove $GE$, at most half, from $AB$ we remove $BH$, more than half. So $GD > HA$.
- Again, from $GD$ we remove the half $FG$, from $HA$ more than half. So $DF > AK$.

After $n - 1$ steps the right-hand side is a single copy $DF = C$, and the remainder @AK of $AB$ is smaller. The invariant is "remainder of $nC$ > remainder of $a$", and it holds because $nC$ loses at most half at each step while $a$ loses more.

:::code An invariant and a loop that terminates
```ts
// r: what is left of a; m: copies of c still standing (m·c > r throughout)
function exhaust(a: number, c: number, takeMoreThanHalf: (r: number) => number) {
  let m = Math.floor(a / c) + 1;        // Archimedes: m·c > a
  let r = a;
  while (m > 1) {
    r -= takeMoreThanHalf(r);           // r shrinks by more than half …
    m -= 1;                             // … m·c by at most half
  }
  return r;                             // r < c
}
```
The loop runs a fixed number of times, known in advance: $m - 1$ where $mc > a$. This is the ε–N argument of analysis in its first recorded form: given any $c$ (an ε), there is a step (an N) after which the remainder is below it.
:::

:::gap What is assumed
Euclid cites nothing, but the first step is the Archimedean property, stated in Book V only as a definition ([[5.def.4]]). In a non-Archimedean ordered field (one with infinitesimals) the lemma fails: halving $1$ again and again never gets below an infinitesimal $\varepsilon$. So X.1 is exactly as strong as the Archimedean assumption, and is equivalent to it.
:::

::exhaustion

:::leads Where it leads
This is the engine of the *method of exhaustion*. [[12.2]] shows that circles are as the squares on their diameters: inscribed polygons with doubled numbers of sides take away more than half of what is left of the circle each time (the widget above), so by X.1 the difference between circle and polygon falls below any given area, and a double reductio finishes the proof. The same lemma drives the volumes of pyramids and cones in [[12.5]] and [[12.10]], and Archimedes' quadrature of the parabola. Today it is the statement that $2^{-n} \to 0$, or that a geometric series with ratio below $\tfrac12$ has terms tending to 0.
:::
