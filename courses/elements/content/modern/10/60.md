---
title: The square of a binomial, applied to a rational line, gives a first binomial
---
**Statement.** Let @AB $= \sqrt a + \sqrt b$ be a binomial, cut at @C into its terms. Apply $AB^2$ to a rational line @DE $= 1$. The breadth @DG is a first binomial. In algebra:
$$(\sqrt a + \sqrt b)^2 = (a + b) + 2\sqrt{ab},$$
and $(a + b)^2 - 4ab = (a - b)^2$ is a square. The default $\sqrt5 + \sqrt2$ gives $DG = 7 + 2\sqrt{10}$. This is the converse of [[10.54]].

**Proof.** Apply @figure(DH) $= AC^2$ and @figure(KL) $= CB^2$, so the rest, @figure(MF), is $2\,AC\cdot CB$; bisect it by @NO.

- @figure(DL) $= AC^2 + CB^2$ is rational, so its breadth @DM is rational and commensurable with $DE$ ([[10.20]]).
- $MF = 2\,AC \cdot CB$ is medial ([[10.21]]), so @MG is rational and incommensurable in length with $DE$ ([[10.22]]), hence with $DM$. So $DG$ is a binomial.
- $DM > MG$, since $AC^2 + CB^2 > 2\,AC\cdot CB$ (the lemma after [[10.59]]).
- $AC\cdot CB$ is the mean proportional between $AC^2$ and $CB^2$, so $DK \cdot KM = MN^2 = \tfrac14 MG^2$. And $DK$, $KM$ are commensurable, because $AC^2$, $CB^2$ are. By [[10.17]], $\sqrt{DM^2 - MG^2}$ is commensurable with $DM$.

The greater term $DM$ is commensurable with $DE$: a first binomial.

**The pattern.** [[10.61]]–[[10.65]] repeat this for the other five additive irrationals. What decides the order is visible in the algebra: $\sqrt{DM^2 - MG^2} = DK - KM = AC^2 - CB^2$, which is commensurable with $DM = AC^2 + CB^2$ exactly when $AC^2$ and $CB^2$ are commensurable.
