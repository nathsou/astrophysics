---
title: Greatest common measure of three magnitudes
---
**Statement.** Three commensurable magnitudes $a, b, c$ have a greatest common measure, and every common measure divides it. The same holds for any number of magnitudes.

**Proof.** As for numbers ([[7.3]]): $\gcd(a, b, c) = \gcd(\gcd(a, b), c)$. Let @D be the greatest common measure of @A and @B ([[10.3]]). If $D$ measures @C, it is the answer. If not, $C$ and $D$ are still commensurable, since any common measure of $A, B, C$ measures $D$ by the porism of [[10.3]]. Let @E be their greatest common measure. It measures $D$, hence $A$ and $B$, and it measures $C$. Any common measure @F of all three measures $D$ and $C$, hence $E$, so it cannot be greater than $E$.

In the figure the three magnitudes are $a, b, c$ times a common length, and $E$ is $\gcd(a, b, c)$ times it.
