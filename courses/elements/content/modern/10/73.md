---
title: The apotome
---
**Statement.** Let @AB and @BC be rational lines, commensurable in square only, with $BC < AB$. Take $BC$ away from $AB$. The remainder @AC is irrational; Euclid calls it an **apotome** (from the Greek for "cut off"). In algebra, with $\rho = 1$:
$$\sqrt a - \sqrt b, \qquad a > b \text{ positive rationals}, \ \sqrt{a/b} \text{ irrational}.$$
$AB$ is the *whole* and $BC$ the *annex*: the line that must be annexed to the apotome to restore a rational whole. Examples: $\sqrt5 - \sqrt2$ (the figure's default), $3 - \sqrt5$, and the golden section $\tfrac{\sqrt5 - 1}{2}$ (see below).

The apotome is the binomial ([[10.36]]) with a minus sign, and [[10.73]]–[[10.78]] repeat [[10.36]]–[[10.41]] with subtraction: from each additive irrational $x + y$ comes a subtractive one $x - y$ with the same two parts.

**Proof (Euclid's).** Everything is done with the squares and the rectangle.

1. $AB$ is incommensurable in length with $BC$, and $AB^2 : AB \cdot BC = AB : BC$, so $AB^2$ is incommensurable with $AB \cdot BC$ ([[10.11]]).
2. $AB^2 + BC^2$ is commensurable with $AB^2$ ([[10.15]]), and $2\,AB \cdot BC$ with $AB \cdot BC$ ([[10.6]]). So $AB^2 + BC^2$ is incommensurable with $2\,AB\cdot BC$ ([[10.13]]).
3. By [[2.7]], $AB^2 + BC^2 = 2\,AB\cdot BC + AC^2$. If a sum is incommensurable with one of its parts, it is incommensurable with the other ([[10.16]]). So $AB^2 + BC^2$ is incommensurable with $AC^2$.
4. $AB^2 + BC^2$ is rational, so $AC^2$ is not, and $AC$ is irrational ([[10.def1.4]]).

In algebra, $AC^2 = (a + b) - 2\sqrt{ab}$: a rational number minus an irrational one. The figure checks step 3 exactly: the readout gives $AC^2$ as a surd, and the claim tests that its ratio to $AB^2 + BC^2$ is not rational.

:::code Binomials and apotomes are conjugates
```ts
// both stored by the squares of their terms: a > b, a/b not a square
type Binomial = { a: Rational; b: Rational }; // √a + √b
type Apotome  = { a: Rational; b: Rational }; // √a − √b: whole √a, annex √b

const conjugate = (x: Binomial): Apotome => ({ a: x.a, b: x.b });
// (√a + √b)(√a − √b) = a − b, a rational number
```
The map is well defined only because the terms are unique: [[10.42]] for binomials, [[10.79]] for apotomes. Multiplying by the conjugate turns an irrational into a rational number, the fact behind [[10.112]] and [[10.113]], and behind rationalizing denominators: $\frac{1}{\sqrt5 + \sqrt2} = \frac{\sqrt5 - \sqrt2}{3}$.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
Book X names the apotome because Book XIII needs it. If a rational line is cut in extreme and mean ratio, both segments are apotomes ([[13.6]]): for a line of length 1 the greater segment is $\tfrac{\sqrt5 - 1}{2}$, whole $\tfrac{\sqrt5}{2}$ and annex $\tfrac12$. The side of the dodecahedron in a sphere of rational diameter is an apotome too ([[13.17]]), while the pentagon's side ([[13.11]]) and the icosahedron's ([[13.16]]) are minors ([[10.76]]). In modern terms, all of these lengths live in quadratic extensions of the rationals, and Book X is a classification of the simplest elements of those fields.
:::
