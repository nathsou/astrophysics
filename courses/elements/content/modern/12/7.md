---
title: A pyramid is a third of its prism
---
**Statement.** A triangular prism splits into three pyramids of equal volume. Hence (the porism) every pyramid is one third of the prism with the same base and height:
$$V_{\text{pyramid}} = \tfrac13\, B h.$$

**Proof.** In the prism @solid(ABCDEF), with $D, E, F$ over $A, B, C$, draw the diagonals @BD, @EC and @CD of the side faces. They cut the prism into three pyramids:

- base @triangle(ABD), vertex @C;
- base @triangle(EBC), vertex @D (the same solid as base $DEB$, vertex $C$);
- base @triangle(ECF), vertex @D.

The diagonal @BD halves the parallelogram $ABED$ ([[1.34]]), so the first two pyramids stand on equal bases $ABD$, $EBD$ with the common vertex $C$; by [[12.5]] they are equal. Likewise @CE halves $FCBE$, so the pyramids on $BCE$ and $ECF$ with the common vertex $D$ are equal. All three are equal, and each is a third of the prism. The first one is also the pyramid with base $ABC$ and vertex $D$, which proves the porism for triangular bases; a polygonal base is cut into triangles.

**In the figure.** Slant the prism, change its height, and pull the three pieces apart: their volumes stay equal, even though they are not congruent (in general no two of them are).

:::gap Why not cut and rearrange?
In Book I, Euclid compares areas by cutting: a triangle is half a parallelogram because two copies of it make one. The natural hope is that the three pyramids could be cut into finitely many pieces and rearranged into one another, which would prove the porism without [[12.5]] and so without exhaustion. For polygons this always works: any two polygons of equal area can be cut into the same finite set of pieces (the Wallace–Bolyai–Gerwien theorem). In 1900 Hilbert asked, as the third of his famous problems, whether the same is true for polyhedra. Max Dehn answered no in the same year. He attached to every polyhedron an invariant, computed from its edge lengths and dihedral angles, that cutting and reassembling cannot change; a regular tetrahedron and a cube of equal volume have different invariants. So the volume of a pyramid cannot, in general, be found by dissection alone. Some limiting process, here the method of exhaustion, is unavoidable.
:::

:::history
Archimedes, in the preface to *On the Sphere and Cylinder*, says that Eudoxus first proved that a pyramid is a third of the prism and a cone a third of the cylinder on the same base and height, and elsewhere that Democritus had stated these facts without proof.
:::
