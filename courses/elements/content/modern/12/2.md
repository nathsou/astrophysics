---
title: Circles are as the squares on their diameters
---
**Statement.** For any two circles with diameters $d$ and $d'$,
$$\text{circle} : \text{circle}' = d^2 : d'^2.$$
In modern terms, the area of a circle is $k\,d^2$ with the *same* constant $k$ for every circle. Euclid does not say what $k$ is (it is $\pi/4$), and his method cannot find it.

**The difficulty.** [[12.1]] proves the proportion for similar polygons inscribed in the circles. A circle is not a polygon, and Euclid has no limits: there is no "polygon with infinitely many sides". He needs a proof that uses only finite polygons.

**The squeeze.** First, a circle can be exhausted by polygons. Inscribe the square @figure(EFGH) in @circle(EFGH): it is more than half the circle, because it is half the circumscribed square. Bisect each arc at @K, @L, @M, @N and add the triangles such as @triangle(EKF). Each triangle is more than half of the segment of the circle it stands on, because it is half of the rectangle on its base that encloses the segment. So each doubling of the sides removes more than half of what is left of the circle. By [[10.1]], repeating this leaves less than any given area.

**Proof by double reductio.** Suppose $d^2 : d'^2 = \text{circle}\ ABCD : S$ for some area $S$. Euclid shows that $S$ can be neither less nor greater than @circle(EFGH).

1. *$S$ is not less.* If it were, the circle would exceed $S$ by some positive amount $\varepsilon$. Exhaust @circle(EFGH) until the leftover segments are less than $\varepsilon$; the inscribed polygon @figure(EKFLGMHN) is then greater than $S$. Inscribe the similar polygon @figure(AOBPCQDR) in @circle(ABCD). By [[12.1]] and the hypothesis,
$$AOBPCQDR : EKFLGMHN = d^2 : d'^2 = \text{circle}\ ABCD : S,$$
and alternating ([[5.16]]),
$$\text{circle}\ ABCD : AOBPCQDR = S : EKFLGMHN.$$ The left ratio is of a greater to a less, so $S > EKFLGMHN$. Contradiction.
2. *$S$ is not greater.* If it were, invert the proportion and use the lemma (a fourth proportional exists, and is less than @circle(ABCD)) to get a proportion of the forbidden kind of case 1 with the circles swapped.

So $S$ is the circle $EFGH$ itself.

::exhaustion

:::leads Where it leads
This is the ancestor of the limit. Read "for every $\varepsilon > 0$ the polygon comes within $\varepsilon$ of the circle" and "a quantity that is neither less nor greater than $S$ is $S$", and you have the $\varepsilon$-argument that Cauchy and Weierstrass made the foundation of analysis in the nineteenth century. The difference is that Euclid never forms an infinite sequence or its limit. Every step handles one finite polygon, and infinity is avoided entirely: the reductio does the work that a limit would do. The same scheme proves [[12.5]], [[12.10]]–[[12.12]] and [[12.18]].
:::

:::code The shape of the argument
The proof is a proof that two reals are equal by excluding both strict inequalities, with each exclusion reduced to a finite search:

```ts
// To refute S < circle: find a polygon that beats S.
function witness(circle: number, S: number) {
  let sides = 4;
  // X.1 guarantees that this loop ends
  while (circle - inscribed(sides) >= circle - S) sides *= 2;
  return sides; // this polygon is > S: contradiction
}
```

The loop terminates because the gap more than halves at every doubling. That termination is exactly [[10.1]], and in turn the Archimedean property of the magnitudes ([[5.def.4]]).

:::history
Eudemus, as reported by later commentators, credited Hippocrates of Chios with showing that circles are as the squares on their diameters, in the fifth century BC. The proof by exhaustion given here is generally attributed to Eudoxus. Archimedes later found the constant: a circle equals the right triangle whose legs are the radius and the circumference, and the ratio of circumference to diameter lies between $3\tfrac{10}{71}$ and $3\tfrac{1}{7}$.
:::
