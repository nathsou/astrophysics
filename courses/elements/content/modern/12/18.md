---
title: Spheres are as the cubes of their diameters
---
**Statement.** Spheres are to one another in the triplicate ratio of their diameters:
$$\text{sphere } ABC : \text{sphere } DEF = (BC : EF)^3.$$
In modern terms, $V = k\,d^3$ with the same constant $k$ for every sphere.

**Proof.** The last and hardest exhaustion of the book, with the same shape as [[12.2]]. Suppose the sphere @solid(ABC) has the ratio $(BC : EF)^3$ to a sphere @solid(GHK) less than @solid(DEF). Place $GHK$ concentric with $DEF$ and inscribe in $DEF$ a polyhedron that misses $GHK$ ([[12.17]]). Inscribe a similar polyhedron in $ABC$. By the porism of [[12.17]], the polyhedra are in the ratio $(BC : EF)^3$, the same as $ABC : GHK$. Alternating,
$$ABC : \text{polyhedron in } ABC = GHK : \text{polyhedron in } DEF.$$
The left ratio is of a greater to a less, so $GHK$ is greater than the polyhedron in $DEF$. But the polyhedron encloses $GHK$. Contradiction. The case of a greater sphere @solid(LMN) reduces to this one with the spheres exchanged, by the lemma of [[12.2]].

**In the figure.** The polyhedra of [[12.17]] are drawn in both spheres; the second slider refines them. Their volumes are always exactly in the ratio of the cubes of the diameters, and they fill more and more of their spheres. The dashed spheres are the supposed $GHK$ and $LMN$; with enough divisions the polyhedron in $DEF$ is larger than $GHK$, which is the contradiction.

:::leads Where it leads
Euclid ends the book knowing that $V = k d^3$ but not the value of $k$. He never finds $\pi$, and the Elements contains no formula for the area of a circle, the surface of a sphere or its volume. Archimedes, a generation or two later, found all of them. In *On the Sphere and Cylinder* he proved that a sphere is two thirds of the cylinder circumscribed about it, so $V = \tfrac23 \cdot \pi r^2 \cdot 2r = \tfrac43 \pi r^3$ and $k = \pi/6$, and that its surface is four times a great circle. According to Plutarch, he asked for a sphere in a cylinder to be placed on his tomb. His proofs use the same double reductio as Book XII, with better-chosen approximating figures.
:::

:::gap Existence of the fourth proportional
The reductio starts from a magnitude $GHK$ with $ABC : GHK = (BC : EF)^3$. Euclid assumes that such a fourth proportional exists, here and in [[12.2]], [[12.5]], [[12.11]] and [[12.12]], and here he even assumes it is a sphere. That amounts to a sphere of any given volume, and its existence is a continuity assumption, in the spirit of the circle–circle intersection of [[1.1]]. With real numbers it is immediate: take the diameter $\sqrt[3]{V/k}$.
:::
