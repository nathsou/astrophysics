---
title: A cone is a third of its cylinder
---
**Statement.** A cone is one third of the cylinder with the same base and height:
$$V_{\text{cone}} = \tfrac13 V_{\text{cylinder}}.$$

**Proof.** The polygonal version is already known: a pyramid on a polygon is a third of the prism on the same polygon ([[12.7]], porism). Exhaustion passes it to the circle.

*Exhaustion.* Inscribe the square @figure(ABCD) in the base @circle(ABCD). The prism on it is more than half the cylinder, since it is half the prism on the circumscribed square, which contains the cylinder. Bisecting the arcs at @E, @F, @G, @H and building prisms on the new triangles @triangle(AEB), @triangle(BFC), … removes more than half of each leftover piece of the cylinder, because each such prism is half of a box that contains the piece. By [[10.1]] the prism on some inscribed polygon comes as close to the cylinder as we like. The same holds for pyramids inside the cone.

*Double reductio.*

1. Suppose the cylinder were more than three cones. Exhaust the cylinder until the prism on the polygon (the octagon @figure(AEBFCGDH), say) exceeds three cones. The pyramid on the same polygon is a third of the prism, so it exceeds the cone. But the pyramid is inside the cone. Contradiction.
2. Suppose the cylinder were less than three cones, so that the cone is more than a third of the cylinder. Exhaust the cone until the inscribed pyramid exceeds a third of the cylinder. Then the prism on the same polygon, three times the pyramid, exceeds the cylinder that contains it. Contradiction.

So the cylinder is exactly three cones.

Unlike [[12.2]], this proof compares a curved solid with a fixed multiple of another, rather than two figures of the same kind, and the two halves need different exhaustions: one of the cylinder, one of the cone.

:::leads Where it leads
With the cylinder's volume $\pi r^2 h$, this gives $\tfrac13 \pi r^2 h$ for the cone. Euclid has neither formula: the constant $\pi$ does not appear in the Elements. The relation "one third" belongs to every cone over every region, as Cavalieri's principle (1635) and then integration showed: $\int_0^h A (x/h)^2\,dx = \tfrac13 A h$ for any base area $A$.
:::
