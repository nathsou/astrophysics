---
title: A polyhedron between two concentric spheres
---
**Statement.** Given two concentric spheres, one can inscribe in the greater a polyhedron that does not touch the lesser. The porism: similar polyhedra inscribed in two spheres are in the triplicate ratio of the diameters.

**Construction.** Cut both spheres by a plane through the centre @A, giving great circles @circle(BCDE) and @circle(FGH). Inscribe in @circle(BCDE) the polygon of [[12.16]] that misses @circle(FGH); in the quadrant $BE$ its sides are @BK, @KL, @LM, @ME. Through the pole @O and each vertex, draw a great semicircle such as @circle(BOD) and @circle(KON), and divide each quadrant from the equator to the pole into as many equal parts: @P, @Q, @R on $BO$, @S, @T, @U on $KO$. Joining neighbours gives a globe-like polyhedron, with faces such as the quadrilaterals @figure(KBPS), @figure(SPQT), @figure(TQRU) and the triangle @triangle(URO) at the pole.

**Proof.** Two things need showing.

1. *The faces are plane.* The perpendiculars @PV and @SW to the equator plane are equal, so @WV is parallel to @KB and equal to @SP; hence $SP \parallel KB$ and $KBPS$ lies in one plane ([[11.7]]).
2. *The faces miss the lesser sphere.* Let @X be the foot of the perpendicular from @A to the plane of $KBPS$. It is equidistant from $K, B, P, S$, so $KBPS$ is inscribed in a circle of radius @BX. Euclid shows $KB^2 > 2\,BX^2$ (three equal sides and a shorter fourth) and $KB^2 < 2\,KZ^2$, where @Z is the foot of the perpendicular from @K to @BD. So $KZ > BX$, and by Pythagoras in the right triangles at $X$ and $Z$, $AX > AZ$. By the construction of [[12.16]], the chord through $K$ perpendicular to $BD$ misses the lesser circle, so $AZ > AG$, and $AX > AZ > AG$: the face is farther from the centre than the lesser sphere.

The porism follows from [[12.8]]: similar polyhedra split into similar pyramids from the centre, each in the triplicate ratio of the radii.

**In the figure.** The shaded faces are the four in the lune between the quadrants $BO$ and $KO$; the thin lines show the rest of the upper half of the polyhedron. The first slider brings the lesser sphere closer; the readouts check that the faces still miss it.

:::gap A generous construction
Euclid proves only that *this* face misses the lesser sphere, and concludes it for the whole polyhedron. The faces nearest the pole are different in shape (the last one is a triangle), and their distance from the centre is not the same as that of $KBPS$. The claim is true, as the figure checks for all the faces in the lune, but the text does not prove it for each face. (It holds because the faces nearer the pole have smaller circumscribed circles, and the plane of a smaller circle on the sphere lies farther from the centre.)
:::
