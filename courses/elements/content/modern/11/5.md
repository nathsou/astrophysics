---
title: The perpendiculars at a point form a plane
---
**Statement.** If a line @AB is perpendicular at @B to three lines @BC, @BD, @BE, the three lines lie in one plane.

This is the converse of [[11.4]]. Together they say that the lines perpendicular to @AB at @B fill out exactly one plane.

**Proof (Euclid's).** Suppose @BD and @BE lie in the plane of reference and @BC rises above it. The plane through @AB and @BC meets the plane of reference in a line @BF ([[11.3]]). By [[11.4]], @AB is perpendicular to the plane of reference, so $\angle ABF$ is right. But $\angle ABC$ is right by hypothesis, and both angles lie in the one plane $ABF$ with $C$ and $F$ on the same side. A part would equal the whole, which is impossible. In the figure, @BC is drawn as it would have to be, and $\angle ABC$ falls short of $\angle ABF$.

:::code A kernel is a plane
The set $\{x : a \cdot x = 0\}$ for a nonzero vector $a$ is the kernel of a nonzero linear functional on $\mathbb{R}^3$. By rank–nullity it has dimension $3 - 1 = 2$: it is a plane. Three vectors in it are linearly dependent, which is what "the three lines are in one plane" means.
:::
