---
title: A line joining two parallels lies in their plane
---
**Statement.** If @AB and @CD are parallel and @E, @F are points on them, the line @EF lies in the plane of the parallels.

**Proof (Euclid's).** If not, let it rise above the plane, as the dashed $EGF$. The plane through $EGF$ meets the plane of the parallels in a straight line from @E to @F ([[11.3]]). Then two straight lines join @E to @F and enclose an area, which is absurd.

This is [[11.1]] again, in a special case. It is needed because parallel lines are defined as lines *in one plane* that do not meet ([[1.def.23]]), so every argument about parallels in space must first check that the lines involved are coplanar. [[11.8]] uses it this way.

:::code Check
The figure computes the triple product $\big((B-A) \times (C-A)\big) \cdot (F - E)$, which is zero exactly when $E$, $F$, $A$, $B$, $C$ are coplanar.
:::
