---
title: Two intersecting lines determine a plane
---
**Statement.** Two lines that cut one another lie in one plane, and so does every triangle.

**Proof (Euclid's).** Let @AB and @CD cut at @E, and consider the triangle $ECB$. If part of it (say the corner $FHC$, or the strip $FCBG$) were in one plane and the rest in another, then part of @EC or @EB would be in one plane and part in another, contradicting [[11.1]]. So the triangle lies in one plane, and with it the lines $EC$, $EB$, hence $AB$ and $CD$ ([[11.1]] again).

:::gap Not really a proof
The argument assumes what it sets out to prove: it takes for granted that the triangle lies in *some* plane, and only shows that it cannot be split between two. What is really needed is an existence statement, and Hilbert takes it as an axiom (I.4): any three points not on a line lie in exactly one plane.
:::

:::code The plane through three points
In coordinates the plane through $A$, $B$, $C$ has normal $n = (B - A) \times (C - A)$, which is nonzero exactly when the points are not collinear. Two lines through $E$ with directions $u$, $w$ lie in the plane $\{E + su + tw\}$. The figure checks that every point it draws lies in it (all the triple products vanish).
:::
