---
title: A parallelepiped cut parallel to two faces
---
**Statement.** If a parallelepiped is cut by a plane @plane(FG) parallel to two opposite faces, the two parts are to each other as their bases:
$$\text{solid } AU : \text{solid } UH = \text{base } AF : \text{base } FH.$$

This is the solid version of [[6.1]] (triangles and parallelograms of the same height are as their bases), and the proof is the same.

**Proof (Euclid's).** Lay off $AE$ any number of times along the line to the left (@K, @L) and $EH$ any number of times to the right (@M, @N), and complete the solids on the new bases. Solids on equal bases in this row are equal, because their faces are equal and similar ([[11.24]], [[11.def.10]]). So whatever multiple the base @figure(LF) is of @figure(AF), the solid $LU$ is the same multiple of the solid $AU$, and likewise on the right. If base $LF$ is greater than, equal to or less than base $NF$, the same holds for the solids $LU$ and $NU$. That is exactly Eudoxus' definition of equal ratios ([[5.def.5]]).

:::code Homogeneity without real numbers
With volume as a function $V(a, b, c)$ of the three edge vectors, the proposition says $V(\lambda a, b, c) = \lambda\, V(a, b, c)$ for every $\lambda > 0$, rational or not. For whole numbers this is additivity: $\lambda$ copies side by side. The step to arbitrary $\lambda$ is where one usually invokes continuity or monotonicity. Euclid invokes [[5.def.5]]: to compare two ratios it is enough to compare all their integer multiples, and multiples are just more copies. The figure uses three copies on each side.
:::

:::gap Equal faces, equal solids
The step "the solids have equal faces, so they are equal" appeals to [[11.def.10]], which is not reliable in general (see there). Here it is harmless: the solids in the row are translates of one another.
:::
