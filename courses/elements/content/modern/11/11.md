---
title: Dropping a perpendicular to a plane
---
**Statement.** From a point @A outside a plane, draw the perpendicular to the plane.

**Construction.**

1. In the plane take any line @BC, and drop the perpendicular @AD from @A to it ([[1.12]]).
2. In the plane, draw @DE perpendicular to @BC at @D ([[1.11]]).
3. Drop the perpendicular @AF from @A to @DE ([[1.12]]).

Then @AF is perpendicular to the plane. (If @AD already is, stop at step 1.)

**Why it works.** @BC is perpendicular to @DA and to @DE, so it is perpendicular to the plane $ADE$ ([[11.4]]). Draw @GH through @F parallel to @BC. Then @GH is also perpendicular to the plane $ADE$ ([[11.8]]), which contains @AF, so $AF \perp GH$. Also $AF \perp DE$ by construction. So @AF is perpendicular to two lines of the plane through @F, hence to the plane ([[11.4]]).

Every step is a plane construction: the three perpendiculars are each drawn in a plane that contains everything needed.

:::code Projection in two steps
In coordinates the foot is $F = A - \frac{(A - O)\cdot n}{n \cdot n}\, n$ for any point $O$ of the plane and a normal $n$. Euclid has no normal to hand, so he builds $F$ from two one-dimensional projections along orthogonal directions of the plane:
```ts
const D = projectOntoLine(A, B, C);   // step 1
const e = perpendicularInPlane(C - B); // step 2: direction of DE
const F = projectOntoLine(A, D, D + e); // step 3
```
This is Gram–Schmidt in miniature: project onto an orthogonal basis of the plane, one vector at a time. The figure checks $\vec{AF}\cdot\vec{DE} = \vec{AF}\cdot\vec{GH} = 0$ as you move @A and @BC.
:::
