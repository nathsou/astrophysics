---
title: A line lies wholly in a plane or meets it once
---
**Statement.** A straight line cannot have one part in a plane and another part outside it. If a segment of a line lies in a plane, the whole line does.

**Proof (Euclid's).** Suppose @AB lies in the plane of reference and the continuation @BC rises above it. Produce @AB within the plane to @D. Then @AB is a common segment of two different straight lines, $ABC$ and $ABD$. Euclid rules this out by a picture: the circle with centre @B through @A would be cut by the "diameters" $ABC$ and $ABD$ into unequal parts. In the figure, $\angle ABD = 180^\circ$ but $\angle ABC$ is not.

:::gap An axiom in disguise
The proof uses two facts that Euclid never states: a line in a plane can be produced *within the plane*, and two distinct straight lines cannot share a segment. The second was sometimes added to the Elements as an extra postulate. Hilbert makes the whole proposition an axiom (I.6): if two points of a line lie in a plane, every point of the line lies in it.
:::

:::code In coordinates
A plane is $\{X : n \cdot X = c\}$ and a line is $\{A + t(B - A)\}$. The function $f(t) = n \cdot (A + t(B-A)) - c$ is affine in $t$. If it vanishes at two values of $t$, it vanishes everywhere. So a line meets a plane in no point, in one point, or entirely.
:::
