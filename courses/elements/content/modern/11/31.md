---
title: Equal bases and equal heights give equal volumes
---
**Statement.** Parallelepipeds @solid(AE) and @solid(CF) of the same height on equal bases @figure(AB) and @figure(CD) are equal.

The bases need only be equal in *area*: they may have different shapes. With [[11.29]] and [[11.30]], this makes volume a function of base area and height alone, and [[11.32]] shows that it is proportional to each.

**Proof (Euclid's), when the edges are perpendicular to the bases.**

1. *Copy the first base next to the second.* Produce @CR to @T with $RT = AL$, make $\angle TRU = \angle ALB$ with $RU = LB$, and complete the parallelogram @figure(RW) and the solid $XU$ on it. It is congruent to @solid(AE) ([[11.def.10]]).
2. *Shear it.* Produce @DR and @WU to meet at @Y. The solid $YX$ on the base @figure(YT) is obtained from $XU$ by a shear (same base face $RX$, tops on the same lines), so $YX = XU = AE$ ([[11.29]]).
3. *Compare bases in the plane.* $RUWT = YT$ ([[1.35]]), and $RUWT = AB = CD$, so $YT = CD$.
4. *Compare solids with ratios.* Let $RI$ be the solid on the base @figure(DT). By [[11.25]], $CF : RI = CD : DT$ and $YX : RI = YT : DT$. The right-hand sides are equal, so $CF = YX$ ([[5.9]]), and $CF = AE$.

When the edges are oblique, drop perpendiculars from the top vertices to get upright solids on the same bases. They are equal to the oblique ones by [[11.29]] and [[11.30]], and to each other by the first case. (The text reuses several letters for this second case; the figure shows the first.)

:::code Volume is the determinant
Write a parallelepiped as its three edge vectors $a, b, c$ from one vertex. Its volume is
$$V = |\det[a\ b\ c]| = |(a \times b) \cdot c| = |a \times b| \cdot h,$$
base area times height, where $h$ is the component of $c$ along the unit normal of the base. Book XI proves the properties that characterize this function, one proposition at a time:

| property | determinant | Euclid |
|---|---|---|
| unchanged by adding a multiple of one edge to another | $\det[a, b, c + \lambda b] = \det[a,b,c]$ | [[11.29]], [[11.30]] |
| proportional to each edge | $\det[\lambda a, b, c] = \lambda \det[a,b,c]$ | [[11.25]], [[11.32]] |
| unchanged by rigid motion (and reflection) | $\lvert\det Q\rvert = 1$ for orthogonal $Q$ | [[11.def.10]], [[11.28]] |

Up to a constant factor, $|\det|$ is the only function with these properties. Euclid never multiplies an area by a length: every comparison is a ratio of like magnitudes, so "base times height" appears only as a proportion.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
These are the tools Book XII needs. There, triangular prisms are split into three pyramids of equal volume ([[12.7]]), which gives the volume of a pyramid. That step cannot be done by cutting and pasting: Dehn showed in 1900 (Hilbert's third problem) that a regular tetrahedron cannot be cut into finitely many pieces and reassembled into a cube. This is why Book XII needs the method of exhaustion, while parallelepipeds and prisms can be handled, as here, by shears and ratios.
:::
