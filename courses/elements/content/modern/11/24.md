---
title: Opposite faces of a parallelepiped
---
**Statement.** If a solid is bounded by three pairs of parallel planes, its opposite faces are equal parallelograms.

This solid is the *parallelepiped*, which Euclid uses from here to the end of the book without defining it. The figure labels it with the bottom face $ABCD$ and the top face $GHFE$, with @G above @A, @H above @B, @F above @C and @E above @D.

**Proof (Euclid's).** The parallel planes @plane(BG) and @plane(CE) are cut by the plane @plane(AC) in parallel lines ([[11.16]]), so $AB \parallel DC$. Likewise $BC \parallel AD$, so @plane(AC) is a parallelogram; and so is every other face. Next, $AB \parallel DC$ and $BH \parallel CF$, so $\angle ABH = \angle DCF$ ([[11.10]]). With $AB = DC$ and $BH = CF$ ([[1.34]]), the triangles $ABH$ and $DCF$ are congruent (SAS). The faces @plane(BG) and @plane(CE) are twice these triangles, so they are equal.

:::code In vectors
A parallelepiped is $\{O + s\,a + t\,b + u\,c : s, t, u \in [0,1]\}$. Its opposite faces are translates of each other by $a$, $b$ or $c$: for example the face $u = 1$ is the face $u = 0$ moved by $c$. Translations preserve shape and area. The areas are $|a \times b|$, $|b \times c|$ and $|c \times a|$, each occurring twice, and the figure checks the three equalities.
:::
