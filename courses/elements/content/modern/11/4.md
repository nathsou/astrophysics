---
title: Perpendicular to two lines, perpendicular to the plane
---
**Statement.** Let @AB and @CD meet at @E, and let @EF be perpendicular to both at @E. Then @EF is perpendicular to every line through @E in their plane, so it is perpendicular to the plane ([[11.def.3]]).

This is the working criterion for perpendicularity to a plane. The definition asks for right angles with infinitely many lines; the theorem says two are enough.

**Proof (Euclid's).** Cut off $AE = EB = CE = ED$ and draw any line @GH through @E, meeting @AD at @G and @CB at @H. Take @F anywhere on the perpendicular.

1. In the plane: $\triangle AED \cong \triangle CEB$ (SAS, [[1.4]], vertical angles [[1.15]]), so $AD = CB$ and $\angle DAE = \angle EBC$. Then $\triangle AGE \cong \triangle BHE$ (ASA, [[1.26]]), so $GE = EH$ and $AG = BH$.
2. Out of the plane: $FA = FB$ and $FC = FD$, by SAS with the right angles at @E.
3. $\triangle FAD \cong \triangle FBC$ (SSS, [[1.8]]), so $\angle FAG = \angle FBH$; then $\triangle FAG \cong \triangle FBH$ (SAS), so $FG = FH$.
4. $\triangle FEG \cong \triangle FEH$ (SSS), so the adjacent angles $\angle FEG$ and $\angle FEH$ are equal, and each is a right angle.

Every triangle in the argument lies in a plane, so Book I applies to it. This is the method of all of Book XI: reduce space to planes.

:::code The proof is one identity
Put $E$ at the origin and write $f$ for the vector @EF. Euclid's points come in opposite pairs, $B = -A$, $C = -D$, $H = -G$, and the whole proof shows that $F$ is equally far from $G$ and $-G$. Now expand:
$$|f - g|^2 - |f + g|^2 = -4\, f \cdot g.$$
So $FG = FH$ says exactly that $f \cdot g = 0$. The hypotheses give $f \cdot a = 0$ and $f \cdot d = 0$, and $g$ is a combination $s\,a + t\,d$, so $f \cdot g = s\,(f\cdot a) + t\,(f\cdot d) = 0$ by linearity. Euclid proves this linearity with four congruences. The figure checks $f \cdot (H - G) = 0$ as you turn @GH.

In practice this is how a normal is found: take two independent directions $u$, $w$ in the plane, and $n = u \times w$ is orthogonal to both, hence to the plane.
:::

:::gap Which lines?
Euclid treats lines through @E that cross the segments @AD and @CB. A line through @E in the other pair of vertical angles crosses @AC and @DB instead, and the same argument works with the letters changed. Lines of the plane that do not pass through @E do not meet @EF at all, so [[11.def.3]] says nothing about them. (With the modern convention that perpendicularity is a relation between directions, they are covered by [[11.10]].)
:::

:::leads Where it leads
[[11.5]] is the converse, and [[11.8]], [[11.11]], [[11.14]] and [[11.15]] use this proposition at their key step. In linear algebra it says that the orthogonal complement of a set of vectors is the orthogonal complement of their span. It is also the idea behind the plane equation $n \cdot X = c$ and behind least-squares projection: the residual is orthogonal to the whole subspace as soon as it is orthogonal to a basis.
:::
