---
title: Two planes meet in a line
---
**Statement.** If two planes cut one another, their common section is a straight line.

**Proof (Euclid's).** Let @D and @B be two points common to the planes @plane(AB) and @plane(BC). Join @D to @B by a straight line in the first plane and by a straight line in the second. If these were different lines ($DEB$ and $DFB$ in the figure, drawn bent because straight they would coincide), two straight lines would enclose an area, which is absurd. So the straight line @DB lies in both planes: it is the common section.

:::gap What is proved and what is not
The argument shows that the line @DB lies in both planes. It does not show that the planes have two common points to begin with, nor that they have no common point off @DB. The first is Hilbert's axiom I.7 (two planes with one common point have another). The second follows from I.5: three non-collinear common points would make the planes coincide.
:::

:::code Solving two linear equations
With $n_1 \cdot X = c_1$ and $n_2 \cdot X = c_2$ and non-parallel normals, the solutions form a line with direction $n_1 \times n_2$: the kernel of a rank-2 linear map from $\mathbb{R}^3$ is one-dimensional. Drag the slider: the section @DB stays in both planes at every angle.
:::
