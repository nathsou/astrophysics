---
title: The face angles of a solid angle sum to less than 360°
---
**Statement.** The plane angles containing a solid angle add up to less than four right angles:
$$\angle BAC + \angle CAD + \angle DAB < 360^\circ.$$

Lower the vertex @A towards the base in the figure: the sum approaches $360^\circ$, and the solid angle flattens into a plane. A solid angle needs some "room" to close up, and this theorem measures it.

**Proof (Euclid's).** Take @B, @C, @D on the three edges and join @BC, @CD, @DB.

1. At @B there is a solid angle contained by $\angle CBA$, $\angle ABD$, $\angle CBD$. By [[11.20]], $\angle CBA + \angle ABD > \angle CBD$. Likewise at @C and @D.
2. Adding the three inequalities: the six angles at the base of the side faces exceed the three angles of the triangle $BCD$, which add up to $180^\circ$ ([[1.32]]).
3. The three side faces $ABC$, $ACD$, $ADB$ are triangles, so their nine angles add up to $3 \cdot 180^\circ = 540^\circ$.
4. The three angles at @A are what is left: $540^\circ$ minus something greater than $180^\circ$, so less than $360^\circ$.

The figure shows the sum and checks the inequality of step 2.

:::gap More than three faces, and convexity
Euclid proves the case of three faces and says "any solid angle". With $n$ faces the same argument works if the base $BCD\ldots$ is a convex polygon: the angle sum of the base is $(n-2)\cdot 180^\circ$, the side triangles contribute $n \cdot 180^\circ$, and the apex angles are less than $n \cdot 180^\circ - (n-2)\cdot 180^\circ = 360^\circ$. The theorem needs the solid angle to be convex. For a non-convex solid angle, with some faces folded inwards, the sum can exceed $360^\circ$.
:::

:::leads Why there are only five regular solids
At each vertex of a regular solid at least three regular polygons meet. The angle of a regular $p$-gon is $180^\circ(1 - 2/p)$. If $q$ of them meet at a vertex, the theorem requires
$$q \cdot 180^\circ \left(1 - \tfrac{2}{p}\right) < 360^\circ \iff (p-2)(q-2) < 4.$$
With $p, q \ge 3$ there are exactly five solutions: $(3,3)$, $(3,4)$, $(3,5)$, $(4,3)$, $(5,3)$. They are the tetrahedron, the octahedron, the icosahedron, the cube and the dodecahedron. This is the argument that closes the Elements ([[13.18]]).
:::

::regular-solids

:::leads Angular defect
The amount by which the angles at a vertex fall short of $360^\circ$ is the *angular defect*. Descartes found that the defects of a convex polyhedron always add up to $720^\circ$, a fact equivalent to Euler's formula $V - E + F = 2$ and a discrete form of the Gauss–Bonnet theorem.
:::
