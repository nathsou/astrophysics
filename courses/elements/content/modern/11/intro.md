Book XI moves from the plane into space. It is the first of three books of solid geometry: Book XII measures pyramids, cones, cylinders and spheres by the method of exhaustion, and Book XIII constructs the five regular solids. Book XI supplies the foundations both of them use.

The 28 definitions come first. They cover perpendicularity and inclination for lines and planes ([[11.def.3]]–[[11.def.8]]), solid angles ([[11.def.11]]), pyramids and prisms, the sphere, cone and cylinder generated by rotation ([[11.def.14]]–[[11.def.24]]), and the regular solids ([[11.def.25]]–[[11.def.28]]).

The propositions fall into four groups.

1. **Lines and planes** ([[11.1]]–[[11.19]]). Incidence facts ([[11.1]]–[[11.3]]), then perpendiculars. The key result is [[11.4]]: a line perpendicular to two intersecting lines of a plane is perpendicular to the whole plane. It is the synthetic form of the fact that a normal vector is orthogonal to a plane as soon as it is orthogonal to two independent directions in it. From it follow the construction of perpendiculars ([[11.11]], [[11.12]]), parallelism in space ([[11.6]]–[[11.10]], [[11.14]]–[[11.16]]), and a three-dimensional theorem of Thales ([[11.17]]).
2. **Solid angles** ([[11.20]]–[[11.26]]). The face angles of a solid angle satisfy a triangle inequality ([[11.20]]) and add up to less than four right angles ([[11.21]]). [[11.23]] shows that these conditions are also sufficient. [[11.21]] is what later limits the regular solids to five ([[13.18]]).
3. **Parallelepipeds** ([[11.24]]–[[11.37]]). This is a theory of volume without numbers. [[11.29]]–[[11.31]] show that volume is unchanged by shearing, the 3D analogue of [[1.35]]–[[1.36]]; [[11.25]] and [[11.32]] that it is proportional to the base; [[11.33]] that similar solids are as the cubes of their sides. Read with coordinates, these are the properties that characterize the determinant.
4. **Two lemmas** ([[11.38]], [[11.39]]) prepared for [[13.17]] and [[12.3]]–[[12.4]].

**What to look out for.** Euclid states no axioms for space. There is no postulate saying that three points determine a plane or that two planes meeting in a point meet in a line, and the first three propositions try to prove what are really axioms. Hilbert's axioms of incidence for space (1899) supply exactly what is missing. From [[11.4]] onwards, though, the reasoning is careful, and most of it reduces space to plane geometry: every argument is carried out in triangles that each lie in a plane.
