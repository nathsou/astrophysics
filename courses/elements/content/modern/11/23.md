---
title: Building a solid angle from three face angles
---
**Statement.** Given three plane angles $\angle ABC$, $\angle DEF$, $\angle GHK$, any two of which together exceed the third and whose sum is less than $360^\circ$, construct a solid angle with these face angles.

So the necessary conditions of [[11.20]] and [[11.21]] are also sufficient.

**Construction (Euclid's).**

1. Make the arms equal and join the chords @AC, @DF, @GK. By [[11.22]] they form a triangle; construct it as $LMN$, with $LM = AC$, $MN = DF$, $NL = GK$.
2. Circumscribe the circle about $LMN$ ([[4.5]]), with centre @O.
3. Show that $AB > LO$ (below).
4. At @O set up @OR perpendicular to the plane of the circle ([[11.12]]), with $OR^2 = AB^2 - LO^2$. A lemma at the end constructs such a length with a semicircle ([[3.31]], [[1.47]]).
5. Then $RL^2 = LO^2 + OR^2 = AB^2$, so $RL = RM = RN = AB$, and $\triangle LRM \cong \triangle ABC$ (SSS), and so on. The solid angle at @R has the three given face angles.

**Why $AB > LO$.** If $AB = LO$, the triangles $ABC$ and $LOM$ would be congruent, and the three given angles would equal the three angles at @O, which fill $360^\circ$. If $AB < LO$, marking $OP = OQ = AB$ on @OL, @OM would give $PQ \parallel LM$ and $PQ < LM = AC$, so each given angle would exceed the corresponding angle at @O, and again the sum would exceed $360^\circ$. Both contradict the hypothesis.

:::gap The position of the centre
The argument that $AB > LO$ uses $\angle LOM + \angle MON + \angle NOL = 360^\circ$. That holds when the centre @O lies inside the triangle $LMN$. If the triangle is right-angled or obtuse, @O lies on a side or outside, and the three angles at @O are related differently: one of them equals the sum of the other two. The conclusion $AB > LO$ is still true, but those cases need their own arguments, which the text omits.
:::

:::code Gram matrices
Three unit vectors $u, v, w$ with pairwise angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ exist in $\mathbb{R}^3$, not coplanar, exactly when the Gram matrix
$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \cos\gamma & \cos\beta \\ \cos\gamma & 1 & \cos\alpha \\ \cos\beta & \cos\alpha & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$
is positive definite. For angles in $(0^\circ, 180^\circ)$ this is equivalent to Euclid's two conditions. His construction is an explicit factorization: the triangle $LMN$ fixes the directions in the base plane, and the height $OR$ is the square root of what is left.
:::
