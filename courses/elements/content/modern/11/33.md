---
title: Similar solids are as the cubes of their sides
---
**Statement.** If @solid(AB) and @solid(CD) are similar parallelepipeds with corresponding sides @AE and @CF, then
$$\text{solid } AB : \text{solid } CD = (AE : CF)^3.$$
Euclid says "the triplicate ratio": the ratio compounded three times ([[5.def.10]]).

**Proof (Euclid's).** At the vertex @E, produce the three edges of @solid(AB) backwards: $EK = CF$, $EL = FN$, $EM = FR$. The solid on $EK, EL, EM$ is equal and similar to @solid(CD). Now change the edges one at a time, through two intermediate solids $EO$ (edges $EG, EK, EH$) and $QL$ (edges $EK, EL, EH$):
$$AB \to EO \to QL \to KP.$$
Each step replaces one edge by the corresponding edge of the other solid, keeping the other two. By [[11.32]] (and [[6.1]] for the bases) each step divides the volume by the ratio of that edge. Because the solids are similar, all three edge ratios are the same ratio $k = AE : CF$. So $AB, EO, QL, KP$ are in continued proportion with ratio $k$, and $AB : KP = k^3$.

:::code Multilinearity
The same proof in one line: $\det[ka,\ kb,\ kc] = k \det[a, kb, kc] = k^2 \det[a, b, kc] = k^3 \det[a, b, c]$. Each equality sign is one of Euclid's intermediate solids. In $\mathbb{R}^n$ the factor is $k^n$.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
The porism turns the theorem around: if $a : b = b : c = c : d$, then a solid on $a$ is to the similar solid on $b$ as $a : d$. To double a cube one needs $a : d = 1 : 2$, that is two mean proportionals between the side and its double. Hippocrates of Chios reduced the doubling of the cube to this problem of finding two mean proportionals.

The same scaling law, volume growing as the cube and area as the square, is the "square–cube law" Galileo used to explain why animals and structures cannot simply be scaled up. Book XII proves the analogous statements for pyramids ([[12.8]]), cones and cylinders ([[12.12]]) and spheres ([[12.18]]).
:::
