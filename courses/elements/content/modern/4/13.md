---
title: The circle inscribed in a regular pentagon
---
**Statement.** In a given regular pentagon, inscribe a circle.

**Construction.** Bisect the angles at @C and @D ([[1.9]]); the bisectors @CF and @DF meet at @F. Drop the perpendiculars @FG, @FH, @FK, @FL, @FM to the five sides.

**Proof.** First, @F lies on the bisectors of all five angles. The triangles $BCF$ and $DCF$ are congruent by SAS ([[1.4]]): $BC = CD$, @CF is common, and the angles at @C are equal. So $\angle CBF = \angle CDF$, which is half the pentagon's angle at @D. The pentagon is equiangular, so this is also half its angle at @B: @BF bisects the angle $ABC$. The same argument works round the pentagon.

Then the perpendiculars are equal: the triangles $FHC$ and $FKC$ have equal angles at @C, right angles at @H and @K, and the common side @FC, so $FH = FK$ ([[1.26]]). In the same way all five are equal, and the circle with centre @F through @G touches all five sides ([[3.16]]).

This is [[4.4]] for the pentagon. The hypothesis matters: a pentagon that is not equiangular need not have an inscribed circle, and here the equal angles are what carry the bisector from @C and @D round to the other vertices.
