---
title: The regular hexagon
---
**Statement.** In a given circle, inscribe a regular hexagon. Its side equals the radius (the porism).

**Construction.** Draw the diameter @AD through the centre @G. The circle with centre @D through @G meets the given circle at @E and @C. Produce @EG and @CG to meet the circle again at @B and @F. Then $ABCDEF$ is the hexagon.

**Proof.** $GE = GD$ (radii of the given circle) and $DE = DG$ (radii of the second circle), so the triangle $EGD$ is equilateral, and each of its angles is a third of two right angles, $60°$ ([[1.5]], [[1.32]]). The same holds for $DGC$. Since @EB is a straight line, the angle $CGB$ is $180° - 60° - 60° = 60°$ as well ([[1.13]]), and the vertical angles $BGA$, $AGF$, $FGE$ equal these three ([[1.15]]). So the six angles at @G are equal, and they stand on six equal arcs cut off by six equal chords ([[3.26]], [[3.29]]).

The hexagon is equiangular because each of its angles stands on four of the six arcs ([[3.27]]).

**Porism.** The side of the hexagon equals the radius. Put another way, the six equilateral triangles of [[1.1]] fit exactly around a point.

**In the figure.** Drag @G or @A. Every side stays equal to the radius.

:::leads Where it leads
Bisecting the arcs gives the 12-gon, 24-gon and so on. Archimedes, in *Measurement of a Circle*, started from the hexagon and doubled four times to the 96-gon, which gave $3\frac{10}{71} < \pi < 3\frac17$. Ptolemy's table of chords in the *Almagest* begins with the sides of the regular polygons of Books IV and XIII: the chord of $60°$ is the radius, by this porism.
:::
