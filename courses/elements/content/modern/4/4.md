---
title: The incircle of a triangle
---
**Statement.** Every triangle has an inscribed circle. Its centre is where the bisectors of the angles meet.

**Construction.** Bisect the angles at @B and @C ([[1.9]]), and let the bisectors meet at @D. Drop the perpendiculars @DE, @DF, @DG to the sides.

**Proof.** The triangles $EBD$ and $FBD$ have equal angles at @B, right angles at @E and @F, and the common side @BD opposite a right angle. So they are congruent (AAS, [[1.26]]) and $DE = DF$. In the same way $DG = DF$. The circle with centre @D through @E passes through @F and @G, and it touches the three sides there, because a line perpendicular to a radius at its end is a tangent ([[3.16]]).

:::gap Two unproved steps
Euclid does not show that the two bisectors meet, nor that they meet *inside* the triangle. They do: the half-angles at @B and @C add up to less than two right angles, so the bisectors meet by Postulate 5 ([[1.post.5]]), and on the same side of @BC as @A. He also leaves out a corollary that his proof contains: since @D is equidistant from @AB and @CA, it lies on the bisector of the angle at @A as well. So *the three angle bisectors of a triangle are concurrent*.
:::

**In the figure.** Drag the vertices. @D stays equidistant from the three sides.

:::leads Where it leads
The incentre is one of the classical centres of a triangle, with the circumcentre ([[4.5]]), the centroid and the orthocentre. In barycentric coordinates it is $(a : b : c)$, the weights being the lengths of the opposite sides; the radius is $r = \text{area}/s$, with $s$ the semiperimeter. [[4.13]] repeats this construction for the regular pentagon.
:::
