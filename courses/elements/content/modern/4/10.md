---
title: The golden triangle
---
**Statement.** Construct an isosceles triangle whose base angles are each double the apex angle. Since the angles add up to $180°$, the apex angle is $36°$ and the base angles $72°$.

**Construction.**

1. Cut @AB at @C so that $AB \cdot BC = AC^2$ (the golden section, [[2.11]]).
2. Draw the circle @circle(BDE) with centre @A through @B, and fit into it the chord @BD equal to @AC ([[4.1]]).
3. Join @AD and @DC, and draw the circle @circle(ACD) through $A, C, D$ ([[4.5]]).

The triangle @triangle(ABD) is the one required.

**Proof.** The whole argument turns on the circle $ACD$.

1. $AB \cdot BC = AC^2 = BD^2$. From the outside point @B, the line @BA cuts the circle $ACD$ at @C and @A, and the rectangle $AB \cdot BC$ equals the square on @BD. By the converse of the tangent–secant theorem ([[3.37]]), @BD *touches* the circle at @D.
2. So the angle between the tangent @BD and the chord @DC equals the angle in the alternate segment: $\angle BDC = \angle DAC$ ([[3.32]]).
3. Add $\angle CDA$: $\angle BDA = \angle CDA + \angle DAC$, which is the exterior angle $\angle BCD$ of the triangle $ACD$ ([[1.32]]).
4. $AB = AD$ (radii), so $\angle BDA = \angle DBA$ ([[1.5]]). Hence $\angle DBC = \angle BCD$, and $DC = BD = CA$ ([[1.6]]).
5. The triangle $ACD$ is then isosceles, so $\angle CDA = \angle DAC$, and the exterior angle $\angle BCD$ is twice $\angle DAC$.

So each base angle, $\angle ABD = \angle ADB = \angle BCD$, is twice the apex angle $\angle BAD$.

**In the figure.** Drag @A and @B. The readouts check the tangency of @BD and the angles, which stay at $36°$ and $72°$.

:::code Where the golden ratio comes from
In the triangle $ABD$, with legs $AB = AD = 1$, the base is $BD = AC = 1/\varphi \approx 0.618$. The small triangle $BCD$ has the same angles ($72°, 72°, 36°$) as $ABD$, so it is a scaled copy, with ratio $1/\varphi$. Cutting the smaller triangle the same way gives a smaller copy again, forever: the self-similarity of $\varphi$ as a picture. Reading off the base, $\cos 72° = \frac{1}{2\varphi} = \frac{\sqrt5 - 1}{4}$, and $\cos 36° = \frac{\varphi}{2}$.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
The diagonals of a regular pentagon cut it into golden triangles and their obtuse companions, with angles $36°, 36°, 108°$: this is how [[4.11]] uses the triangle. The same two triangles are the building blocks of Penrose's non-periodic tilings of the plane.
:::
