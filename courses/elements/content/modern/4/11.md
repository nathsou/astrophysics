---
title: The regular pentagon inscribed in a circle
---
**Statement.** In a given circle, inscribe a regular pentagon: equal sides and equal angles.

**Construction.**

1. Take a golden triangle @triangle(FGH), with angles $36°$ at @F and $72°$ at @G and @H ([[4.10]]).
2. Inscribe in the circle a triangle $ACD$ with the same angles ([[4.2]]): $\angle CAD = 36°$, $\angle ACD = \angle CDA = 72°$.
3. Bisect the angles at @C and @D ([[1.9]]); the bisectors @CE and @DB meet the circle at @E and @B.

Then @A, @B, @C, @D, @E are the vertices of the pentagon.

**Proof.** After the bisection, the five angles $DAC$, $ACE$, $ECD$, $CDB$, $BDA$ are all $36°$. They are inscribed angles standing on the five arcs @arc(AB), @arc(BC), @arc(CD), @arc(DE), @arc(EA), and equal inscribed angles stand on equal arcs ([[3.26]]), which are cut off by equal chords ([[3.29]]). So the pentagon is equilateral.

For the angles: the arc @arc(ABCD) is the arc @arc(AB) plus @arc(BCD), and @arc(EDCB) is @arc(ED) plus the same @arc(BCD). So they are equal, and the angles $AED$ and $BAE$ that stand on them are equal ([[3.27]]). The same holds at every vertex, so the pentagon is equiangular.

The golden triangle does the real work: its angle of $36°$ is exactly one fifth of two right angles, and an inscribed angle of $36°$ cuts off one fifth of the circle.

**In the figure.** Drag @A around the circle, and drag @F and @G to change the golden triangle's size and position: it is always the same shape, so the pentagon does not change.

:::code The pentagon in coordinates
The vertices of the regular pentagon in the unit circle are $\left(\cos \frac{2\pi k}{5}, \sin \frac{2\pi k}{5}\right)$, and
$$\cos \frac{2\pi}{5} = \frac{\sqrt5 - 1}{4}, \qquad \sin \frac{2\pi}{5} = \frac{\sqrt{10 + 2\sqrt5}}{4}.$$
Only square roots appear, which is why a straightedge and compass can do it. Algebraically, $z = e^{2\pi i/5}$ satisfies $z^4 + z^3 + z^2 + z + 1 = 0$, and $w = z + 1/z = 2\cos\frac{2\pi}{5}$ satisfies the quadratic $w^2 + w - 1 = 0$: the equation of the golden section again ([[2.11]]).
:::

**Why this route.** Any construction of the golden section gives the pentagon more directly, by fitting chords equal to the side all round. Euclid's route through [[4.10]] and [[4.2]] is longer, but every step is justified by a proposition already proved. The price is the reliance on [[3.37]], one of the less obvious theorems of Book III.

:::leads Where it leads
[[4.12]]–[[4.14]] do for the pentagon what [[4.3]]–[[4.5]] did for the triangle. [[4.16]] combines the pentagon with the triangle to get the 15-gon. In Book XIII the pentagon's side and diagonal, whose ratio is the golden ratio, are the measurements from which the dodecahedron and icosahedron are built ([[13.16]], [[13.17]]).
:::
