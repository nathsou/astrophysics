---
title: The regular 15-gon
---
**Statement.** In a given circle, inscribe a regular polygon with fifteen sides.

**Construction.** From a point @A of the circle, fit in @AC, the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle, and @AB, the side of an inscribed regular pentagon ([[4.2]], [[4.11]]), on the same side of @A. Bisect the arc between @B and @C at @E ([[3.30]]). Then @BE and @EC are sides of the 15-gon, and fitting chords equal to @BE round the circle ([[4.1]]) completes it.

**Proof.** Count in fifteenths of the circle. The arc @arc(ABC) is a third, that is $\frac{5}{15}$; the arc @arc(AB) is a fifth, $\frac{3}{15}$. So the remaining arc @arc(BC) is $\frac{2}{15}$, and its halves @arc(BE) and @arc(EC) are $\frac{1}{15}$ each. Equal arcs have equal chords, and, as for the pentagon, the polygon is equiangular.

**In the figure.** The equilateral triangle $ACD$ is found as in [[4.15]], with a circle of the same radius centred at the far end of the diameter through @A; @B comes from the pentagon construction of [[4.11]]. Fifteen chords equal to @BE, each fitted after the last, close up exactly at @A: the last readout checks it. Drag @A around the circle.

:::code Bézout, in arcs
The trick is arithmetic: $\frac13 - \frac15 = \frac{2}{15}$. More generally, if $m$ and $n$ are coprime, there are integers with $um + vn = 1$ (Bézout), so
$$\frac{u}{n} + \frac{v}{m} = \frac{um + vn}{mn} = \frac{1}{mn}.$$
If the regular $m$-gon and $n$-gon can be constructed, so can the $mn$-gon: step $u$ sides of the $n$-gon and $v$ sides of the $m$-gon (backwards when negative). For the 15-gon, $2 \cdot 3 - 1 \cdot 5 = 1$ gives $\frac{2}{5} - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{15}$, one fifteenth directly, with no bisection needed. Euclid's route uses $\frac13 - \frac15$ and a bisection instead.
:::

:::leads Gauss, the 17-gon, and which polygons can be constructed
Euclid's methods construct the regular $n$-gon when $n$ is $3$, $4$, $5$ or $15$ times a power of two: the triangle, square, pentagon and 15-gon of this book, and bisection of arcs to double the number of sides. For about two thousand years nothing was added. In 1796 the young Carl Friedrich Gauss showed that the regular 17-gon can be constructed with straightedge and compass, and in his *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801) he gave a general sufficient condition.

**The Gauss–Wantzel theorem.** A regular $n$-gon ($n \ge 3$) can be constructed with straightedge and compass if and only if
$$n = 2^k\, p_1 p_2 \cdots p_m,$$
where $k \ge 0$ and the $p_i$ are *distinct* Fermat primes, primes of the form $2^{2^j} + 1$.

Gauss proved that the condition is sufficient and asserted that it is necessary; Pierre Wantzel published the proof of necessity in 1837. Only five Fermat primes are known: $3, 5, 17, 257, 65537$. Whether there are more is an open problem.

The reason is algebraic. A length constructible with straightedge and compass is obtained from the given ones by $+, -, \times, \div$ and square roots ([[2.14]]), so it lies in a tower of field extensions each of degree 2. The number $\cos\frac{2\pi}{n}$ has degree $\varphi(n)/2$ over the rationals (with $\varphi$ Euler's totient here), and the $n$-gon is constructible exactly when $\varphi(n)$ is a power of two, which gives the condition above. For $n = 17$, $\varphi(17) = 16 = 2^4$, and $\cos\frac{2\pi}{17}$ can be written with nothing but nested square roots.

So the heptagon ($n = 7$) and the 9-gon are impossible: $7$ is not a Fermat prime, and $9 = 3^2$ repeats one. The 9-gon's impossibility is the same fact as the impossibility of trisecting a $60°$ angle.

:::history Why fifteen?
Proclus, in his commentary on Book I, reports that the 15-gon was included for its use in astronomy: the obliquity of the ecliptic, the angle between the equator and the sun's path, was then taken to be about $24°$, which is the arc cut off by the side of the 15-gon.
:::
