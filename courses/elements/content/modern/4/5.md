---
title: The circumcircle of a triangle
---
**Statement.** Every triangle has a circumscribed circle. Its centre is where the perpendicular bisectors of the sides meet.

**Construction.** Bisect @AB at @D and @AC at @E ([[1.10]]), and draw @DF and @EF at right angles to them ([[1.11]]), meeting at @F.

**Proof.** The triangles $ADF$ and $BDF$ have $AD = DB$, the common side @DF and right angles at @D, so $FA = FB$ (SAS, [[1.4]]). In the same way $FC = FA$. The circle with centre @F through @A passes through @B and @C.

Euclid treats three cases: @F inside the triangle, on @BC, or outside it. The proof is the same in each. His closing remark sorts them by the angle at @A: the centre is inside when $\angle BAC$ is acute, on @BC when it is right, and outside when it is obtuse ([[3.31]]).

**In the figure.** Drag @A over the circle's arc until the angle at @A becomes obtuse, and watch @F cross @BC. The last readout checks Euclid's remark in every configuration.

:::gap Do the perpendiculars meet?
The perpendicular bisectors of @AB and @AC meet because @AB and @AC are not parallel: two lines perpendicular to intersecting lines intersect. Euclid does not say so. The argument needs the parallel postulate, and in hyperbolic geometry it fails: some triangles there have no circumscribed circle, because their perpendicular bisectors do not meet. In fact the statement "every triangle has a circumcircle" is equivalent to Postulate 5 (given the other axioms), a result due to Farkas Bolyai.
:::
