Book IV is about fitting figures into circles and circles into figures. Its seven definitions say what "inscribed" and "circumscribed" mean, and its sixteen propositions are all constructions, most of them producing a regular polygon: a polygon with all sides and all angles equal.

It falls into three groups.

- **Triangles and circles** ([[4.1]]–[[4.5]]). A chord of given length ([[4.1]]), a triangle of given shape inscribed in or circumscribed about a circle ([[4.2]], [[4.3]]), and the two circles every triangle has: the incircle, centred where the angle bisectors meet ([[4.4]]), and the circumcircle, centred where the perpendicular bisectors meet ([[4.5]]).
- **The square** ([[4.6]]–[[4.9]]), inscribed in and circumscribed about a circle, and the reverse.
- **The pentagon, the hexagon and the 15-gon** ([[4.10]]–[[4.16]]). The pentagon is the summit of the book. It needs the golden section of [[2.11]], which [[4.10]] turns into a triangle with angles $36°, 72°, 72°$, and [[4.11]] turns that triangle into the pentagon. The hexagon is easy, because its side equals the radius ([[4.15]]). Combining the triangle and the pentagon gives the 15-gon ([[4.16]]).

Bisecting arcs doubles the number of sides, so Euclid can construct the regular $n$-gon for $n = 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 15, 16, 20, \dots$: every $n$ of the form $2^k$, $3 \cdot 2^k$, $5 \cdot 2^k$ or $15 \cdot 2^k$. No one added a new odd prime to the list for more than two thousand years, until Gauss constructed the 17-gon in 1796. The note to [[4.16]] explains which polygons can be constructed, and why.

Book IV relies on the tangent and angle theorems of Book III, particularly [[3.32]] (the angle between a tangent and a chord) and [[3.37]] (a converse of the tangent–secant theorem). The pentagon returns in Book XIII, where it is used to build the dodecahedron and the icosahedron.
