---
title: The regular pentagon circumscribed about a circle
---
**Statement.** About a given circle, circumscribe a regular pentagon.

**Construction.** Take the vertices @A, @B, @C, @D, @E of an inscribed regular pentagon ([[4.11]]), and draw the tangents at them. Consecutive tangents meet at @G, @H, @K, @L, @M.

**Proof.** Work at the vertex @K, between the points of contact @B and @C.

1. The radii @FB and @FC are perpendicular to the tangents ([[3.18]]). Pythagoras on the right triangles $FBK$ and $FCK$, which share the hypotenuse @FK, gives $BK = CK$ ([[1.47]]). The triangles are congruent (SSS, [[1.8]]), so @FK bisects both $\angle BFC$ and $\angle BKC$.
2. The arcs $BC$ and $CD$ are equal, so the central angles $BFC$ and $CFD$ are equal ([[3.27]]), and so are their halves $KFC$ and $LFC$.
3. The triangles $FKC$ and $FLC$ now have two angles and the common side @FC equal, so they are congruent ([[1.26]]): $KC = CL$ and $\angle FKC = \angle FLC$.

From 3, $KL = 2\,KC$, and in the same way every side of the pentagon is twice one of these equal tangent lengths, so the sides are equal. The angle at each vertex is twice the matching angle $FKC$, so the angles are equal too.

**In the figure.** Drag the centre @F, or @A around the circle.

:::code Duality
The inscribed and circumscribed pentagons are related by *polarity* in the circle: each vertex of one corresponds to a side of the other. The same argument works for any regular polygon, which is why Euclid can say at the end of [[4.15]] and [[4.16]] that the hexagon and 15-gon can be circumscribed "in like manner". The circumscribed pentagon is the inscribed one scaled by $1/\cos 36° = 2/\varphi$ and turned by $36°$.
:::
