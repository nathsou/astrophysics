---
title: Mean proportional (square roots)
---
**Construction.** Given two segments @AB and @BC, find $DB$ with
$$AB : DB = DB : BC, \quad\text{i.e.}\quad DB = \sqrt{AB \cdot BC}.$$
Place @AB and @BC in one straight line, draw the semicircle @circle(ADC) on @AC, raise the perpendicular @BD at @B, and let it meet the semicircle at @D.

**Why it works.** The angle $ADC$ is in a semicircle, so it is right ([[3.31]]). In the right triangle $ADC$, $DB$ is the altitude on the hypotenuse, so by the porism of [[6.8]] it is the mean proportional between the segments $AB$ and $BC$:
$$DB^2 = AB \cdot BC.$$

:::code Constructing a square root
Take $BC = 1$. Then $DB = \sqrt{AB}$: the construction computes the square root of any given length. Drag @B along @AC to watch it.

```ts
// √x with ruler and compass: semicircle on a segment of length x + 1
const sqrtByRuler = (x: number) => {
  const r = (x + 1) / 2;         // radius of the semicircle on AC
  const d = x - r;               // distance from the centre to B
  return Math.sqrt(r * r - d * d);  // height of the circle above B = √x
};
```

The last line uses Pythagoras to *check* the construction; the construction itself needs no square roots, only a circle and a perpendicular. The height above $B$ is $\sqrt{r^2 - d^2} = \sqrt{(r-d)(r+d)} = \sqrt{BC\cdot AB}$.
:::

Two other readings of the same fact are useful. The **geometric mean** $\sqrt{ab}$ of two lengths is the side of the square equal to the rectangle $a\cdot b$; so this proposition does in one step what [[2.14]] does, the squaring of a rectangle. And since the geometric mean never exceeds the radius $\tfrac{a+b}{2}$, the figure is a proof of the AM–GM inequality $\sqrt{ab} \le \tfrac{a+b}{2}$, with equality when $B$ is the centre.

:::leads Where it leads
Square roots are the only irrational operation ruler and compass can perform. The lengths constructible from a unit are exactly those reachable from $1$ by $+, -, \times, \div$ and $\sqrt{\ }$. Wantzel used this in 1837 to prove that doubling the cube ($\sqrt[3]{2}$) and trisecting a general angle are impossible. Book X classifies lines built by repeated square roots, such as $\sqrt{\sqrt a \pm \sqrt b}$.
:::
