---
title: Angles are proportional to arcs
---
**Statement.** In equal circles $ABC$ and $DEF$, angles at the centres (@angle(BGC), @angle(EHF)) and angles at the circumferences (@angle(BAC), @angle(EDF)) are in the same ratio as the arcs they stand on:
$$\text{arc } BC : \text{arc } EF = \angle BGC : \angle EHF = \angle BAC : \angle EDF.$$

**Proof (Euclid's).** This is the proof of [[6.1]] again, with arcs in place of bases and angles in place of triangles. Lay off arcs @CK, @KL equal to $BC$, and @FM, @MN equal to $EF$.

- Equal arcs subtend equal angles at the centre ([[3.27]]). So any multiple of the arc $BC$, such as $BL = 3\cdot BC$, subtends the same multiple of the angle $BGC$, and likewise for $EF$.
- Equal arcs in equal circles have equal angles, a greater arc a greater angle, and a smaller arc a smaller angle.
- That is the Eudoxus condition ([[5.def.5]]), so $\text{arc }BC : \text{arc }EF = \angle BGC : \angle EHF$.

The angles at the circumference are halves of those at the centre ([[3.20]]), so they have the same ratio ([[5.15]]).

:::gap Angles of more than two right angles
The multiples $\angle BGL$ quickly exceed two right angles, and for large $m$ they wrap around the circle. Euclid's angles are always less than two right angles, so "the angle $BGL$" is not an angle in his sense once the arc $BL$ exceeds a semicircle. The proof needs angles of any size, which amounts to measuring rotations rather than angles. Keeping the arcs small, as the sliders here do, avoids the problem only for small multiples.
:::

:::leads Radians
The proposition says that the map from angles to arcs is proportional: $\text{arc} = \kappa\,\cdot\text{angle}$, with a constant $\kappa$ that depends only on the circle. In equal circles it is the same constant. Choosing the unit of angle so that $\kappa$ is the radius gives the **radian**: an angle at the centre measures $\theta = s / r$, the arc length divided by the radius. The figure shows this readout.

That choice is what makes calculus with angles clean. With $\theta$ in radians, $\sin'\theta = \cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ for small $\theta$; in degrees, every derivative would carry a factor $\pi/180$. Euler's formula $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ also needs radians. This proposition is the reason that the radian measure is well defined.
:::
