---
title: Angle-bisector theorem
---
**Statement.** In triangle $ABC$, let @AD meet @BC at @D. Then
$$AD \text{ bisects } \angle BAC \iff BD : DC = BA : AC.$$
The bisector divides the opposite side in the ratio of the adjacent sides.

**Proof (Euclid's).** Draw @CE through @C parallel to @AD, meeting $BA$ produced at @E.

- $\angle ACE = \angle CAD$ (alternate angles, [[1.29]]), and $\angle AEC = \angle BAD$ (corresponding angles, [[1.29]]).
- If $AD$ is the bisector, $\angle CAD = \angle BAD$, so $\angle ACE = \angle AEC$. The triangle $ACE$ is isosceles and $AE = AC$ ([[1.6]]).
- In triangle $BCE$, $AD$ is parallel to the side $EC$, so $BD : DC = BA : AE$ by [[6.2]]. Replace $AE$ by $AC$.

For the converse, the same construction gives $BA : AC = BA : AE$, so $AC = AE$ ([[5.9]]). The base angles of $ACE$ are then equal ([[1.5]]), and the two parallel-angle equalities give $\angle BAD = \angle CAD$.

The trick is to turn the angle condition into a length condition ($AE = AC$) that [[6.2]] can use.

:::leads Where it leads
The same holds for the external bisector, which divides $BC$ externally in the ratio $BA : AC$. Robert Simson added it to his edition as Proposition A of Book VI. The points dividing $BC$ internally and externally in a fixed ratio $k \ne 1$ are the ends of a diameter of the *circle of Apollonius*, the locus of points $P$ with $PB : PC = k$.
:::
