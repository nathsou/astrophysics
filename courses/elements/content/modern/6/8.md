---
title: The altitude on the hypotenuse
---
**Statement.** In a triangle $ABC$ with a right angle at @A, let @AD be perpendicular to @BC. Then the triangles $ABD$ and $ADC$ are similar to $ABC$ and to each other.

**Porism.** The altitude is the mean proportional between the segments of the hypotenuse:
$$BD : DA = DA : DC, \quad\text{i.e.}\quad AD^2 = BD \cdot DC.$$

**Proof (Euclid's).** $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle ABD$ both have a right angle ($\angle BAC$ and $\angle ADB$) and share the angle at $B$, so their third angles are equal too ([[1.32]]): $\angle ACB = \angle BAD$. By [[6.4]] their sides are proportional, so by [[6.def.1]] they are similar. The same argument works for $ADC$. Finally $ABD$ and $ADC$ each have a right angle at $D$, and $\angle BAD = \angle ACB$, so they are equiangular too.

The three similar triangles give three proportions at once:
$$BC : BA = BA : BD, \qquad BC : CA = CA : CD, \qquad BD : DA = DA : DC.$$
The first two say $AB^2 = BC \cdot BD$ and $AC^2 = BC \cdot CD$. Adding them gives $AB^2 + AC^2 = BC\,(BD + DC) = BC^2$, which is Pythagoras' theorem again ([[1.47]]). Euclid does not draw this conclusion here, but [[6.31]] uses exactly this argument.

**In the figure.** @A glides on the semicircle on @BC, which keeps the angle at $A$ right ([[3.31]]). The readouts check the proportions.

:::leads Where it leads
The porism is the key to [[6.13]], which constructs the mean proportional of two segments, and therefore square roots. The proportions $AB^2 = BC\cdot BD$ are the "leg rules" of right-triangle geometry.
:::
