Book VI is where Book V pays off. Book V built a theory of ratio for arbitrary magnitudes, including incommensurable ones, without numbers. Book VI applies it to plane figures, and the result is the theory of **similarity**: figures with the same shape and different sizes.

The book rests on two propositions. [[6.1]] says that triangles under the same height are to one another as their bases, and [[6.2]] says that a line parallel to one side of a triangle cuts the other two sides proportionally. Almost everything else is derived from these two. From them come the angle-bisector theorem ([[6.3]]), the similarity criteria AA, SSS, SAS and a guarded SSA ([[6.4]]–[[6.7]]), and the similar triangles inside a right triangle ([[6.8]]).

Next come constructions with ruler and compass that act as arithmetic on lengths. [[6.9]] and [[6.10]] divide a segment in a given ratio, [[6.11]] and [[6.12]] find third and fourth proportionals (multiplication and division), and [[6.13]] finds the mean proportional, which is the construction of a square root. [[6.16]] shows that a proportion $a:b = c:d$ is the same as an equation of areas $ad = bc$.

Area is the next theme. Similar figures are to one another as the squares of their sides ([[6.19]], [[6.20]]), and the ratio of two parallelograms is the product of the ratios of their sides ([[6.23]]). Late in the book comes the "application of areas" ([[6.27]]–[[6.29]]), which amounts to solving quadratic equations geometrically, together with the condition under which a solution exists. [[6.30]] uses it to cut a line in the golden ratio. [[6.31]] generalizes Pythagoras from squares to any similar figures, and [[6.33]] proves that angles are proportional to the arcs they stand on, which is the idea behind radian measure.

Book VI is used heavily in Book X (irrational lines) and in the solid geometry of Books XI–XIII.
