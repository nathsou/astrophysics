---
title: Proportion as equality of products
---
**Statement.** For four segments,
$$AB : CD = E : F \iff AB \cdot F = CD \cdot E,$$
where $AB \cdot F$ means the rectangle with sides $AB$ and $F$. In words: the product of the extremes equals the product of the means.

This is the "cross-multiplication" rule, and it is the bridge between the two languages of Greek mathematics: proportions of lines (Book V) and equalities of areas (Books I–II). Euclid needs it as a theorem because for him a ratio is not a fraction and $AB\cdot F$ is not a number.

**Proof (Euclid's).** Raise @AG $= F$ perpendicular to @AB at @A, and @CH $= E$ perpendicular to @CD at @C. Complete the rectangles @figure(BG) $= AB \cdot F$ and @figure(DH) $= CD \cdot E$. They are equiangular (all angles right). The hypothesis says $AB : CD = CH : AG$, which is the "reciprocal proportion" of [[6.14]]. That proposition says exactly that equiangular parallelograms with reciprocally proportional sides are equal, and conversely.

:::code From ratios to equations
[[6.16]] is what lets later Greek geometry, and Descartes after it, turn proportions into equations. Where Euclid writes $a : b = c : d$, algebra writes $ad = bc$. With this rule, solving a proportion for an unknown becomes solving a linear equation, and [[6.12]] is its geometric solution: $x = bc/a$.
:::

[[6.17]] is the special case $CD = E$.
