---
title: Triangles of equal height are as their bases
---
**Statement.** If triangles $ABC$ and $ACD$ have the same height (their apex @A is common and their bases @BC, @CD lie on one line), then
$$\triangle ABC : \triangle ACD = BC : CD,$$
and the same holds for the parallelograms @figure(EC) and @figure(CF) on those bases.

In coordinates this is one line: area $= \tfrac12 \cdot \text{base} \cdot h$ with the same $h$, so the ratio of the areas is the ratio of the bases. Euclid cannot argue that way. For him a length and an area are not numbers, and $BC : CD$ may be incommensurable, so there may be no common unit to count with. The proof has to go through the definition of proportion in Book V.

**Proof.** Book V says ([[5.def.5]]) that $a : b = c : d$ when, for every pair of whole numbers $m, n$,
$$ma \gtreqless nb \iff mc \gtreqless nd,$$
with the same sign on both sides. So it is enough to take arbitrary multiples of the base and of the triangle and compare.

- Lay off copies of @BC to the left: $BG = GH = BC$. The triangles $ABC$, $AGB$, $AHG$ have equal bases under the same apex, so they are equal ([[1.38]]). Hence with $m$ copies, the base $HC = m\cdot BC$ carries the triangle $AHC = m \cdot \triangle ABC$. The figure uses $m = 3$.
- In the same way, $n$ copies of @CD to the right give $LC = n \cdot CD$ and $\triangle ALC = n \cdot \triangle ACD$.
- Triangles under the same apex compare as their bases compare *in size*: if $HC > CL$ then $\triangle AHC > \triangle ACL$, if equal then equal, and if less then less ([[1.38]] and its consequences).

That is exactly the condition of [[5.def.5]], so $BC : CD = \triangle ABC : \triangle ACD$. The parallelograms are twice the triangles ([[1.41]]), and magnitudes have the same ratio as their doubles ([[5.15]]).

:::code The Eudoxus test as a loop
[[5.def.5]] is a universally quantified statement, and this proof is a textbook case of discharging it. With lengths and areas as floats, the test it checks is:

```ts
// a : b = c : d  in the sense of Eudoxus (checked up to N)
const sameRatio = (a: number, b: number, c: number, d: number, N = 50) => {
  for (let m = 1; m <= N; m++)
    for (let n = 1; n <= N; n++)
      if (Math.sign(m * a - n * b) !== Math.sign(m * c - n * d)) return false;
  return true;
};
```

Euclid proves the loop body once, for arbitrary $m$ and $n$, and never has to run it. The trick is that "take $m$ copies of the base" and "take $m$ copies of the triangle" are the same geometric act.
:::

:::gap Comparing triangles by their bases
The step "if $HC > CL$ then $\triangle AHC > \triangle ACL$" is not stated anywhere in Book I. It follows from [[1.38]] by cutting off the equal part, but it also needs the fact that a part of a figure is less than the whole ([[1.cn.5]]), applied to areas. Modern treatments use a theory of area (a measure function) in which this monotonicity is an axiom or a lemma.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
This is one of the two foundations of Book VI. [[6.2]] follows at once, and through it every result on similar figures. The same equimultiple argument reappears in [[6.33]] with angles and arcs in place of triangles and bases, and in Book XII for circles and pyramids.
:::
