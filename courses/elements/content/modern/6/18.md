---
title: Copying a figure onto a given segment
---
**Construction.** Given a segment @AB and a polygon @figure(CE), construct on $AB$ a polygon similar to $CE$ and similarly situated, with $AB$ corresponding to @CD.

Cut $CE$ into triangles by diagonals; the figure's quadrilateral needs one, @DF. Copy each triangle's angles in turn:

1. At @A and @B, make $\angle GAB = \angle C$ and $\angle ABG = \angle CDF$ ([[1.23]]). Then $GAB$ is equiangular with $FCD$.
2. On @BG, make $\angle BGH = \angle DFE$ and $\angle GBH = \angle FDE$. Then $GBH$ is equiangular with $FDE$.

**Why it works.** Each pair of triangles is equiangular, so by [[6.4]] its sides are proportional with the common factor $FD : GB$. The angles of the polygons are equal because they are sums of equal angles, for example $\angle AGH = \angle AGB + \angle BGH = \angle CFD + \angle DFE = \angle CFE$. So the polygons are equiangular with proportional sides, i.e. similar ([[6.def.1]]).

For a polygon with more sides, the method is the same with more diagonals, which amounts to an induction on the number of triangles.

:::code A similarity is determined by two points
In coordinates, the result is the image of $CE$ under the unique direct similarity $z \mapsto a + (z - c)\dfrac{b - a}{d - c}$ (complex numbers) taking $C \mapsto A$ and $D \mapsto B$. That is how the figure computes @G and @H. Euclid builds the same map one triangle at a time, using only angle copying.
:::
