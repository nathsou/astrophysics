---
title: The golden section
---
**Problem.** Cut a given segment @AB in extreme and mean ratio ([[6.def.3]]): find @E on it with
$$BA : AE = AE : EB.$$

**Construction (Euclid's).** Describe the square @figure(BC) on $AB$. To @AC apply a parallelogram @figure(CD) equal to the square $BC$ and exceeding by a figure @figure(AD) similar to $BC$, i.e. a square ([[6.29]]). The side of that square marks @E on $AB$.

**Why it works.** $CD = BC$; removing the common part @figure(CE) leaves @figure(BF) $=$ @figure(AD). These are equiangular, so their sides are reciprocally proportional ([[6.14]]): $FE : ED = AE : EB$. But $FE = AB$ and $ED = AE$, so $BA : AE = AE : EB$.

In algebra: with $AB = a$ and $AE = x$, the application says $x(a + x) = a^2$. Hence
$$x = \frac{\sqrt5 - 1}{2}\, a, \qquad \frac{AB}{AE} = \frac{1+\sqrt5}{2} = \varphi \approx 1.618.$$

This is the same cut as [[2.11]], which finds $E$ with $AB \cdot EB = AE^2$. That is the same condition, as [[6.17]] shows. Book II states it as an equality of areas, and Book VI as a proportion.

:::code Self-similarity and Fibonacci
The defining equation $\varphi = 1 + 1/\varphi$ says that removing the square $AD$ from the rectangle $CD$ leaves a rectangle $BF$ of the same shape. Iterating gives the continued fraction $\varphi = [1; 1, 1, 1, \dots]$. Its convergents are ratios of consecutive Fibonacci numbers:

```ts
let [p, q] = [1, 1];
for (let i = 0; i < 20; i++) [p, q] = [p + q, p]; // p/q → φ
```

These are the "worst approximable" irrationals, and the Euclidean algorithm ([[7.2]]) run on $a$ and $a/\varphi$ never terminates: every step is the same subtraction. That is the geometric reason why the diagonal and the side of a regular pentagon are incommensurable.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
The golden section builds the regular pentagon ([[4.10]], [[4.11]]), and Book XIII uses it throughout to construct the icosahedron and dodecahedron ([[13.1]]–[[13.6]], [[13.16]], [[13.17]]).
:::
