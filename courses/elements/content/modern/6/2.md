---
title: The side-splitter theorem
---
**Statement.** In triangle $ABC$, let @D lie on @AB and @E on @AC. Then
$$DE \parallel BC \iff BD : DA = CE : EA.$$

This is the basic theorem of similarity. Dragging @D along @AB dilates the small triangle $ADE$ about @A, and the line @DE stays parallel to @BC.

**Proof (Euclid's), forward direction.** Join @BE and @CD.

1. The triangles $BDE$ and $CDE$ have the same base @DE and lie between the parallels $DE$ and $BC$, so they are equal in area ([[1.38]]).
2. Equal magnitudes have the same ratio to a third one ([[5.7]]), so
$$\triangle BDE : \triangle ADE = \triangle CDE : \triangle ADE.$$
3. The triangles $BDE$ and $ADE$ have their bases $BD$, $DA$ on one line and the same apex $E$, so by [[6.1]] $\triangle BDE : \triangle ADE = BD : DA$. In the same way $\triangle CDE : \triangle ADE = CE : EA$.
4. Chaining the equal ratios ([[5.11]]) gives $BD : DA = CE : EA$.

**Converse.** Run the same chain backwards. If $BD : DA = CE : EA$, then $\triangle BDE$ and $\triangle CDE$ have the same ratio to $\triangle ADE$, so they are equal ([[5.9]]). Two equal triangles on the same base and on the same side of it lie between the same parallels ([[1.39]]), so $DE \parallel BC$.

The argument never measures a length. It turns a statement about ratios of segments into a statement about equality of areas, which Book I can handle.

:::code The theorem as a linear map
With vectors and $A$ as origin, "$D$ divides $AB$ in the ratio $t$" means $D = tB$. The theorem says that $E = tC$ exactly when $E - D = t(C - B)$, i.e. when $DE$ is parallel to $BC$ and $DE = t\cdot BC$. In other words, the map $x \mapsto tx$ (a dilation about $A$) takes lines to parallel lines. The whole of similarity theory is the study of this map, and Book VI proves its properties without coordinates.
:::

:::gap The case of incommensurable ratios
The proof is complete even when $BD : DA$ is irrational, because [[6.1]] was proved with Eudoxus' definition. A proof that divides the sides into equal parts, which is the natural first attempt, works only when the ratio is rational. This is the reason for Book V. Euclid does not treat the case in which $D$ and $E$ lie on the extensions of the sides beyond $A$ or beyond $B$, $C$; the same argument covers it with the obvious changes.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
[[6.4]] (equiangular triangles have proportional sides) is two applications of this theorem, and the constructions [[6.9]]–[[6.12]] are applications of it too. In projective geometry the same idea leads to the cross-ratio, and in linear algebra to the fact that dilations are linear maps.
:::
