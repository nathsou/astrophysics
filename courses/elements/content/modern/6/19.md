---
title: Areas of similar triangles
---
**Statement.** If triangles $ABC$ and $DEF$ are similar, with @BC corresponding to @EF, then
$$\triangle ABC : \triangle DEF = (BC : EF)^2.$$
Euclid says "in the **duplicate ratio**" of $BC$ to $EF$ ([[5.def.9]]): if $BC : EF = EF : BG$, then $BC : BG$ is the duplicate of $BC : EF$. In modern terms, it is the square of the ratio. Area scales as the square of length.

**Proof (Euclid's).** Take @BG, the third proportional to $BC$ and $EF$ ([[6.11]]), so that $BC : EF = EF : BG$, and join @AG.

- From the similarity, $AB : DE = BC : EF = EF : BG$. So the triangles $ABG$ and $DEF$ have equal angles at $B$ and $E$, with the sides about them reciprocally proportional. By [[6.15]], $\triangle ABG = \triangle DEF$.
- $ABC$ and $ABG$ have the same apex and bases on one line, so $\triangle ABC : \triangle ABG = BC : BG$ ([[6.1]]).
- $BC : BG$ is the duplicate ratio of $BC : EF$, and $\triangle ABG = \triangle DEF$.

Euclid never multiplies ratios. He builds a segment $BG$ that *realizes* the square of the ratio, then compares areas through it. With $k = EF : BC$ (the slider), $BG = k^2 \cdot BC$.

**Porism.** If $a : b = b : c$, then $a : c$ equals the ratio of a figure on $a$ to a similar figure on $b$. In other words, to compare similar figures on $a$ and $b$, find the third proportional and compare lines. [[6.20]], [[6.22]], [[6.25]] and [[6.31]] all use this.

:::leads Where it leads
Area scales with $k^2$ and volume with $k^3$: a statue twice as tall needs eight times the bronze. Book XII proves the analogues for circles ([[12.2]], as the squares on the diameters) and for spheres ([[12.18]], in the triplicate ratio of their diameters). In analysis the same fact reads $\mu(kS) = k^n \mu(S)$ for the Lebesgue measure in $\mathbb R^n$, the starting point of fractal dimension.
:::
