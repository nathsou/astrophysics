---
title: Equiangular triangles have proportional sides (AA)
---
**Statement.** If triangles $ABC$ and $DCE$ have $\angle ABC = \angle DCE$, $\angle BAC = \angle CDE$ and $\angle ACB = \angle CED$, then the sides opposite equal angles are proportional:
$$AB : BC = DC : CE,\qquad BC : CA = CE : ED,\qquad BA : AC = CD : DE.$$
Since the angles of a triangle add up to two right angles ([[1.32]]), two equal angles are enough. This is the **AA criterion** for similarity.

**Proof (Euclid's).** Place the triangles side by side with @BC and @CE on one line, as in the figure (the slider sets the scale factor $CE : BC$).

- The angles at @B and @E add up to less than two right angles, so $BA$ and $ED$ produced meet, at @F ([[1.post.5]]).
- $\angle DCE = \angle ABC$ gives $CD \parallel BF$, and $\angle ACB = \angle DEC$ gives $AC \parallel FE$ ([[1.28]]). So $FACD$ is a parallelogram, and $FA = DC$, $AC = FD$ ([[1.34]]).
- In triangle $FBE$, $AC$ is parallel to $FE$, so $BA : AF = BC : CE$ ([[6.2]]). Replace $AF$ by $CD$ and swap the middle terms ([[5.16]]): $AB : BC = DC : CE$.
- In the same triangle, $CD \parallel BF$ gives $BC : CE = FD : DE = AC : DE$, and so $BC : CA = CE : ED$.
- Combine the two ([[5.22]], *ex aequali*) to get the third proportion.

The whole proof is two applications of [[6.2]] inside one big triangle. Euclid places the two triangles cleverly, so that the big triangle is actually there to be cut.

:::gap Moving a triangle
"Let $BC$ be placed in a straight line with $CE$" moves a triangle to a new position. Euclid means: construct a copy of $DCE$ in that position, which [[1.23]] and [[1.22]] allow. It is the same kind of step as the superposition in [[1.4]].
:::

:::leads Where it leads
Combined with [[6.def.1]], this says that equiangular triangles are similar. It is the proposition used most often in the rest of the book, and in trigonometry it is the reason why $\sin$, $\cos$, $\tan$ are functions of the angle alone: every right triangle with a given acute angle has the same ratios of sides.
:::
