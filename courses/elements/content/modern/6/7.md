---
title: SSA similarity, with a condition
---
**Statement.** Suppose triangles $ABC$ and $DEF$ have $\angle A = \angle D$ and $AB : BC = DE : EF$ (so the given angle is *not* between the proportional sides). If the angles at $C$ and $F$ are both acute, or both not acute, the triangles are equiangular.

Without the condition the statement is false. Given the angle at @A, the side @AB and the length @BC, there can be two triangles. In the figure, @G is the second point of the line @AC at distance $BC$ from @B. The triangle $ABG$ has the same angle at $A$ and the same sides $AB$ and $BG = BC$, but its angle at $G$ is obtuse while the angle at $C$ is acute. This is the "ambiguous case" of trigonometry.

**Proof (Euclid's), first case.** Suppose the angles at $C$ and $F$ are acute, and suppose $\angle ABC > \angle DEF$. Cut off $\angle ABG = \angle DEF$ with @G on @AC ([[1.23]]).

- $ABG$ and $DEF$ are equiangular, so $AB : BG = DE : EF = AB : BC$, hence $BG = BC$ ([[6.4]], [[5.9]]).
- Then $\angle BGC = \angle BCG$, which is acute ([[1.5]]), so its supplement $\angle AGB$ is obtuse ([[1.13]]).
- But $\angle AGB = \angle F$ (from the equiangular triangles), and $\angle F$ is acute. Contradiction.

So $\angle ABC = \angle DEF$, and the third angles are equal too ([[1.32]]). In the second case the same construction gives a triangle $BGC$ with two angles that together are not less than two right angles, which contradicts [[1.17]].

:::gap What the figure shows
Euclid's $G$ is an impossible point: it exists only under the false assumption $\angle ABC > \angle DEF$. The figure draws a real point instead, the second intersection of the circle about @B through @C with the line @AC. When it lies between $A$ and $C$ it is exactly Euclid's $G$, and the angle at $G$ is obtuse, as the proof says.
:::
