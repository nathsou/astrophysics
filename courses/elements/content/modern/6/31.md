---
title: Pythagoras for any similar figures
---
**Statement.** In a triangle $ABC$ with a right angle at @A, the figure on @BC equals the similar and similarly described figures on @BA and @AC together.

For squares this is [[1.47]]. Here the three figures can have any shape, provided they are similar to one another: triangles, as in the figure, or semicircles, or pentagons.

**Proof (Euclid's).** Draw the altitude @AD.

1. By [[6.8]], the triangles $ABD$ and $ADC$ are similar to $ABC$, so $CB : BA = AB : BD$. Three lines in continued proportion: $BA$ is the mean between $CB$ and $BD$.
2. By the porism of [[6.19]], the first is to the third as the figure on the first is to the similar figure on the second:
$$CB : BD = \text{fig}(CB) : \text{fig}(BA).$$
In the same way, $BC : CD = \text{fig}(BC) : \text{fig}(CA)$.
3. Inverting and adding ([[5.24]]): $(BD + DC) : BC = (\text{fig}(BA) + \text{fig}(AC)) : \text{fig}(BC)$.
4. $BD + DC = BC$, so the ratio is equality, and $\text{fig}(BA) + \text{fig}(AC) = \text{fig}(BC)$.

This proof does not cut and rearrange anything, unlike [[1.47]]. It uses similarity, and it proves Pythagoras' theorem again as the special case of squares.

:::code Why any shape works
Every figure of a given shape has area proportional to the square of a chosen side: $\text{fig}(s) = \kappa\, s^2$, with a constant $\kappa$ that depends only on the shape ([[6.19]], [[6.20]]). So
$$\text{fig}(BC) = \kappa\, BC^2 = \kappa\,(AB^2 + AC^2) = \text{fig}(AB) + \text{fig}(AC).$$
The proposition is [[1.47]] multiplied by $\kappa$. The sliders change the shape, that is $\kappa$, and the equality of the readouts does not change.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
With semicircles as the figures, the proposition shows that the two crescent-shaped lunes over the legs together equal the triangle. This generalizes Hippocrates of Chios' quadrature of a lune, one of the earliest exact quadratures of a curved figure. The idea that Pythagoras' theorem is really about any quadratic quantity leads to the parallelogram law and to the inner product spaces in which $\lVert x + y\rVert^2 = \lVert x\rVert^2 + \lVert y\rVert^2$ for orthogonal $x, y$.
:::
