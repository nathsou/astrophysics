---
title: Square of a sum
---
**Statement.** If $AB$ is cut at $C$, then
$$AB^2 = AC^2 + CB^2 + 2\,AC \cdot CB,$$
that is, $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + 2ab$ with $AC = a$ and $CB = b$.

**Proof.** Draw the square $ADEB$ on @AB and its diagonal @BD. The parallel to @AD through @C meets the diagonal at @G, and the parallel to @AB through @G finishes the dissection into four pieces.

1. @figure(CK) is a square. The diagonal makes $\angle CGB = \angle ADB$ ([[1.29]]), and $\angle ADB = \angle ABD$ because the triangle $ABD$ is isosceles ([[1.5]]). So the triangle $CGB$ has two equal angles, and $CG = CB$ ([[1.6]]). The angle at $B$ is right, so all its angles are right ([[1.29]], [[1.34]]).
2. In the same way @figure(HF) is a square, with side $HG = AC$.
3. The two rectangles @figure(AG) and @figure(GE) are complements about the diagonal, so they are equal ([[1.43]]), and each is $AC \cdot CB$.

The four pieces make up the square on @AB.

This is the first place in the book where a *diagonal* does the work. A line through a point of the diagonal, parallel to the sides, always cuts off squares about the diagonal. [[2.5]] to [[2.8]] use the same device.

**In the figure.** Drag @C along @AB. The two squares on the diagonal grow and shrink, and the two rectangles stay equal to each other.

:::code A block decomposition
Split each side of the square into the parts $a$ and $b$. The square becomes a $2 \times 2$ grid of blocks, like a block matrix:

| | $a$ | $b$ |
|---|---|---|
| $a$ | $a^2$ (@figure(HF)) | $ab$ (@figure(AG)) |
| $b$ | $ab$ (@figure(GE)) | $b^2$ (@figure(CK)) |

The off-diagonal blocks are equal, which is where the factor 2 comes from. Euclid's extra work, the angle chase with the diagonal, is the proof that the diagonal blocks are really squares.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
[[2.12]] and [[2.13]] use this identity and [[2.7]] to prove the law of cosines. The same dissection, with the square cut into $n$ strips, is the picture behind the binomial theorem for $n = 2$.
:::
