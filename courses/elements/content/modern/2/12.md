---
title: Law of cosines (obtuse angle)
---
**Statement.** In a triangle $ABC$ with an obtuse angle at $A$, let $D$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $B$ to $CA$ produced. Then
$$BC^2 = BA^2 + AC^2 + 2\, CA \cdot AD.$$
Since $AD = AB \cos(180° - A) = -AB\cos A$, this is the law of cosines
$$a^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc\cos A$$
for an obtuse angle, where $\cos A < 0$ and the correction term is positive: the opposite side is longer than Pythagoras would give.

**Proof.** The line @CD is cut at @A, so by [[2.4]]
$$CD^2 = CA^2 + AD^2 + 2\,CA \cdot AD.$$
Add $DB^2$ to both sides. On the left, $CD^2 + DB^2 = CB^2$, and on the right $AD^2 + DB^2 = AB^2$, both by Pythagoras in the right triangles at @D ([[1.47]]). That is the statement.

**In the figure.** Drag the vertices. The figure refuses to make @angle(BAC) acute, since the hypothesis requires it obtuse. [[2.13]] is the acute case, and when the angle is right, @D coincides with @A, the term $2\,CA \cdot AD$ vanishes, and the statement becomes [[1.47]].

:::code Dot products
In vectors, with $\vec u = \overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\vec v = \overrightarrow{AC}$,
$$|\vec u - \vec v|^2 = |\vec u|^2 + |\vec v|^2 - 2\,\vec u \cdot \vec v .$$
Euclid's rectangle $CA \cdot AD$ is $-\vec u \cdot \vec v$: the length of $AC$ times the (signed) projection of $AB$ onto it. The sign of the dot product decides between II.12 and II.13, so the modern formula covers both propositions and Pythagoras in one line.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
With [[2.13]] this completes the relation between the three sides of any triangle and its angles. Trigonometry later gives the correction term a name, $-2bc\cos A$. The same computation is at work in the distance formula for the inner product spaces of linear algebra.
:::
