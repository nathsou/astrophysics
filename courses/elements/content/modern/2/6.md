---
title: Difference of squares, with the line produced
---
**Statement.** Let $AB$ be bisected at $C$ and produced to $D$. Then
$$AD \cdot DB + CB^2 = CD^2.$$
With $CB = h$ and $BD = x$: $(2h + x)\,x + h^2 = (h + x)^2$.

**Proof.** The same construction as [[2.5]], on the square $CEFD$ on @CD: its diagonal @DE, the parallel @BG, and the parallel through @H. This time the rectangle @figure(AM), which is $AD \cdot DB$ because $DM = DB$, is moved into the gnomon @figure(NOP).

- @figure(AL) equals @figure(CH) (equal bases $AC = CB$, same parallels, [[1.36]]), and @figure(CH) equals @figure(HF) (complements, [[1.43]]). So @figure(AL) equals @figure(HF).
- Add @figure(CM) to both: @figure(AM) equals the gnomon @figure(NOP).

The gnomon and the square @figure(LG), which is the square on $CB$, make up the square on @CD.

II.5 and II.6 are one identity, $ab + \left(\frac{a-b}{2}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)^2$. The difference is where the point falls: in II.5 @D is inside the line, in II.6 it is on the line produced.

**In the figure.** Drag @D along the line beyond @B.

:::leads Solving quadratics
II.6 solves $x(b + x) = c$: find two lengths with a given *difference* $b$ and product $c$. Take $AB = b$ and look for $D$ on $AB$ produced with $AD \cdot DB = c$. By II.6, $CD^2 = c + (b/2)^2$. That square can always be found ([[1.47]] gives a square equal to the sum of two squares), so
$$x = BD = \sqrt{\left(\frac b2\right)^2 + c}\, - \frac b2$$
always exists. This is the equation $x^2 + bx = c$, and the construction is completing the square, drawn. Euclid uses it at once in [[2.11]], and the general form, applying an area to a line so that it exceeds by a square, is [[6.29]].

:::
