---
title: Law of cosines (acute angle)
---
**Statement.** In a triangle $ABC$ with an acute angle at $B$, let $D$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $A$ to $BC$. Then
$$AC^2 = CB^2 + BA^2 - 2\, CB \cdot BD.$$
Since $BD = BA\cos B$, this is $b^2 = a^2 + c^2 - 2ac\cos B$: the side opposite an acute angle is shorter than Pythagoras would give.

**Proof.** The line @CB is cut at @D, so by [[2.7]]
$$CB^2 + BD^2 = 2\,CB \cdot BD + DC^2.$$
Add $DA^2$ to both sides and use Pythagoras at @D ([[1.47]]): $BD^2 + DA^2 = BA^2$ and $DC^2 + DA^2 = AC^2$. That leaves
$$CB^2 + BA^2 = 2\,CB \cdot BD + AC^2.$$

:::gap Where is the foot of the perpendicular?
Euclid's figure puts @D between @B and @C, and his text cuts @CB at @D. But the foot can fall outside the base: if the angle at @C is obtuse, @D lies on @BC produced beyond @C. The identity still holds, but the cut is different: then @BD is cut at @C, and the right step is [[2.7]] applied to $BD$. Drag @A to the right, beyond @C, to see this second case: the claim still holds. With signed lengths (or the dot product of [[2.12]]) there is only one case.
:::

**In the figure.** The figure refuses to make @angle(ABC) obtuse, which would turn the proposition into [[2.12]] with the roles of the vertices changed.

:::leads Where it leads
II.12 and II.13 together say that the square on a side is greater than, equal to, or less than the sum of the squares on the other two according as the opposite angle is obtuse, right or acute. So the three sides determine the kind of angle: this is the converse of [[1.47]], [[1.48]], extended to all angles. In particular the three sides determine the foot of each altitude, and so the height and the area of the triangle: $BD = \frac{a^2 + c^2 - b^2}{2a}$, with $a = BC$, $b = CA$, $c = AB$.
:::
