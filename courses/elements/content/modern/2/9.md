---
title: Sum of squares from the midpoint
---
**Statement.** Let $AB$ be bisected at $C$ and cut unequally at $D$. Then
$$AD^2 + DB^2 = 2\,(AC^2 + CD^2).$$
With $AC = h$ and $CD = x$: $(h+x)^2 + (h-x)^2 = 2(h^2 + x^2)$.

**Proof.** This time Euclid uses right isosceles triangles instead of squares. Put $CE \perp AB$ with $CE = AC$. Then the triangles $ACE$ and $BCE$ are right isosceles, their base angles are half a right angle each, and $\angle AEB$ is right. Draw @DF parallel to @CE and @FG parallel to @AB. The small triangles $EGF$ and $FDB$ are right isosceles too, so $EG = GF = CD$ and $FD = DB$ ([[1.6]], [[1.34]]).

Now apply Pythagoras ([[1.47]]) four times:
$$AF^2 = AE^2 + EF^2 = 2AC^2 + 2GF^2 = 2AC^2 + 2CD^2,$$
$$AF^2 = AD^2 + DF^2 = AD^2 + DB^2.$$

**In the figure.** Drag @D between @C and @B.

:::leads Where it leads
In vector form this is the parallelogram law, $|u + v|^2 + |u - v|^2 = 2|u|^2 + 2|v|^2$, the identity that characterizes norms coming from an inner product. It also gives Apollonius' theorem on the median of a triangle.
:::
