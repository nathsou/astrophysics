---
title: Squaring a polygon
---
**Statement.** For any polygon @A there is a square with the same area, and it can be constructed. This is the *quadrature* of rectilineal figures.

**Construction.**

1. Build a rectangle @figure(BD) equal to @A ([[1.45]]: cut the polygon into triangles and apply each to a line as a parallelogram, all in one right angle). If its sides @BE and @ED are equal, it is the square, and we are done.
2. Otherwise produce @BE to @F with $EF = ED$, bisect @BF at @G, and draw the semicircle @circle(BHF) on @BF.
3. Produce @DE to meet the semicircle at @H. The square on @EH is the answer.

**Proof.** @BF is cut equally at @G and unequally at @E, so by [[2.5]]
$$BE \cdot EF + EG^2 = GF^2 = GH^2 = EG^2 + EH^2,$$
using $GF = GH$ (radii) and Pythagoras in the right triangle $GEH$ ([[1.47]]). Subtract $EG^2$:
$$EH^2 = BE \cdot EF = BE \cdot ED = \text{area of } BD = \text{area of } A.$$

So the side of the square is $EH = \sqrt{BE \cdot ED}$, the *geometric mean* of the rectangle's sides. The semicircle is a square-root machine: it turns a product of two lengths into a single length.

**In the figure.** Drag the corners of the pentagon @A, or @B and @E, which fix the side on which the rectangle is built. The rectangle, the semicircle and the square on @EH follow, and the readouts check $EH^2 = $ area of @A.

:::code The square root, constructed
Step 2 is an algorithm for $\sqrt{xy}$ with straightedge and compass:

```ts
// the mean proportional of x and y (II.14, later VI.13)
function geometricMean(x: number, y: number): number {
  const r = (x + y) / 2;           // radius of the semicircle on x + y
  const d = r - y;                 // distance from its centre to the cut point E
  return Math.sqrt(r * r - d * d); // the half-chord EH, by I.47; equals √(x·y)
}
```

With a unit length available, $\sqrt{x} = \sqrt{x \cdot 1}$. Together with the constructions for $+$, $-$, $\times$ and $\div$ (Book VI), this shows that the lengths constructible with straightedge and compass are closed under square roots. The converse, that *only* square roots are available, is what makes some constructions impossible (see [[4.16]]).
:::

:::gap The size of the answer
Euclid proves that the square exists and equals @A, but "equal" here is in area only. The question of whether two polygons of equal area can be cut into the same finite set of pieces was settled much later: yes, by the Wallace–Bolyai–Gerwien theorem, proved in the early nineteenth century. For polyhedra the answer is no: this was Hilbert's third problem, solved by Dehn in 1900. A second gap is that Euclid never defines area. He adds and subtracts "equal" figures with the common notions ([[1.cn.2]], [[1.cn.3]]) and assumes that a part is less than the whole ([[1.cn.5]]). Hilbert's *Foundations of Geometry* rebuilds this theory of "equal content" and proves that it is consistent, for instance that a polygon cannot be equal to a part of itself.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
Quadrature became the Greek standard for *measuring* a figure: to know its area was to have a square equal to it. After polygons, the natural next step was the circle. Hippocrates of Chios squared certain lunes, and Archimedes showed the circle equals a right triangle with legs the radius and the circumference, which reduces the problem to straightening the circumference. With straightedge and compass it is impossible: Lindemann proved in 1882 that $\pi$ is transcendental, so $\sqrt\pi$ is not constructible. [[6.13]] redoes II.14's key step in the language of proportion, $BE : EH = EH : ED$.
:::
