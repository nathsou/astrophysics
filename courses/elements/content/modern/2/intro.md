Book II is short: two definitions and fourteen propositions, almost all about rectangles and squares built on a straight line that has been cut into pieces. Read with modern eyes, most of it is algebra. If a line is cut into pieces of lengths $a$ and $b$, then "the rectangle contained by" two lines is a product, "the square on" a line is a square, and adding figures is adding areas. With that dictionary, II.1 is the distributive law $a(b+c+d) = ab+ac+ad$ ([[2.1]]), II.4 is $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$ ([[2.4]]), and II.5 is a difference of squares ([[2.5]]).

Euclid does not write it this way. His quantities are lengths and areas, never numbers, and every identity is proved by cutting a square into pieces and moving them. This is why the book is often called *geometric algebra*. The name is a modern interpretation, promoted in the late nineteenth century and used by Heath. Some historians object that it reads later algebra back into Greek geometry. In any case, the dictionary is exact, and it is the fastest way to see what each proposition says.

The book has three parts.

- **Identities, II.1–II.10.** Each figure here has a cut point you can drag, and the figure checks both sides of the identity as you move it. The *gnomon* ([[2.def.2]]), an L-shaped region, does most of the work.
- **Quadratic equations.** II.5 and II.6 are the geometric core of solving $x(b \mp x) = c$, and [[2.11]] uses II.6 to cut a line in the golden section, the ratio behind the pentagon of [[4.11]].
- **Two applications.** [[2.12]] and [[2.13]] extend Pythagoras to triangles that are not right-angled: together they are the law of cosines. [[2.14]] closes the book by constructing a square equal to any polygon.

Almost everything rests on Book I's theory of parallelograms, especially [[1.43]] (complements are equal) and [[1.47]].
