---
title: Sum of squares, with the line produced
---
**Statement.** Let $AB$ be bisected at $C$ and produced to $D$. Then
$$AD^2 + DB^2 = 2\,(AC^2 + CD^2).$$
With $AC = h$ and $BD = x$: $(2h + x)^2 + x^2 = 2\big(h^2 + (h + x)^2\big)$.

**Proof.** The same idea as [[2.9]], with @D outside. Put $CE \perp AB$ with $CE = AC$, join @EA and @EB, and complete the rectangle $CEFD$. The lines @EB and @FD meet at @G below @AB. They must meet, because the angles $FEB$ and $EFD$ make less than two right angles: Euclid cites the parallel postulate explicitly here ([[1.post.5]]).

All the triangles in the figure are right isosceles, so $DG = DB$ and $GF = EF = CD$. Then [[1.47]] gives
$$AG^2 = AE^2 + EG^2 = 2AC^2 + 2CD^2, \qquad AG^2 = AD^2 + DG^2 = AD^2 + DB^2.$$

**In the figure.** Drag @D along the line beyond @B.

:::code One identity, two cases
II.9 and II.10 are the same identity, $(h+x)^2 + (h-x)^2 = 2(h^2 + x^2)$, read with $x < h$ and with $x > h$ (put $x \to h + x$). Euclid proves them separately because a point inside a line and a point on the line produced give different figures. Signed lengths, which he did not have, merge the two cases, as they merge [[2.5]] with [[2.6]].
:::
