---
title: The golden section
---
**Statement.** Any segment $AB$ can be cut at a point $H$ so that
$$AB \cdot BH = AH^2.$$
If $AB = 1$ and $AH = x$, this is $1 - x = x^2$, so $x = \frac{\sqrt5 - 1}{2} \approx 0.618$, and $AB : AH = \varphi = \frac{1 + \sqrt 5}{2}$. The greater part is to the smaller as the whole is to the greater. Euclid will call this "extreme and mean ratio" in [[6.def.3]]. The name "golden section" is modern: it dates from the nineteenth century.

**Construction.**

1. Draw the square $ABDC$ on @AB and bisect its side @AC at @E.
2. Join @BE, produce @CA beyond @A, and cut off @EF equal to @EB (the circle with centre @E through @B).
3. Draw the square @figure(FH) on @AF. Its corner @H on @AB is the point.

**Proof.** The line @AC is bisected at @E and produced to @F, so by [[2.6]],
$$CF \cdot FA + AE^2 = EF^2 = EB^2 = AB^2 + AE^2,$$
the last step by Pythagoras in the right triangle $BAE$ ([[1.47]]). Subtract $AE^2$: $CF \cdot FA = AB^2$. In the figure, $CF \cdot FA$ is the rectangle @figure(FK), since $FA = FG$, and $AB^2$ is the square @figure(AD). Take away the common part @figure(AK): what remains is @figure(FH) $=$ @figure(HD), and that says $AH^2 = AB \cdot BH$.

In algebra: with $AB = a$ and $AF = x$, step 2 makes $x = \sqrt{a^2 + (a/2)^2} - a/2$, which is exactly the positive root of $x^2 + ax = a^2$, found by [[2.6]].

:::gap Where do F and H fall?
Euclid takes for granted that @F lies beyond @A and that @H lies between @A and @B. Both are true, and both need a line of proof. $EF = EB > EA$, because $EB$ is the hypotenuse of the triangle $BAE$ ([[1.19]]), so @F is beyond @A. And $AF = EB - EA < AB$ by the triangle inequality ([[1.20]]), so @H falls inside @AB. Modern treatments make such order facts explicit with Hilbert's axioms of betweenness.
:::

:::code The golden ratio in code
The golden cut is the fixed point of $x \mapsto 1/(1+x)$, so its continued fraction is $[0; 1, 1, 1, \dots]$ and its convergents are ratios of Fibonacci numbers:

```ts
let [a, b] = [1, 1];
for (let i = 0; i < 20; i++) [a, b] = [b, a + b];
a / b; // 0.6180339887…, and (√5 − 1)/2 = 0.6180339887…
```

All partial quotients equal 1, so the convergents approach it as slowly as possible. In this sense $\varphi$ is the irrational number hardest to approximate by fractions. Euclid's algorithm of repeated subtraction, applied to $AB$ and $AH$, never stops: cutting $AH$ off $AB$ leaves $BH$, and $AH : BH$ is again the golden ratio. By [[10.2]], the two lengths are therefore incommensurable.
:::

**In the figure.** Drag @A or @B: the readout $AH : AB$ stays at $0.618034$.

:::leads Where it leads
The golden section is the key to the regular pentagon. [[4.10]] cuts a line this way to build a triangle with angles $36°, 72°, 72°$, and [[4.11]] uses that triangle to inscribe the pentagon. [[6.30]] repeats the construction in the language of proportion, and Book XIII uses it throughout to build the dodecahedron and the icosahedron.
:::
