Book XIII is the summit of the Elements. It constructs the five regular solids, fits each of them into a given sphere, computes its edge in terms of the diameter, and ends by proving that there are no others. Plane geometry, proportion, the irrational lines of Book X and the solid geometry of Book XI all meet here.

- **The golden section** ([[13.1]]–[[13.6]]). Five lemmas about a line cut in extreme and mean ratio ([[6.def.3]]), the ratio now called $\varphi = \frac{1 + \sqrt5}{2}$. Each is an identity of the form "some square is five (or three) times another", proved with squares and gnomons in the style of Book II. [[13.6]] shows that both segments of a rational line cut in this ratio are irrational, of the kind Book X calls *apotomes*.
- **Polygons in the circle** ([[13.7]]–[[13.12]]). The regular pentagon, whose diagonals cut each other in the golden ratio ([[13.8]]); the hexagon and decagon, whose sides together form a line cut in that ratio ([[13.9]]); and [[13.10]], which says the side of the pentagon, the side of the hexagon and the side of the decagon in the same circle form a right triangle. That fact builds the icosahedron.
- **The five solids** ([[13.13]]–[[13.17]]). The pyramid (tetrahedron), octahedron, cube, icosahedron and dodecahedron, each constructed from a given diameter and proved to be inscribed in the sphere. For the first three, the square on the edge is a simple fraction of the square on the diameter: $\tfrac23$, $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac13$. The icosahedron and dodecahedron have irrational edges, of the kinds Book X calls *minor* and *apotome*.
- **The comparison** ([[13.18]]). All five edges are laid out on one diameter, and a final argument about the angles at a vertex shows that no sixth regular solid exists.

An ancient scholium attributes the octahedron and icosahedron to Theaetetus, and the solids are often called Platonic because Plato's *Timaeus* matched four of them to the elements. The later commentator Proclus even read the whole Elements as aimed at this book. Read today, Book XIII is also the first classification theorem in mathematics: a complete list of the objects of a kind, with a proof that the list is complete.
