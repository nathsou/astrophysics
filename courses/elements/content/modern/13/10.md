---
title: Pentagon, hexagon and decagon form a right triangle
---
**Statement.** For polygons inscribed in the same circle,
$$(\text{side of pentagon})^2 = (\text{side of hexagon})^2 + (\text{side of decagon})^2.$$
With radius 1: $\frac{10 - 2\sqrt5}{4} = 1 + \frac{6 - 2\sqrt5}{4}$.

**Proof.** Let @F be the centre, @AB a side of the pentagon, @FB a radius (the hexagon's side, [[4.15]]) and @AK a side of the decagon, where @K halves the arc $AB$. Let @M halve the arc $AK$, and let $FM$ meet $AB$ at @N. Two pairs of similar triangles give two rectangles:

1. $\angle BFN = \angle FAB$ (both are $54°$; Euclid gets this from arcs), so the triangles $ABF$ and $FBN$ are similar and $AB \cdot BN = BF^2$ ([[6.17]]).
2. $FM$ is the perpendicular bisector of $AK$, so $KN = AN$ and the triangles $AKB$ and $AKN$ are similar: $BA \cdot AN = AK^2$.

Adding, $AB \cdot BN + BA \cdot AN = AB^2$ ([[2.2]]), which equals $BF^2 + AK^2$.

**In the figure.** Drag @A; the readouts check both rectangles and the sum.

:::leads Where it leads
Put a hexagon side at right angles to a decagon side, and the hypotenuse is a pentagon side. [[13.16]] does exactly that in space: a vertical edge equal to the radius over a horizontal decagon side gives an edge of the icosahedron equal to the pentagon's side, which is why all its triangles are equilateral.
:::
