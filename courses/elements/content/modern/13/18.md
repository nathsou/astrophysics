---
title: The five edges compared, and why there are only five
---
**Statement.** Two results close the Elements.

1. The edges of the five regular solids inscribed in one sphere can all be constructed on its diameter and compared:
$$e_{\text{tet}} > e_{\text{oct}} > e_{\text{cube}} > e_{\text{ico}} > e_{\text{dod}},$$
where $e$ is the edge of each solid inscribed in the same sphere.
2. No other solid is bounded by equal regular polygons.

**The five edges on one diameter.** Let @AB be the diameter, bisected at @C, with @AD $= 2\,DB$. On the semicircle @circle(AEB), raise @CE and @DF perpendicular to $AB$. By [[6.8]], the chord from an end of the diameter to the point over a division of it has square proportional to that division, so:

- @AF is the tetrahedron's edge: $AF^2 = \tfrac23 AB^2$ ([[13.13]]);
- @BE is the octahedron's edge: $BE^2 = \tfrac12 AB^2$ ([[13.14]]);
- @BF is the cube's edge: $BF^2 = \tfrac13 AB^2$ ([[13.15]]).

For the icosahedron, draw @AG $= AB$ perpendicular to $AB$ and join @GC, meeting the semicircle at @H; drop @HK. Then $CK^2 = \tfrac15 BC^2$. Lay off @CL $= CK$ and raise @LM: then @KL is the radius of the circle the icosahedron is built on, @AK and @LB are decagon sides, and @MB, hexagon and decagon at right angles, is the icosahedron's edge ([[13.10]], [[13.16]]). For the dodecahedron, cut the cube's edge $FB$ in extreme and mean ratio at @N: @NB is the edge ([[13.17]], porism).

Euclid notes that the first three are "in rational ratios": in units where $AB^2 = 6$, their squares are $4$, $3$ and $2$. The other two are irrational, a minor line and an apotome, and have no rational ratio to anything. Proving $MB > NB$ takes a small argument: $AD^2 = 4DB^2 > 3DB^2 = FB^2$, so $AL > FB$; the greater segments keep the order, so $KL > NB$; and $MB > LM = KL$.

**Only five.** At each vertex of a solid, at least three faces meet, and their angles add up to less than four right angles ([[11.21]]); otherwise they lie flat or overlap. The angle of a regular polygon is $60°$ for the triangle, $90°$ for the square, $108°$ for the pentagon (the lemma), and $120°$ or more for everything else. So the possibilities are:

| faces at a vertex | angle sum | solid |
|---|---|---|
| 3 triangles | $180°$ | tetrahedron |
| 4 triangles | $240°$ | octahedron |
| 5 triangles | $300°$ | icosahedron |
| 6 triangles | $360°$ | flat: impossible |
| 3 squares | $270°$ | cube |
| 4 squares | $360°$ | flat: impossible |
| 3 pentagons | $324°$ | dodecahedron |
| 4 pentagons | $432°$ | impossible |
| 3 hexagons | $360°$ | flat: impossible |

Five survive, and [[13.13]]–[[13.17]] construct all five.

**The five solids.**

| solid | faces | at a vertex | $V$ | $E$ | $F$ | $e^2 : d^2$ | $e / d$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| tetrahedron | triangles | 3 | 4 | 6 | 4 | $2 : 3$ | $0.8165$ |
| octahedron | triangles | 4 | 6 | 12 | 8 | $1 : 2$ | $0.7071$ |
| cube | squares | 3 | 8 | 12 | 6 | $1 : 3$ | $0.5774$ |
| icosahedron | triangles | 5 | 12 | 30 | 20 | $(5 - \sqrt5) : 10$ | $0.5257$ |
| dodecahedron | pentagons | 3 | 20 | 30 | 12 | $(3 - \sqrt5) : 6$ | $0.3568$ |

:::gap What "regular" means
Euclid's condition is "contained by equilateral and equiangular figures equal to one another", and his proof looks at a single vertex. Both leave something out. Equal regular faces do not force all vertices to look alike: two tetrahedra glued along a face give a solid with six equal equilateral triangles, with three faces at some vertices and four at others. A modern definition requires the vertices to be alike too (or the symmetry group to act transitively on flags). Convexity is also assumed: the angle-sum argument is about convex corners, and without convexity there are the four regular star polyhedra of Kepler and Poinsot. And the table shows only that each vertex type gives *at most* one solid; that each gives exactly one, up to size, needs the constructions of [[13.13]]–[[13.17]] and a uniqueness argument that Euclid does not state.
:::

:::leads Euler's formula
For every convex polyhedron, $V - E + F = 2$. The table checks it five times: $4 - 6 + 4 = 6 - 12 + 8 = 8 - 12 + 6 = 12 - 30 + 20 = 20 - 30 + 12 = 2$. This is not in Euclid; Euler stated it in the 1750s, and it became one of the starting points of topology. It gives a second proof that there are only five. If every face has $p$ sides and $q$ faces meet at each vertex, then $pF = 2E = qV$, and substituting into Euler's formula,
$$\frac1p + \frac1q = \frac12 + \frac1E > \frac12,$$
whose only solutions with $p, q \ge 3$ are $(3,3)$, $(3,4)$, $(4,3)$, $(3,5)$, $(5,3)$. This version uses no angles at all, only counting. A related fact, due to Descartes, adds the angles back: the "missing angle" $360° - \text{angle sum}$ at each vertex, added over all the vertices, is always $720°$. The table gives $4 \cdot 180°$, $6 \cdot 120°$, $8 \cdot 90°$, $12 \cdot 60°$, $20 \cdot 36°$.
:::

:::history
Plato's *Timaeus* assigned the tetrahedron to fire, the octahedron to air, the icosahedron to water and the cube to earth, and used the dodecahedron for the whole cosmos, which is why the five are often called the Platonic solids. Kepler, in 1596, tried to explain the spacing of the six known planets by nesting the five solids between their spheres. Euclid's aim was more modest and more lasting: to construct them exactly and prove the list complete.
:::

## Try it without code

At a vertex of a convex regular solid, the face angles must sum to less than 360°. An equilateral triangle contributes 60°: three, four or five can meet, but six flatten out. A square contributes 90° and a regular pentagon 108°: only three of either can meet. Explain why a regular hexagon, whose angle is 120°, cannot produce another case.
