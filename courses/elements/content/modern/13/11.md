---
title: The side of the pentagon is a minor line
---
**Statement.** If a regular pentagon is inscribed in a circle with a rational diameter, its side is the irrational line that Book X calls *minor* ([[10.76]]).

**Proof.** Take the radius @FA $= 1$ (a rational line, since the diameter is). Lay off @FK $= \tfrac14$ on the radius beyond @F, so that @BK $= \tfrac54$. Let @M be the midpoint of @AC. Similar triangles and [[13.8]] and [[13.1]] give $MK^2 = 5\,KF^2$, so $MK = \frac{\sqrt5}{4}$, rational in square only. Then $BK$ and $MK$ are rational lines commensurable in square only, and $MB = BK - KM = \frac{5 - \sqrt5}{4}$ is an apotome; Euclid checks it is a *fourth* apotome, using the auxiliary line @N with $N^2 = BK^2 - KM^2$. Finally $AB^2 = HB \cdot BM$ ([[6.8]]), and the side of a square equal to the rectangle contained by a rational line and a fourth apotome is a minor line ([[10.94]]).

In numbers: $AB^2 = 2 \cdot \frac{5 - \sqrt5}{4} = \frac{5 - \sqrt5}{2}$, so $AB = \sqrt{\frac{5 - \sqrt5}{2}} \approx 1.1756$, and $AB$ divided by the diameter is $0.5878$. The square of a minor line is a rational area less a medial one: here $\tfrac52 - \tfrac{\sqrt5}{2}$.

**In the figure.** The readouts check the three squares and give the side as a fraction of the diameter.

:::leads Where it leads
The icosahedron's edge is the side of a pentagon in a circle whose diameter is rational when the sphere's is ([[13.16]]), so it is minor too. This proposition, [[13.6]] and [[13.17]] are among the few places where the Elements applies Book X's classification of irrational lines to a particular figure.
:::
