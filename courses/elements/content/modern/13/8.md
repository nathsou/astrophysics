---
title: The diagonals of a pentagon cut each other in the golden ratio
---
**Statement.** In a regular pentagon $ABCDE$, the diagonals @AC and @BE meet at @H, and each is cut in extreme and mean ratio there. The greater segments @HE and @HC equal the side:
$$BE : EH = EH : HB, \qquad EH = AB.$$

**Proof.** Circumscribe the circle @circle(ABCDE). The triangles @triangle(ABE) and @triangle(ABC) are congruent, so $\angle BAC = \angle ABE$, and the exterior angle $\angle AHE$ of the triangle $ABH$ is $2\angle BAH$. The angle $\angle EAC$ stands on the arc $EDC$, twice the arc $CB$, so it too is $2\angle BAC$ ([[6.33]]). Hence the triangle $AHE$ is isosceles, and $HE = EA = AB$.

The triangles @triangle(ABE) and @triangle(ABH) share the angle at $B$ and have $\angle BAH = \angle BEA$, so they are similar, and $EB : BA = AB : BH$. Replacing $BA$ by $EH$ gives the golden cut.

**In the figure.** Drag @A round the circle; the readout shows the ratio of diagonal to side, $\varphi = \frac{1 + \sqrt5}{2} \approx 1.618$.

:::leads Where it leads
The diagonal of a regular pentagon is $\varphi$ times the side. This is why $\varphi$ runs through the pentagon, the pentagram and the icosahedron and dodecahedron built from them, and why the diagonal and side of a pentagon are incommensurable: Euclid's algorithm on them never stops, since each step produces a smaller pentagon.
:::
