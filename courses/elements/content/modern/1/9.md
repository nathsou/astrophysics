---
title: Bisecting an angle
---
**Statement.** Given an angle $BAC$, construct a ray $AF$ with $\angle BAF = \angle FAC$.

**Construction.** Take any @D on @AB. Cut off $AE = AD$ on @AC ([[1.3]]). Build the equilateral triangle @triangle(DEF) on @DE, on the side away from @A ([[1.1]]). Join @AF.

**Why it works.** In triangles $ADF$ and $AEF$: $AD = AE$, $AF$ is common, and $DF = EF$. By SSS ([[1.8]]), $\angle DAF = \angle EAF$.

The equilateral triangle is more than needed: any point equidistant from @D and @E would do. Euclid uses I.1 because it is the tool he has.

:::gap
Euclid builds @F on the side of @DE away from @A, but does not say why. If @F were on the same side, it could coincide with @A (when the angle is sixty degrees), and the construction would collapse. It is also left unproved that @AF lies *inside* the angle, between @AB and @AC.
:::

:::leads
With I.9 and I.10, angles and segments can be halved as often as one likes. Trisecting an angle by straightedge and compass, by contrast, is impossible in general. This was proved by Wantzel in 1837, with the algebra of field extensions: a constructible length satisfies a tower of quadratic equations, and $\cos 20^\circ$ satisfies an irreducible cubic.
:::
