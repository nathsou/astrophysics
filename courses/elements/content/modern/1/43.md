---
title: Complements of a parallelogram
---
**Statement.** Let $K$ be a point on the diagonal $AC$ of a parallelogram $ABCD$. The lines through $K$ parallel to the sides cut $ABCD$ into four parallelograms. The two through which the diagonal passes, $EH$ and $FG$, are the parallelograms *about the diameter*. The other two, @figure(BK) and @figure(KD), are the *complements*. The complements have equal area.

**Proof.** A diagonal bisects every parallelogram it crosses ([[1.34]]):
$$\triangle ABC = \triangle ACD, \qquad \triangle AEK = \triangle AHK, \qquad \triangle KGC = \triangle KFC.$$
The triangle $ABC$ is made of $\triangle AEK$, $\triangle KGC$ and the complement $BK$. The triangle $ACD$ is made of $\triangle AHK$, $\triangle KFC$ and the complement $KD$. Subtract the equal pieces from the equal wholes ([[1.cn.3]]): $BK = KD$.

:::code Cross-multiplication in disguise
Take a rectangle with $A$ at the origin, and let $K = (x, y)$ be on the diagonal to $C = (a, b)$. Being on the diagonal means $x : y = a : b$, that is $bx = ay$. The complements have areas $x(b - y)$ and $(a - x)y$, and they are equal exactly when $bx = ay$. So I.43 is the geometric form of
$$\frac{x}{y} = \frac{a}{b} \iff bx = ay.$$
In Euclid this lemma becomes the engine of *geometric algebra*. [[1.44]] uses it to divide an area by a length, and Book VI uses it for proportions ([[6.14]], [[6.16]]).
:::
