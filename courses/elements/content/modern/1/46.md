---
title: Constructing a square
---
**Construction.** Given a segment $AB$, construct a square on it.

1. Draw @AC at right angles to @AB at @point(A) ([[1.11]]).
2. Cut off @AD equal to @AB ([[1.3]]).
3. Through @point(D) draw @DE parallel to @AB, and through @point(B) draw @BE parallel to @AD ([[1.31]]).

**Why it works.** A square, by [[1.def.22]], is a quadrilateral that is both equilateral and right-angled. Euclid checks both.

- *Equilateral.* $ADEB$ is a parallelogram, so $DE = AB$ and $BE = AD$ ([[1.34]]). Since $AD = AB$, all four sides are equal.
- *Right-angled.* $AD$ crosses the parallels $AB$ and $DE$, so $\angle BAD + \angle ADE = 180^\circ$ ([[1.29]]). $\angle BAD$ is right, so $\angle ADE$ is right. Opposite angles of a parallelogram are equal ([[1.34]]), so the other two angles are right as well.

:::gap Squares need Postulate 5
The proof goes through [[1.29]], and it has to: in the hyperbolic plane there are no squares. A quadrilateral with three right angles has an acute fourth angle there (a Lambert quadrilateral). Try Euclid's recipe there: on $AB$ erect equal perpendiculars $AD$ and $BE$ at both ends. The side $DE$ comes out longer than $AB$, and the angles at $D$ and $E$ are acute (a Saccheri quadrilateral). So the existence of a single square (or rectangle) is one of the equivalents of the parallel postulate, given Archimedes' axiom.

This is why the square appears so late in Book I, even though squares are defined in [[1.def.22]]. Euclid avoids using a figure before he has shown that it can be constructed.
:::

:::code A constructor, not a type assertion
Definition 22 says what a square *is*: a type with invariants (four equal sides, four right angles). This proposition is its constructor: a function `square(A, B)` that returns an object and proves that it satisfies those invariants. Until it exists, the type might be empty, and in the hyperbolic plane it is. The next proposition, [[1.47]], calls this constructor three times.
:::

**In the figure.** Drag @point(A) or @point(B). The arc about @point(A) through @point(B) marks off @AD, and the figure checks the four sides and the four right angles.
