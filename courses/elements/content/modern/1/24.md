---
title: The hinge theorem
---
**Statement.** If triangles $ABC$ and $DEF$ have $AB = DE$ and $AC = DF$, but $\angle BAC > \angle EDF$, then $BC > EF$.

Picture two rods hinged at @A: the wider you open them, the farther apart their ends. This is the inequality version of SAS ([[1.4]]).

**Proof (Euclid's).** At @D, construct $\angle EDG = \angle BAC$ ([[1.23]]) with $DG = AC = DF$. Join @EG and @FG.

1. $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEG$ by SAS ([[1.4]]), so $BC = EG$.
2. $DF = DG$, so $\angle DGF = \angle DFG$ ([[1.5]]).
3. Then $\angle EFG > \angle DFG = \angle DGF > \angle EGF$.
4. In @triangle(EFG), the greater angle is opposite the greater side ([[1.19]]): $EG > EF$. So $BC > EF$.

:::gap Where does F fall?
Step 3 reads the angles off Euclid's figure, in which @F lies below @EG. If @F lies *on* @EG, the result is immediate; if it lies above, the angles in step 3 are in different positions and the argument must be changed. Euclid treats only one case. Later editors fix this by choosing the notation so that $DE \le DF$, which forces @F below @EG; this needs a further lemma. The figure computes the correct inequality in all cases, and you can drag @E and @F to find the others.
:::

:::leads
The converse is [[1.25]]. Together they say that, with two sides fixed, the third side is a strictly increasing function of the included angle. With the law of cosines this is plain: $a^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc\cos\alpha$, and $\cos$ decreases on $(0, \pi)$. Euclid proves the monotonicity without any measure of angles.
:::

**In the figure.** @E and @F glide on circles about @D, so $DE = AB$ and $DF = AC$ always. The angle at @A must stay the greater; other drags are refused.
