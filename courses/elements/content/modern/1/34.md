---
title: Properties of a parallelogram
---
**Statement.** In a parallelogram $ACDB$ (opposite sides parallel):

- opposite sides are equal: $AB = CD$ and $AC = BD$;
- opposite angles are equal: $\angle BAC = \angle CDB$ and $\angle ABD = \angle ACD$;
- the diagonal $BC$ bisects the area: $\triangle ABC = \triangle DCB$.

**Proof.** The diagonal @BC is a transversal for both pairs of parallel sides. By [[1.29]], $\angle ABC = \angle BCD$ and $\angle ACB = \angle CBD$. So triangles $ABC$ and $DCB$ have two angles equal and the included side $BC$ in common, and they are congruent by ASA ([[1.26]]). This gives the equal sides and $\angle BAC = \angle CDB$. Adding the two pairs of equal angles at $B$ and $C$ gives $\angle ABD = \angle ACD$. Congruent triangles have equal area, so $BC$ bisects the parallelogram.

:::gap A word Euclid never defined
Euclid's definitions include squares, oblongs, rhombi and rhomboids ([[1.def.22]]), but not parallelograms. The term first appears here, as "parallelogrammic area", and is used from then on to mean a quadrilateral whose opposite sides are parallel. Also, "the diameter bisects the area" is the first time Book I compares areas. Here equal areas still come from congruent triangles. From [[1.35]] on, "equal" will mean equal in area for figures that are not congruent at all.
:::

**Where it is used.** Almost everywhere from here on: every area argument in [[1.35]]–[[1.45]] starts by halving a parallelogram with a diagonal. [[1.47]] uses this proposition through [[1.41]].
