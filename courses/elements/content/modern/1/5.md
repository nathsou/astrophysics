---
title: Base angles of an isosceles triangle
---
**Statement.** If $AB = AC$ in triangle $ABC$, then $\angle ABC = \angle ACB$. If @AB and @AC are produced to @D and @E, the angles under the base are also equal: $\angle CBD = \angle BCE$.

**Proof (Euclid's).** Take any @F on @BD and cut off $AG = AF$ on @AE ([[1.3]]). Join @FC and @GB.

1. $\triangle AFC \cong \triangle AGB$ by SAS ([[1.4]]): $AF = AG$, $AC = AB$, and the angle at @A is shared. So $FC = GB$, $\angle ACF = \angle ABG$ and $\angle AFC = \angle AGB$.
2. $BF = AF - AB = AG - AC = CG$ ([[1.cn.3]]).
3. $\triangle BFC \cong \triangle CGB$ by SAS again: $BF = CG$, $FC = GB$ and $\angle BFC = \angle CGB$ from step 1. So $\angle FBC = \angle GCB$ (the angles under the base) and $\angle BCF = \angle CBG$.
4. Subtract: $\angle ABC = \angle ABG - \angle CBG = \angle ACF - \angle BCF = \angle ACB$.

The auxiliary points are there because Euclid will not compare a triangle with itself. Pappus, as Proclus reports, did exactly that: $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle ACB$ (the same triangle, read in the other order) satisfy SAS, since $AB = AC$, $AC = AB$ and the angle at $A$ is common. So $\angle ABC = \angle ACB$ in one step. Whether one finds this elegant or suspicious says a lot about how one thinks of congruence: as a relation between *labelled* triangles, it is perfectly sound.

:::code The one-line proof as a symmetry
In modern terms, Pappus's proof says that the reflection in the bisector of the angle at @A swaps @B and @C and fixes the triangle. Any quantity defined symmetrically, such as the angle at a base vertex, is swapped with its partner, so the two are equal. Programmers meet the same idea when a function satisfies $f(x, y) = f(y, x)$: you only need to prove a property for one argument order.
:::

:::gap
Step 4 subtracts angles, which requires the ray @BG to lie inside the angle @angle(ABF), and step 2 requires @B to lie between @A and @F. Both are true, but they follow only from betweenness axioms that Euclid does not have (see the gap in [[1.7]]). The second half of the statement, about the angles under the base, is never used in Euclid's own proofs. Proclus explains that it is needed for a case of [[1.7]] that Euclid leaves out.
:::

**In the figure.** @C glides on the circle about @A through @B, so the triangle stays isosceles. Drag @F along @BD: the auxiliary triangles change, the conclusion does not.

:::history Pons asinorum
This is traditionally the "bridge of asses": the first proposition with a proof long enough to stop weak students. Another explanation of the name is the shape of Euclid's figure, which looks like a trestle bridge.
:::
