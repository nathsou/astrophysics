---
title: Parallelogram equal to a polygon
---
**Construction.** Given a rectilineal figure $ABCD$ and an angle $E$, construct a parallelogram with angle $E$ and area equal to the figure.

1. Cut the figure along the diagonal @DB into triangles $ABD$ and $DBC$.
2. Construct @figure(FH) equal to $\triangle ABD$, with $\angle HKF = E$ ([[1.42]]).
3. Apply @figure(GM) to the side @GH, equal to $\triangle DBC$, with $\angle GHM = E$ ([[1.44]]).

Then @figure(KFLM) is the required parallelogram.

**Why it works.** The area is right by construction: $FH + GM = \triangle ABD + \triangle DBC = ABCD$. The real work is showing that the two parallelograms fit together into *one* parallelogram. Their sides must line up:

- $\angle KHG + \angle GHM = \angle KHG + \angle HKF = 180^\circ$ ([[1.29]]), so $KH$ and $HM$ form one straight line ([[1.14]]);
- in the same way $FG$ and $GL$ form one straight line;
- $KF$ and $ML$ are both equal and parallel to $HG$, so $KFLM$ is a parallelogram ([[1.30]], [[1.33]]).

:::code A fold over a triangulation
The general case is a loop: split the polygon into triangles, and apply each triangle's parallelogram to the last side built.

```ts
function toParallelogram(poly: Point[], angle: Angle): Parallelogram {
  const [first, ...rest] = triangulate(poly);
  return rest.reduce((acc, t) => extend(acc, applyTo(side(acc), t, angle)), fromTriangle(first, angle));
}
```

`fromTriangle` is [[1.42]], `applyTo` is [[1.44]], and `extend` is the collinearity argument above. Euclid writes out the loop for two triangles and says the rest works the same way.
:::

:::gap Triangulation is assumed
Euclid's figure is a quadrilateral, split by one diagonal. For a general polygon, the proof needs two facts that are not proved: every polygon can be cut into triangles by diagonals (true, but it needs proof, for instance the "two ears" theorem for simple polygons), and the area of the whole is the sum of the pieces however it is cut. Euclid treats both as evident. If $ABCD$ is not convex, the diagonal $BD$ may also lie outside the figure. Then the other diagonal must be used.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
With [[2.14]], any polygon can be turned into a square of equal area. So any two polygons can be compared by comparing two squares, that is, two lengths. The Bolyai–Gerwien theorem (1830s) shows more: two polygons of equal area can always be cut into finitely many pieces that reassemble from one into the other. In three dimensions this fails. Dehn (1900) proved that a cube and a regular tetrahedron of equal volume cannot be cut into matching pieces, which settled Hilbert's third problem. Squaring a *circle* in the same way is impossible with ruler and compass, because $\pi$ is transcendental (Lindemann, 1882).
:::
