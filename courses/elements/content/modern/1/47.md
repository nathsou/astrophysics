---
title: Pythagoras’ theorem
---
**Statement.** In a triangle $ABC$ with a right angle at $A$,
$$BC^2 = AB^2 + AC^2.$$
For Euclid, $BC^2$ is not a number. It is a square figure, and "equal" means *equal in area*. The theorem says that the square on the hypotenuse can be cut and rearranged into the squares on the other two sides. This version needs no measurement at all.

**Proof (Euclid's).** Draw the three squares outward, and draw the line through @A parallel to @BD. It meets @DE at @L and splits the big square into two rectangles, @figure(BL) and @figure(CL). The claim is that each rectangle equals one of the small squares:
$$\text{rect } BL = \text{square } GB, \qquad \text{rect } CL = \text{square } HC.$$
Take the left pair.

- $\triangle ABD \cong \triangle FBC$ by SAS ([[1.4]]). $AB = FB$ and $BD = BC$ are sides of squares, and $\angle ABD = \angle FBC$ because each is a right angle plus $\angle ABC$.
- $\triangle ABD$ and the rectangle $BL$ have the same base $BD$ and lie between the same parallels $BD$ and $AL$, so the rectangle is twice the triangle ([[1.41]]).
- In the same way, the square $GB$ is twice $\triangle FBC$. The base is $FB$ and the parallels are $FB$ and $GC$. This is where the right angle at $A$ is used: it makes $G, A, C$ collinear ([[1.14]]).

Twice equal things are equal, so $BL = GB$. The same argument gives $CL = HC$, and adding the two gives the theorem.

:::code Two ways to check it
The figure checks the statement numerically as you drag @A along the semicircle, which keeps the angle at @A right ([[3.31]]). Each proof step is an invariant that holds in every configuration, not just the one drawn: this is how a property-based test tests a function, by running it on many inputs.

In coordinates, with $A$ at the origin and $B=(b,0)$, $C=(0,c)$, it is one line: $|BC|^2 = b^2 + c^2$. Euclid had no coordinates. His proof works entirely with areas, and it needs the whole of Book I to get there: its longest chain of citations is sixteen propositions deep (see the [graph](#/graph?focus=1.47)).
:::

:::gap The parallel postulate is essential
The proof uses [[1.41]], which rests on the theory of parallels (I.29 onward), and that rests on Postulate 5. In hyperbolic geometry the theorem is false. For a right triangle there, $\cosh c = \cosh a \cosh b$, which for small triangles is close to $c^2 \approx a^2 + b^2$ but never equal to it. The [explorations](#/explore/geometries) show the same triangle in both geometries.
:::

:::leads Where it leads
The converse is the next proposition, [[1.48]]. [[2.12]] and [[2.13]] generalize the theorem to any triangle (the law of cosines), and [[6.31]] replaces the squares with any similar figures. The distance formula of analytic geometry is this theorem applied to the coordinate axes.
:::
