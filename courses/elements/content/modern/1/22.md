---
title: Triangle from three given lengths
---
**Statement.** Given three lengths $A$, $B$, $C$ such that each two together are greater than the third, construct a triangle with sides $A$, $B$, $C$.

The condition is necessary by [[1.20]]. I.22 claims it is also sufficient. It is the first proposition in the Elements with a *diorism*, a statement of when a problem can be solved.

**Construction.** On a ray from @D, lay off $DF = A$, $FG = B$, $GH = C$ ([[1.3]]). Draw the circle about @F through @D and the circle about @G through @H. Let @K be a point where they meet. Then @triangle(KFG) is the triangle.

**Why it works.** $KF = FD = A$ and $KG = GH = C$ as radii, and $FG = B$ by construction.

:::gap Do the circles meet?
Euclid does not prove it, and this is exactly where the diorism should be used. The circles have radii $A$ and $C$ and centres $B$ apart. Two circles meet if and only if
$$|A - C| \le B \le A + C,$$
which is the triangle condition. But "if and only if" needs more than algebra: it needs the *circle–circle intersection property*, the same continuity principle that [[1.1]] needs. In $\mathbb{Q}^2$ the construction fails for $A = B = C = 1$, as it does in I.1. With the principle as an axiom, the proof becomes: one circle has a point inside the other and a point outside it, so they meet.
:::

:::code The diorism as a guard clause
A diorism is a precondition. In code:

```ts
function triangleFromSides(a: number, b: number, c: number): Triangle {
  if (!(a + b > c && b + c > a && c + a > b)) throw new RangeError('no such triangle');
  // … I.22's construction
}
```

In the figure, the sliders set the three lengths. Push one past the sum of the other two, and the circles separate: the construction is refused.
:::

**Uniqueness.** By [[1.8]], any two triangles with these sides are congruent: the lengths determine the triangle up to its position. The second intersection @L gives the mirror image.
