---
title: Perpendicular from a point to a line
---
**Statement.** Given an infinite line $AB$ and a point $C$ not on it, construct the perpendicular from $C$ to $AB$.

**Construction.** Take @D on the other side of @AB. The circle about @C through @D meets @AB at @E and @G. Bisect @EG at @H ([[1.10]]) and join @CH.

**Why it works.** In triangles $CHG$ and $CHE$: $GH = HE$, $HC$ is common, and $CG = CE$ (radii). By SSS ([[1.8]]), $\angle CHG = \angle CHE$, adjacent angles, so both are right ([[1.def.10]]).

This is the first time Euclid needs a line to be *infinite*: the circle must cut the line in two points, and a finite segment might be too short.

:::gap Why does the circle cut the line?
Because @D is on the other side of the line, the circle through @D contains points on both sides of @AB, and a "continuous" curve crossing from one side to the other must meet the line. That is a continuity argument again, like the circles of [[1.1]]; modern axiomatizations state it as a principle, the line–circle intersection property. The existence of a point @D on the other side is also assumed; it follows from the plane separation property.
:::

**In the figure.** Drag @D anywhere below the line: the circle changes, the foot @H does not. The foot of the perpendicular is determined by @C and the line alone, which Euclid never states, though it follows from [[1.17]]: a triangle cannot have two right angles.
