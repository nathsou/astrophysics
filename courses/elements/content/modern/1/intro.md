Book I builds plane geometry from nothing: 23 definitions, 5 postulates, 5 common notions, and 48 propositions that end with Pythagoras' theorem and its converse.

It falls into three parts.

1. **Constructions, congruence and inequalities** ([[1.1]]–[[1.26]]). The first propositions turn the three drawing postulates into a toolkit: an equilateral triangle ([[1.1]]), copying a length ([[1.2]]), bisecting angles and segments ([[1.9]], [[1.10]]), perpendiculars ([[1.11]], [[1.12]]), copying an angle ([[1.23]]). Alongside them come the congruence theorems, SAS ([[1.4]]), SSS ([[1.8]]) and ASA/AAS ([[1.26]]), and the inequalities: the exterior angle theorem ([[1.16]]), the triangle inequality ([[1.20]]) and the hinge theorem ([[1.24]]).
2. **Parallels** ([[1.27]]–[[1.32]]). Alternate angles, the parallel through a point, and the angle sum of a triangle ([[1.32]]). Postulate 5 is used here for the first time, in [[1.29]].
3. **Area** ([[1.33]]–[[1.48]]). Parallelograms and triangles on equal bases and between the same parallels, the application of areas ([[1.44]]), the square ([[1.46]]), and finally [[1.47]] and [[1.48]].

**What to look out for.** Euclid postpones the parallel postulate for as long as he can. Everything up to [[1.28]] is *neutral geometry*: true in hyperbolic geometry as well as in Euclidean, and marked as such on each page. Read [[1.post.5]] and then [[1.16]], [[1.17]] and [[1.29]] to see where the line falls.

Book I is also where the Elements is least rigorous by modern standards, and the most instructive about why. Circles are assumed to meet ([[1.1]], [[1.22]]), figures are moved about ([[1.4]]), and "inside" and "between" are read off the diagram ([[1.7]], [[1.16]]). The modern versions point out each gap and how Hilbert's axioms (1899) close it. None of the theorems turns out to be false; what was missing was the axioms.

Books II–IV build directly on this one: Book II recasts areas as algebra, Book III studies the circle, and Book IV inscribes regular polygons. Book VI returns to the figures of Book I with the theory of proportion of Book V.
