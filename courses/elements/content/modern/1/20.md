---
title: Triangle inequality
---
**Statement.** In any triangle $ABC$,
$$BA + AC > BC, \qquad AB + BC > AC, \qquad BC + CA > AB.$$

**Proof (Euclid's).** Produce @BA to @D with $AD = AC$ and join @DC. Then $BD = BA + AC$, and it suffices to show $BD > BC$.

- $\triangle ADC$ is isosceles, so $\angle ADC = \angle ACD$ ([[1.5]]).
- @CA lies inside @angle(BCD), so $\angle BCD > \angle ACD = \angle BDC$.
- In @triangle(DCB) the greater angle is opposite the greater side ([[1.19]]): $BD > BC$.

The proof converts a statement about lengths into a statement about angles and back again, using the two directions of [[1.18]] and [[1.19]].

:::history "Even a donkey knows it"
According to Proclus, the Epicureans mocked this proposition as obvious even to an ass, which goes straight to the hay along one side rather than two. Proclus replies that it is one thing to perceive a truth and another to know why it is true.
:::

:::code The metric axiom
The triangle inequality is one of the axioms of a *metric space*: $d(x, z) \le d(x, y) + d(y, z)$. In software it is what makes metric trees prune their nearest-neighbour searches correctly, and a "consistent" heuristic for A* search is precisely one that satisfies a triangle inequality. Here it is a theorem, not an axiom, and it is strict: in a triangle, the three vertices are not collinear.
:::

:::leads
The triangle inequality says the straight segment is shorter than any two-segment path, and by induction than any polygonal path. Archimedes took "the straight line is the shortest between its endpoints" as an assumption; Euclid proves the polygonal version. It holds in neutral geometry.
:::
