---
title: Parallelism is transitive
---
**Statement.** If $AB \parallel EF$ and $CD \parallel EF$, then $AB \parallel CD$ (for distinct lines).

**Proof.** Draw a transversal $GK$ crossing all three lines, at $G$, $H$ and $K$. By [[1.29]], $\angle AGK = \angle GHF$ (alternate angles for the pair $AB$, $EF$) and $\angle GHF = \angle GKD$ (corresponding angles for the pair $EF$, $CD$). So $\angle AGK = \angle GKD$. These are alternate angles for $AB$, $CD$, and [[1.27]] gives $AB \parallel CD$.

:::gap It needs Postulate 5
The proof goes through [[1.29]], and it has to. Over the neutral axioms, transitivity of parallelism is equivalent to Playfair's axiom. If two different lines through a point $P$ were both parallel to $EF$, they would be parallel to each other by transitivity, yet they meet at $P$. In the hyperbolic plane this happens: two lines can each miss a third line and still cross each other.

Euclid also draws only the case where $EF$ lies between the other two. If $EF$ is outside, the same argument works with different angle names. The claim "a transversal meets all three lines" is itself a small gap: a line that meets one of two parallels must meet the other, which is again a form of Postulate 5 (Proclus' axiom).
:::

:::code An equivalence relation
Count a line as parallel to itself. Then I.30 makes "is parallel to" transitive, and it is clearly reflexive and symmetric. So parallelism is an equivalence relation on lines, and its equivalence classes are the *directions* of the plane. Code that stores a line as a point plus a normalized direction vector uses this fact without mentioning it: two lines are parallel exactly when their directions are equal. In hyperbolic geometry directions are not well defined in this way.
:::
