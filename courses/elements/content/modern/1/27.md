---
title: Equal alternate angles give parallels
---
**Statement.** If a transversal $EF$ meets lines $AB$ and $CD$ with equal alternate angles, $\angle AEF = \angle EFD$, then $AB \parallel CD$. "Parallel" here means Euclid's definition [[1.def.23]]: the lines never meet, however far they are produced.

**Proof.** Suppose the lines meet, say on the side of $B$ and $D$, at a point $G$. Then $EFG$ is a triangle. Its exterior angle at $E$ is $\angle AEF$, and $\angle EFG$ is an interior opposite angle. By [[1.16]] the exterior angle is strictly greater, but the hypothesis says they are equal. Meeting on the side of $A$ and $C$ is ruled out in the same way. So the lines do not meet.

**In the figure.** The angle @angle(EFD) is constructed equal to @angle(AEF) at @point(F), on the other side of @EF, and the figure checks that @AB and @CD come out parallel. @point(G) is the meeting point the proof supposes, drawn dashed because it cannot exist.

:::gap The last neutral theorems
I.27 and [[1.28]] do not use Postulate 5. They rest on [[1.16]], which rests on SAS and the betweenness facts before it. So they hold in *neutral* (or *absolute*) geometry: everything that follows from Euclid's axioms without the parallel postulate. In particular they hold in the hyperbolic plane.

This has a consequence people often miss. Parallels always exist, even without Postulate 5: drop a transversal and copy the angle ([[1.31]] does exactly this). Postulate 5 is not needed for *existence*. It is needed for *uniqueness*, and that is where [[1.29]] comes in. In hyperbolic geometry there are infinitely many lines through a point that miss a given line, and only one of them makes equal alternate angles with a given transversal.
:::

:::gap Why lines cannot meet twice
The proof handles "meet towards $B, D$" and "meet towards $A, C$" separately, and does not consider meeting on both sides. On a sphere, with great circles as lines, two lines meet at two antipodal points. There [[1.16]] is false, and so is this proposition: two meridians make equal alternate angles with the equator (both right angles), and they meet at both poles. Euclid's plane escapes this because Postulate 1 is read as saying that two points determine a *unique* line, and Postulate 2 as saying that lines are unbounded. Hilbert states both explicitly.
:::
