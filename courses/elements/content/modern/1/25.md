---
title: Converse of the hinge theorem
---
**Statement.** In triangles $ABC$ and $DEF$ with $AB = DE$ and $AC = DF$,
$$BC > EF \implies \angle BAC > \angle EDF.$$
This is the converse of [[1.24]]. Together they say that, for two fixed side lengths, the third side and the angle opposite it are ordered the same way.

**Proof.** Compare @angle(BAC) with @angle(EDF). There are three possibilities.

- If they were equal, the triangles would be congruent by SAS ([[1.4]]), so $BC = EF$. Contradiction.
- If $\angle BAC < \angle EDF$, then [[1.24]] applied the other way round gives $BC < EF$. Contradiction.

Only $\angle BAC > \angle EDF$ is left.

:::code Converse of a monotone function
Fix the two sides $b = AC$ and $c = AB$. Then the base is a function of the angle between them, $a = f(\alpha)$. [[1.4]] says $f$ is well defined, and [[1.24]] says it is strictly increasing. For a strictly increasing function on a totally ordered domain, the converse comes for free:
$$f(x) > f(y) \implies x > y,$$
because the alternatives $x = y$ and $x < y$ give $f(x) = f(y)$ and $f(x) < f(y)$. Euclid's proof is this argument, case by case. The law of cosines makes $f$ explicit: $a^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc\cos\alpha$, and $\cos$ decreases on $(0, \pi)$.
:::

**In the figure.** @point(E) and @point(F) move on circles about @point(D), so $DE = AB$ and $DF = AC$ always hold. A drag that would make $EF \ge BC$ is refused, because it would break the hypothesis.
