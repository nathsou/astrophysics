---
title: Triangles on the same base
---
**Statement.** Triangles on the same base and between the same parallels have equal area: if $AD \parallel BC$, then $\triangle ABC = \triangle DBC$.

**Proof.** Complete each triangle to a parallelogram. Through @point(B) draw @BE parallel to $CA$, and through @point(C) draw @CF parallel to $BD$ ([[1.31]]), with $E$ and $F$ on the line $AD$. Then @figure(EBCA) and @figure(DBCF) are parallelograms on the same base $BC$ between the same parallels, so they are equal ([[1.35]]). Each triangle is half of its parallelogram, because a diagonal bisects a parallelogram ([[1.34]]). Halves of equal things are equal.

:::code Area as half base times height
In coordinates: $\text{area}(ABC) = \tfrac12 |BC| \cdot h$, where $h$ is the distance of $A$ from the line $BC$. Moving $A$ along a parallel to $BC$ does not change $h$, so it does not change the area. The same fact appears as the cross product: with $B$ at the origin, $\text{area} = \tfrac12 |\vec{BC} \times \vec{BA}|$, and adding a multiple of $\vec{BC}$ to $\vec{BA}$ does not change the cross product.
:::

:::gap "Halves of equal things are equal"
The bracketed line in Heath's text is not one of Euclid's common notions, and the brackets mark it as a probable later addition. The step is needed anyway: if $2x = 2y$ then $x = y$. For magnitudes this needs an argument (for instance, if $x > y$ then $2x > 2y$). Modern treatments with an area function just divide by $2$.
:::
