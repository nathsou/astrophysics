---
title: Shearing a parallelogram
---
**Statement.** Parallelograms on the same base and between the same parallels have equal area. In the figure, @figure(ABCD) and @figure(EBCF) share the base @BC, and their top sides lie on one line parallel to it. So
$$\text{area}(ABCD) = \text{area}(EBCF).$$
This is the first time in the *Elements* that "equal" means *equal in area* for figures that are not congruent. The two parallelograms have different shapes, and in general different angles and side lengths.

**Proof (cut and paste).** Take Heath's configuration, with $A, D, E, F$ in that order along the top line.

1. $AD = BC = EF$ ([[1.34]]), so adding or removing the common piece $DE$ gives $AE = DF$.
2. Triangles $EAB$ and $FDC$ have $EA = FD$, $AB = DC$ ([[1.34]]), and $\angle EAB = \angle FDC$ (corresponding angles, [[1.29]]). So they are congruent ([[1.4]]).
3. From each congruent triangle remove the small triangle $DGE$, where @point(G) is the point where @EB crosses @DC. What remains are the trapezia $ABGD$ and $EGCF$, and they have equal area ([[1.cn.3]]).
4. Add @triangle(GBC) to each trapezium. The results are the two parallelograms ([[1.cn.2]]).

No lengths are multiplied and no numbers appear. Areas are compared by adding, removing and matching congruent pieces.

:::code An invariant under shear
Put $BC$ on the $x$-axis and the top line at height $h$. Sliding the top side by $s$ is the linear map
$$\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 1 & s/h \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix},$$
a *shear*. Its determinant is $1$, so it preserves area. That is I.35 in one line of linear algebra. The shoelace formula for the signed area of a polygon,
```ts
const area = (ps: {x: number; y: number}[]) =>
  ps.reduce((s, p, i) => { const q = ps[(i + 1) % ps.length]; return s + p.x * q.y - q.x * p.y; }, 0) / 2;
```
gives $\text{base} \times \text{height}$ for both parallelograms, whatever the shift. Euclid proves the same invariant without coordinates or multiplication.
:::

:::gap One case of three
Euclid's proof covers one arrangement of the top points. If @point(E) lies between @point(A) and @point(D), the triangles $EAB$ and $FDC$ overlap differently and there is no point $G$ below the top line. If $E = D$, the triangle $DGE$ vanishes. Each case needs its own decomposition, and Euclid gives only one. The figure hides @point(G) when it does not exist. The claims still hold, and they are checked numerically, not by Euclid's decomposition.

There is a deeper point. Euclid's argument shows that the two parallelograms are *equicomplementary*: adding congruent pieces to each gives congruent figures. Hilbert showed that without Archimedes' axiom this is weaker than being *equidecomposable* (cut into finitely many pairwise congruent pieces). For I.35 itself this does not matter: in the Euclidean plane, both notions agree with equality of area for polygons (the Bolyai–Gerwien theorem).
:::

**In the figure.** Drag @point(E) along the top line, past @point(D) and back past @point(A). The shape changes, the shaded area does not.

:::leads Where it leads
[[1.36]]–[[1.41]] extend the result to equal bases and to triangles, and [[1.47]] uses [[1.41]] to move the squares on the legs into the square on the hypotenuse. The shear invariance behind this is Cavalieri's principle in two dimensions: figures with equal cross-sections at every height have equal area.
:::
