---
title: Equal and parallel sides make a parallelogram
---
**Statement.** If $AB$ and $CD$ are equal and parallel, then the segments $AC$ and $BD$ joining their ends "in the same directions" are also equal and parallel.

**Proof.** Join the diagonal @BC. Since $AB \parallel CD$, the alternate angles are equal: $\angle ABC = \angle BCD$ ([[1.29]]). Then triangles $ABC$ and $DCB$ have $AB = DC$, the common side $BC$, and equal included angles, so they are congruent by SAS ([[1.4]]). This gives $AC = BD$ and $\angle ACB = \angle CBD$. The last pair are alternate angles for $AC$ and $BD$ with the transversal $BC$, so $AC \parallel BD$ ([[1.27]]).

:::code Vectors
In vector language: if $\vec{AB} = \vec{CD}$, then $\vec{AC} = \vec{BD}$. The proof is one line, $\vec{AC} = \vec{AB} + \vec{BD} - \vec{CD} = \vec{BD}$. "Equal and parallel, in the same direction" is exactly Euclid's way of saying "equal as vectors", and I.33 is what makes it safe to treat a translation as a single object that can be applied at any point. The figure builds @point(D) as $C + (B - A)$.
:::

:::gap "In the same directions"
Euclid's parenthetical phrase matters. Joining $A$ to $D$ and $B$ to $C$ gives the diagonals, which are neither equal nor parallel in general. Euclid has no formal notion of orientation, so he says in words that $A, C$ are "on the same side" and $B, D$ are too. The proof uses it when it treats $\angle ABC$ and $\angle BCD$ as alternate angles, which needs $A$ and $D$ on opposite sides of $BC$.
:::
