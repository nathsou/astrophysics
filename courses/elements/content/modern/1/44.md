---
title: Applying a parallelogram to a line
---
**Construction.** Given a segment $AB$, a triangle $C$ and an angle $D$, construct a parallelogram on $AB$, with an angle equal to $D$ and area equal to $C$. This is the *application of an area* to a line.

1. Construct @figure(BF) equal to the triangle $C$, with $\angle EBG = D$ ([[1.42]]), and place it with @BE on @AB produced.
2. Produce @FG to meet the parallel to @BG through @point(A) at @point(H) ([[1.31]]). Join @HB.
3. @HB and @FE, produced, meet at @point(K).
4. Through @point(K) draw the parallel to @EA, and produce @HA and @GB to meet it at @point(L) and @point(M).

Then @figure(HLKF) is a parallelogram with diagonal @HK, and @figure(LB) is the answer.

**Why it works.** In $HLKF$, the parallelograms $AG$ and $ME$ lie about the diagonal $HK$, and $LB$ and $BF$ are the complements. By [[1.43]], $LB = BF$, and $BF$ equals the triangle $C$. The angle $ABM$ equals $\angle GBE$ by vertical angles ([[1.15]]), which is $D$. And $LB$ stands on $AB$.

:::code Division, done with a ruler
In numbers, the problem asks for the other side $x$ of a parallelogram with side $|AB|$, angle $\theta$ and area $S$:
$$x = \frac{S}{|AB| \sin\theta}.$$
So I.44 is division of an area by a length. Euclid cannot divide, so he uses the cross-multiplication hidden in [[1.43]]: he starts from a parallelogram of the right area on the *wrong* side, $BE$, and the diagonal $HK$ trades that side for $AB$. This is the ancestor of the fourth-proportional construction [[6.12]].
:::

:::gap Postulate 5 again, this time explicitly
Step 3 needs @HB and @FE to meet. Euclid justifies it with Postulate 5 itself: $\angle BHF + \angle HFE$ is less than $\angle AHF + \angle HFE$, which is two right angles by [[1.29]]. This is one of the few places after [[1.29]] where the postulate is cited directly. Here it is used for what it literally says, that two lines meet.

Step 1 has a smaller gap. [[1.42]] builds its parallelogram wherever the triangle happens to be, and "let it be placed" moves it. Moving a figure is superposition, which Euclid uses openly only in [[1.4]] and [[1.8]]. The fix is to run the construction of [[1.42]] at $B$ from the start, after copying the triangle there with [[1.22]] and [[1.23]]. The figure builds it at $B$ directly: $BE$ is half the triangle's base, as [[1.42]] would make it.
:::

:::history Application of areas
Proclus, following Eudemus, attributes the method of applying areas to the Pythagoreans. It has an exact version (this proposition), and versions where the parallelogram falls short of the line or exceeds it by a figure of given shape ([[6.28]], [[6.29]]). The Greek words for "apply", "fall short" and "exceed", *parabolē*, *elleipsis* and *hyperbolē*, were later used by Apollonius to name the three conic sections.
:::
