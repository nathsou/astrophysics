---
title: Parallelograms on equal bases
---
**Statement.** Parallelograms on equal bases and between the same parallels have equal area. Here @figure(ABCD) and @figure(EFGH) have $BC = FG$ on the lower line, and their top sides on the upper line.

**Proof.** Reduce to the same base, and use [[1.35]] twice. Join @BE and @CH. Now $EH = FG = BC$ ([[1.34]]), and $EH \parallel BC$, so by [[1.33]] $EB$ and $HC$ are equal and parallel, and $EBCH$ is a parallelogram. It shares the base $BC$ with $ABCD$, and the base $EH$ with $EFGH$, all between the same parallels. So
$$ABCD = EBCH = EFGH$$
by [[1.35]] twice.

:::code Composing invariants
The proof builds a bridge object, the parallelogram $EBCH$, that shares one base with each of the two figures being compared. It is like proving $f(x) = f(y)$ by finding a $z$ with $f(x) = f(z)$ and $f(z) = f(y)$, using a lemma that only applies to pairs that share something. In coordinates the conclusion is immediate: both areas are $|BC| \cdot h$, where $h$ is the distance between the parallels. Euclid has no product of lengths, so he needs the bridge.
:::

**In the figure.** Drag @point(E) along the top line and @point(F) along the bottom one. The two shaded parallelograms keep equal areas, and @figure(EBCH), drawn thin, is the bridge between them.
