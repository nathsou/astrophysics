---
title: Parallels make equal angles
---
**Statement.** If a transversal $EF$ crosses parallel lines $AB$ and $CD$ at $G$ and $H$, then

- alternate angles are equal: $\angle AGH = \angle GHD$;
- corresponding angles are equal: $\angle EGB = \angle GHD$;
- co-interior angles are supplementary: $\angle BGH + \angle GHD = 180^\circ$.

This is the converse of [[1.27]] and [[1.28]].

**Proof.** Suppose $\angle AGH \ne \angle GHD$, say $\angle AGH > \angle GHD$. Add $\angle BGH$ to both:
$$\angle AGH + \angle BGH > \angle BGH + \angle GHD.$$
The left side is $180^\circ$ ([[1.13]]), so $\angle BGH + \angle GHD < 180^\circ$. Now **Postulate 5** ([[1.post.5]]) says that two lines making co-interior angles less than two right angles with a transversal meet on that side. So $AB$ and $CD$ meet, which contradicts the hypothesis that they are parallel. The case $\angle AGH < \angle GHD$ is symmetric, working on the other side of the transversal. So the alternate angles are equal. The other two statements follow from vertical angles ([[1.15]]) and [[1.13]].

:::gap The first use of Postulate 5
Euclid held back his fifth postulate for twenty-eight propositions. Everything before this point is *neutral geometry*: it is true in the Euclidean plane and in the hyperbolic plane. Here the two part ways.

In the hyperbolic plane, take a line $CD$ and a point $G$ not on it. Many lines through $G$ miss $CD$. Exactly one of them makes equal alternate angles with the transversal $GH$, and [[1.27]] guarantees that it misses $CD$. The others also miss $CD$, but their alternate angles are unequal. So I.29 is false there, and with it everything that depends on it:

- the angle sum [[1.32]] (hyperbolic triangles have angle sum less than $180^\circ$);
- the parallelogram properties [[1.34]], and the area theory [[1.35]]–[[1.45]] built on them;
- the square [[1.46]] (no quadrilateral in the hyperbolic plane has four right angles), and Pythagoras [[1.47]].

So Book I has a neutral part (I.1–I.28) and a Euclidean part (I.29–I.48), and this proposition is the boundary between them. The [graph](#/graph?focus=1.29) shows how much of the rest of the *Elements* depends on it.
:::

:::code Existence and uniqueness
In programming terms, [[1.31]] (which uses [[1.27]]) is a constructor: given a line and a point, it returns *a* parallel. It needs no Postulate 5. What Postulate 5 adds is uniqueness. It is equivalent, given the neutral axioms, to **Playfair's axiom**: through a point not on a line there is *at most one* parallel. I.29 is uniqueness in disguise. If a parallel through $G$ made unequal alternate angles, the line through $G$ with equal ones (a parallel by [[1.27]]) would be a second parallel.

Many statements are equivalent to Postulate 5 over neutral geometry: Playfair's axiom, I.29, [[1.30]], the existence of two similar but non-congruent triangles. With the help of Archimedes' axiom, so are the angle sum [[1.32]] and the existence of a single rectangle. Proving any one of them from the neutral axioms would prove all of them, and all attempts failed for a reason.
:::

:::history Two thousand years of attempts
Ancient and medieval geometers tried to prove the postulate from the others. Proclus reports such an attempt by Ptolemy and gives one of his own. Saccheri (1733) assumed its negation, hoping for a contradiction. He derived a long list of strange theorems, and ended by claiming a contradiction that was not really one. Around 1830 Lobachevsky and Bolyai, and privately Gauss, accepted those theorems as a geometry in their own right. Beltrami's models (1868) showed that hyperbolic geometry is consistent if Euclidean geometry is, so the postulate cannot be proved. The [explorations](#/explore/geometries) put the two geometries side by side.
:::
