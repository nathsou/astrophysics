---
title: Side–angle–side (SAS) congruence
---
**Statement.** If in triangles $ABC$ and $DEF$
$$AB = DE, \qquad AC = DF, \qquad \angle BAC = \angle EDF,$$
then $BC = EF$, $\angle ABC = \angle DEF$, $\angle ACB = \angle DFE$, and the triangles are equal. Here "equal" means *congruent*: they coincide when one is placed on the other, so in particular they have the same area.

**Proof (Euclid's).** Pick up @triangle(ABC) and put it down on @triangle(DEF), @A on @D and @AB along @DE. Since $AB = DE$, @B lands on @E. Since the angles at @A and @D are equal, @AC falls along @DF, and since $AC = DF$, @C lands on @F. Now @BC and @EF join the same two points, so they coincide: otherwise two straight lines would enclose a space. Everything coincides, so everything is equal ([[1.cn.4]]).

:::gap Superposition is not in the axioms
Nothing in the postulates lets you move a figure. The proof assumes that a triangle can be carried anywhere without changing its sides and angles, which is essentially what it sets out to prove. Euclid seems uneasy with the method himself: he uses it here and in [[1.8]], and almost nowhere else.

The fix is to stop proving SAS. Hilbert takes (a slightly weaker form of) it as an axiom of congruence: if $AB = DE$, $AC = DF$ and $\angle BAC = \angle EDF$, then $\angle ABC = \angle DEF$. The rest of I.4 follows from it. This is not a defeat. Hilbert showed that SAS is *independent* of his other axioms: there are models of all the others in which it fails. Some axiom that ties lengths to angles is needed, and SAS is the natural one. Another modern route defines congruence by the rigid motions of the plane (the isometries) and proves that they act transitively on flags. Superposition then becomes the statement "there is an isometry mapping $A \mapsto D$, $B \mapsto E$", a legitimate object instead of a physical act.

The sentence about two lines enclosing a space, printed in brackets, is regarded by editors as a later addition. It supplies a fact Euclid needs and never states: two points determine at most one straight line.
:::

:::code Congruence as an equivalence of records
Model a triangle as a record of three sides and three angles. Six numbers, but only three are free. SAS says the map
$$(b, c, \alpha) \mapsto (a, \beta, \gamma)$$
is a function: two sides and the included angle determine the rest. In trigonometry this function is the law of cosines, $a^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc\cos\alpha$. Euclid gets there in [[2.12]] and [[2.13]]. The other congruence theorems, [[1.8]] (SSS) and [[1.26]] (ASA, AAS), say that other triples also determine the triangle. SSA does not, which is why there is no such proposition.
:::

**In the figure.** The second triangle is not free. @E glides on the circle about @D with radius $AB$, and @F is placed so that $DF = AC$ and $\angle EDF = \angle BAC$. Drag @E to turn @triangle(DEF): the three conclusions keep holding. The figure is the proposition as a program: the hypotheses are the data, the conclusion is checked.

:::leads Where it leads
I.4 underpins nearly every proof in Book I, starting with [[1.5]]. It holds in neutral geometry: Euclidean, hyperbolic and spherical geometry all satisfy SAS, because each has enough rigid motions. Klein's Erlangen programme (1872) turns this around and defines a geometry by its group of motions.
:::
