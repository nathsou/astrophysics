---
title: Equal triangles on the same base
---
**Statement.** If triangles $ABC$ and $DBC$ have equal areas, share the base $BC$ and lie on the same side of it, then $AD \parallel BC$. This is the converse of [[1.37]].

**Proof.** Suppose not. Draw the parallel to $BC$ through $A$ ([[1.31]]). It meets $BD$ (or $BD$ produced) at a point $E \ne D$. Join $EC$. By [[1.37]], $\triangle EBC = \triangle ABC = \triangle DBC$. But one of $EBC$ and $DBC$ lies inside the other, so the part equals the whole, which is impossible. So the parallel through $A$ passes through $D$, and it is the line $AD$.

:::gap The part and the whole
"The greater equal to the less, which is impossible" appeals to [[1.cn.5]], "the whole is greater than the part", applied to areas. With an area function this is obvious: area is additive and a proper piece has positive area. But Euclid has no area function, only cut-and-paste equality. Could a figure be cut and reassembled into a proper part of itself? In the Euclidean plane, no. Proving this from the axioms of plane geometry was a real problem in the 19th century, and is known as *de Zolt's postulate*. Hilbert proved it by constructing a measure of area from his axioms.
:::
