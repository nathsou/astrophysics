---
title: Converse of the isosceles theorem
---
**Statement.** If $\angle ABC = \angle ACB$ in triangle $ABC$, then $AB = AC$.

**Proof (Euclid's), by contradiction.** Suppose $AB > AC$. Cut off @DB from @AB equal to @AC ([[1.3]]) and join @DC. Then $\triangle DBC$ and $\triangle ACB$ have $DB = AC$, $BC$ common, and the included angles $\angle DBC = \angle ACB$ equal by hypothesis. By SAS ([[1.4]]) the triangles are equal. But $\triangle DBC$ is a part of $\triangle ACB$, and the whole is greater than the part ([[1.cn.5]]). So $AB \not> AC$, and by symmetry $AC \not> AB$, hence $AB = AC$.

This is the first proof by contradiction in the Elements, and the first converse. The contradiction is about *areas*: I.4's "the triangle is equal to the triangle" is used for the first time.

:::gap
The proof relies on trichotomy for lengths (either $AB = AC$, or one is greater) and on the fact that a triangle is not equal in area to a proper part of itself. Both are taken for granted. The second is known as De Zolt's axiom and is provable once area is properly defined, as Hilbert does.
:::

**In the figure.** @A glides on the perpendicular bisector of @BC, which keeps the base angles equal. @D and @DC are dashed: they are the objects of the supposition, and in the true figure @D would have to coincide with @A.
