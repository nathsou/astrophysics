---
title: Angles on a straight line
---
**Statement.** If a ray $BA$ stands on a line $CD$ at $B$, then
$$\angle CBA + \angle ABD = 2\text{ right angles}.$$

**Proof (Euclid's).** If the two angles are equal, each is right by definition ([[1.def.10]]). Otherwise draw @BE perpendicular to @CD ([[1.11]]). Then
$$\angle CBE + \angle EBD = (\angle CBA + \angle ABE) + \angle EBD = \angle CBA + (\angle ABE + \angle EBD) = \angle CBA + \angle ABD,$$
and the left side is two right angles.

The proof is a regrouping of three angles, $\angle CBA$, $\angle ABE$, $\angle EBD$, in two ways, with the common notions ([[1.cn.1]], [[1.cn.2]]) as the rules of algebra. Euclid has no number for "two right angles"; it is a fixed magnitude that any pair of supplementary angles adds up to. In degrees it is $180^\circ$, but degrees are Babylonian, and Euclid does not use them.

:::gap
Adding angles needs a sum whose value is still an angle. Euclid's angles (by [[1.def.8]]) are less than two right angles, so "$\angle CBA + \angle ABD$" is not an angle but a formal sum, and "equal to two right angles" compares formal sums. Modern treatments either use angle measure with values in $(0, \pi)$, as the figure does, or develop an arithmetic of angles with a partial addition. The rearrangement also assumes that @BA lies inside @angle(CBE) or inside @angle(EBD), which is again a betweenness fact.
:::
