---
title: Copying an angle
---
**Statement.** Given an angle $DCE$ and a ray $AB$, construct a ray $AG$ with $\angle FAG = \angle DCE$, where $F$ is on $AB$.

**Construction.** Take any @D and @E on the arms of @angle(DCE) and join @DE. Build @triangle(AFG) with $AF = CD$, $AG = CE$, $FG = DE$ ([[1.22]]), with @AF laid along @AB.

**Why it works.** By SSS ([[1.8]]), the triangles $CDE$ and $AFG$ are congruent, so $\angle FAG = \angle DCE$.

The diorism of [[1.22]] is satisfied automatically: the sides of an existing triangle obey the triangle inequality ([[1.20]]).

With I.2 (copy a length) and I.23 (copy an angle), a figure can be moved anywhere piece by piece. This is how Euclid, having avoided superposition after [[1.8]], gets its effects by construction.

:::gap
The copy can be made on either side of @AB: the circles of [[1.22]] meet in two points. Euclid does not say which one he takes. The figure uses the one above @AB, as in Heath's diagram.
:::
