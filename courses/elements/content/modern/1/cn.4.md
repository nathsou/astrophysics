---
title: Coincidence (superposition)
---
Things that coincide with one another are equal to one another.

This is the logical basis of superposition: if one figure can be placed exactly on another, they are equal. Euclid uses it in [[1.4]] and [[1.8]] to prove the congruence theorems.

Two problems are hidden in it.

- **Motion.** Placing one figure on another requires moving it, and nothing in the postulates allows that, or guarantees that moving a figure leaves its lengths and angles unchanged. That is what congruence was supposed to explain in the first place.
- **Direction.** The notion goes one way: coincidence implies equality. It does not say equal things can be made to coincide, and for areas they cannot: a triangle and a square can be equal in area without coinciding.

:::gap How modern geometry handles it
Hilbert replaces superposition with undefined relations, congruence of segments and of angles, and axioms for them, including SAS. Another route, Klein's, makes the motions themselves the primitive: a geometry is a space together with a group of transformations, and two figures are congruent when a transformation in the group maps one onto the other. Superposition then becomes a precise statement, "there is an isometry $g$ with $g(F_1) = F_2$".
:::
