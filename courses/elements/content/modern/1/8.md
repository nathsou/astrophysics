---
title: Side–side–side (SSS) congruence
---
**Statement.** If triangles $ABC$ and $DEF$ have $AB = DE$, $AC = DF$ and $BC = EF$, then $\angle BAC = \angle EDF$ (and so, by [[1.4]], they are congruent).

**Proof (Euclid's).** Place @triangle(ABC) on @triangle(DEF) with @B on @E and @BC along @EF. Since $BC = EF$, @C lands on @F. If @A did not land on @D, but at some other point @G on the same side of @EF, then on the segment @EF there would be two different points with $EG = ED$ and $GF = DF$. That is impossible by [[1.7]]. So @A lands on @D and the angles coincide.

:::gap
This is the second and last proof by superposition in Book I (see [[1.4]]). Here it can be avoided: put the triangles on opposite sides of a common base, join the apexes, and apply [[1.5]] twice. That is Philo's proof, reported by Proclus, and it is the standard modern one. In Hilbert's axioms SSS is a theorem, proved from SAS in this way.
:::

**In the figure.** @F glides on the circle about @E with radius $BC$, and @D is found as the meeting point of the circles about @E and @F with radii $BA$ and $CA$. The three sides match by construction; the claim is that the angle does too. @G and the dashed lines are the "other" apex of the reductio.

:::code Rigid structures
SSS says that a triangle built from three rods is rigid: its shape is a function of its side lengths. That is why trusses are made of triangles and not of quadrilaterals, whose angles are not determined by their sides. In computational geometry the same fact makes a triangle mesh a sound way to describe a surface.
:::
