---
title: Angle sum of a triangle
---
**Statement.** In any triangle $ABC$, with $BC$ produced to $D$:
$$\angle ACD = \angle CAB + \angle ABC \qquad\text{and}\qquad \angle ABC + \angle BCA + \angle CAB = 180^\circ.$$
The exterior angle is the sum of the two interior opposite angles, and the three angles make two right angles.

**Proof.** Through @point(C) draw @CE parallel to @AB ([[1.31]]). This splits the exterior angle @angle(ACD) into two parts, and [[1.29]] identifies each one:

- $\angle ACE = \angle BAC$: alternate angles, with transversal $AC$;
- $\angle ECD = \angle ABC$: corresponding angles, with transversal $BD$.

Adding, $\angle ACD = \angle BAC + \angle ABC$. Add $\angle ACB$ to both sides. The left side becomes $\angle ACD + \angle ACB = 180^\circ$ ([[1.13]]), and the right side is the angle sum.

Compare [[1.16]] and [[1.17]]. Without parallels, Euclid could only show that the exterior angle is *greater* than each interior opposite angle, and that any two angles sum to *less* than $180^\circ$. Postulate 5, through [[1.29]], turns those inequalities into an exact equation.

:::gap What the other geometries do
The proof uses [[1.29]], so the result depends on Postulate 5, and it fails in the other classical geometries:

- **Hyperbolic plane** (curvature $-1$): the sum is always less than $180^\circ$, and the *defect* $\pi - (\alpha + \beta + \gamma)$ equals the triangle's area. Small triangles are nearly Euclidean, large ones have tiny angles, and no triangle has area more than $\pi$.
- **Sphere** (radius 1): the sum is always more than $180^\circ$, and the *excess* $(\alpha + \beta + \gamma) - \pi$ equals the area. A triangle with a vertex at the north pole and two on the equator, a quarter-turn apart, has three right angles.

Both are cases of the Gauss–Bonnet theorem: in a geometry of constant curvature $K$, the angle sum minus $\pi$ equals $K$ times the area. The Euclidean plane is the case $K = 0$.

Without Postulate 5, one can still prove that the sum is *at most* $180^\circ$ (Saccheri and Legendre), with the help of Archimedes' axiom. In neutral geometry the statement "some triangle has angle sum exactly $180^\circ$" is equivalent to the parallel postulate, again given Archimedes' axiom.
:::

:::code Turtle graphics
Walk around a convex polygon with a turtle: at each vertex it turns left by the exterior angle. When it is back where it started, facing the same way, it has turned through exactly $360^\circ$. For a triangle this gives $\sum(180^\circ - \text{interior}) = 360^\circ$, so the interior angles sum to $180^\circ$. For a convex $n$-gon the interior angles sum to $(n-2)\cdot 180^\circ$.

```ts
const exteriorSum = (interior: number[]) => interior.reduce((s, a) => s + (180 - a), 0);
exteriorSum([60, 60, 60]);        // 360
exteriorSum([90, 90, 90, 90]);    // 360
```

The "total turning is $360^\circ$" argument secretly uses Postulate 5 too. On a sphere a turtle that walks a triangle turns by less than $360^\circ$, and the shortfall is the enclosed area.
:::

:::history
Proclus, quoting Eudemus, credits the Pythagoreans with discovering this theorem and gives a proof of theirs, which draws a parallel to the base through the apex instead of through $C$. Aristotle uses the theorem as a standard example of a necessary truth.
:::
