---
title: Uniqueness of a triangle with given sides
---
**Statement.** On a segment $AB$, and on one side of it, there cannot be two different points $C$ and $D$ with $AC = AD$ and $BC = BD$.

In modern terms: a point on a given side of line $AB$ is determined by its distances from $A$ and $B$. Equivalently, two circles meet in at most one point on each side of the line of their centres.

**Proof (Euclid's), by contradiction.** Suppose there are two such points, and join @CD.

- $AC = AD$, so $\angle ACD = \angle ADC$ ([[1.5]]).
- In Euclid's figure, @D lies outside @triangle(ACB). Then $\angle ADC > \angle DCB$, because the ray @CB lies inside @angle(ACD); so $\angle CDB > \angle ADC > \angle DCB$.
- But $BC = BD$, so $\angle CDB = \angle DCB$ ([[1.5]]). Contradiction.

:::gap Euclid's figure is one case of several
The argument depends on where @D falls relative to @triangle(ACB). If @D lies inside it, the angles compared above are not in the right position, and one needs the equality of the angles *under the base* from [[1.5]]. Proclus gives this second case, and points out that this is what the second half of [[1.5]] is for; there is a third, with @D on @CB. Nothing in Euclid's axioms tells you which case you are in. Deciding "inside or outside" is a betweenness question, and betweenness is exactly what Euclid's system cannot express. Hilbert's order axioms, above all Pasch's axiom (a line entering a triangle through one side leaves it through another), were introduced to make arguments like this one complete.
:::

**In the figure.** @D glides on the circle about @A through @C, so $AD = AC$ holds throughout. Watch the readouts: $DB$ equals $CB$ only when @D reaches @C. The dashed lines are the supposed second pair that the proposition rules out.
