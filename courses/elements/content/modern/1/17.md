---
title: Two angles of a triangle are less than two right angles
---
**Statement.** In any triangle, any two angles together are less than two right angles.

**Proof (Euclid's).** Produce @BC to @D. By [[1.16]], $\angle ACD > \angle ABC$. Add $\angle ACB$ to both: $\angle ACD + \angle ACB > \angle ABC + \angle ACB$. The left side is two right angles ([[1.13]]).

:::leads Postulate 5 and its converse
Compare this with Postulate 5 ([[1.post.5]]). I.17 says: if two lines meet (forming a triangle with a transversal), the interior angles on that side add up to less than two right angles. Postulate 5 is the converse: if the interior angles add up to less than two right angles, the lines meet on that side. Euclid proves the one and must postulate the other.

I.17 also bounds the angle sum of a triangle in neutral geometry. Saccheri and Legendre proved, without Postulate 5 but with the Archimedean property, that the three angles together are *at most* two right angles. In hyperbolic geometry they are always strictly less; the missing amount, the *defect*, is proportional to the area. Only with Postulate 5 does [[1.32]] make the sum exactly two right angles.
:::

**Consequences.** A triangle has at most one right or obtuse angle, so the classification of triangles in [[1.def.21]] is exhaustive and exclusive. The perpendicular from a point to a line is unique, and the base angles of an isosceles triangle are acute.
