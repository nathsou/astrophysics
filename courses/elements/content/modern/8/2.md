---
title: The least progression in a given ratio
---
**Statement.** Let $p : q$ be a ratio in least terms. The least $n + 1$ numbers in continued proportion in that ratio are
$$p^n,\; p^{n-1} q,\; \dots,\; p q^{n-1},\; q^n.$$

**Porism.** In the least progression of three terms the extremes are squares, and in the least progression of four terms they are cubes.

**Construction.** Euclid builds four terms. Multiply @A $= p$ and @B $= q$ by $A$ and by $B$:
$$C = A^2,\quad D = AB,\quad E = B^2,$$
and multiply once more:
$$F = A \cdot C,\quad G = A \cdot D,\quad H = A \cdot E,\quad K = B \cdot E.$$

**Proof.** Multiplying two numbers by the same number keeps their ratio ([[7.17]]), and multiplying the same number by two numbers gives products in the ratio of the multipliers ([[7.18]]). So $C : D = A : B$ (both multiplied by $A$) and $D : E = A : B$ (both multiply $B$). Hence @C, @D, @E are in continued proportion in the ratio $A : B$. The same two rules show that @F, @G, @H, @K are.

They are the least. $A, B$ are least in their ratio, so they are prime to one another ([[7.22]]). Then their squares @C, @E and their cubes @F, @K are prime to one another ([[7.27]]). A progression with coprime extremes is the least in its ratio ([[8.1]]).

:::code A generator
```ts
// The least n+1 numbers in continued proportion in the ratio p : q (gcd(p, q) = 1).
const leastProgression = (p: number, q: number, n: number): number[] =>
  Array.from({ length: n + 1 }, (_, i) => p ** (n - i) * q ** i);

leastProgression(2, 3, 3); // [8, 12, 18, 27]
```
Every progression in the ratio $p : q$ is a multiple $k \cdot$ `leastProgression(p, q, n)` (by [[7.20]] applied to the extremes, as in [[8.8]]). So a progression of whole numbers is fully described by three numbers $k, p, q$. The rest of Book VIII can be read as consequences of this normal form.
:::

:::gap "Let four be prescribed"
Euclid proves the case of four terms and says the method continues. For $n + 1$ terms one needs $\gcd(p^n, q^n) = 1$, and [[7.27]] gives this only for squares and cubes. The general case is an easy induction on [[7.24]]–[[7.26]], but Euclid does not write it. Book VIII argues on representative cases throughout, as Book VII did.
:::
