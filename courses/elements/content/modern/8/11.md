---
title: One mean between two squares
---
**Statement.** Between two squares $c^2$ and $d^2$ there is a mean proportional, namely $cd$:
$$c^2 : cd = cd : d^2 = c : d.$$
And $c^2 : d^2$ is the ratio $c : d$ *duplicated*, that is, $\frac{c^2}{d^2} = \left(\frac cd\right)^2$.

**Proof.** Let @E $= C \cdot D$. Multiplying @C and @D by $C$ gives @A and $E$, so $A : E = C : D$ ([[7.17]]). Multiplying $C$ and $D$ by @D gives $E$ and @B, so $E : B = C : D$ ([[7.18]]). Hence $A, E, B$ is a progression in the ratio $C : D$.

By definition ([[5.def.9]]), when three magnitudes are in continued proportion, the first has to the third the *duplicate* ratio of the first to the second. So $A : B$ is $C : D$ duplicated.

The grey marks show how each rod is built: $A$ is $C$ copies of $C$, $E$ is $D$ copies of $C$, and $B$ is $D$ copies of $D$.

:::leads Where it leads
This is the first half of the characterization in [[8.18]]–[[8.20]]: one mean fits between two numbers exactly when they are similar plane numbers, and squares are the simplest case. [[10.9]] uses this proposition to show that squares on commensurable lengths are in the ratio of a square number to a square number.
:::
