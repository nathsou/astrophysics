---
title: A least progression has coprime extremes
---
**Statement.** If $a_0, \dots, a_n$ are the least numbers in continued proportion in their ratio, then $\gcd(a_0, a_n) = 1$. This is the converse of [[8.1]].

**Proof.** Take the least two numbers @E, @F in the ratio $A : B$ ([[7.33]]), and from them, by [[8.2]], the least three @G, @H, @K and the least four @L, @M, @N, @O in the same ratio. $E, F$ are least, so they are prime to one another ([[7.22]]). $L$ and $O$ are the cubes of $E$ and $F$ ([[8.2]], porism), so they are prime to one another too ([[7.27]]).

Now @A, @B, @C, @D and $L, M, N, O$ are both the least numbers in the same ratio, and there are equally many of them, so they are the same numbers. In particular $A = L$ and $D = O$, and these are prime to one another.

In the figure the sliders produce least progressions $a^3, a^2 b, a b^2, b^3$, and $L, M, N, O$ always coincide with them.

**Together with [[8.1]] and [[8.2]].** For a progression $a_0, \dots, a_n$ in the ratio $p : q$ (lowest terms), the following are equivalent:

1. it is the least in its ratio;
2. $\gcd(a_0, a_n) = 1$;
3. it is $p^n, p^{n-1}q, \dots, q^n$.

:::gap Why "the least" is unique
Euclid moves from "both are least" to "they are equal" without comment. The step is sound: by [[7.20]] applied to the extremes, each least progression divides the other term by term, so they are equal. Today one says that the primitive progression in a given ratio is unique, just as a fraction has only one form in lowest terms.
:::
