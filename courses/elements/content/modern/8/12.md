---
title: Two means between two cubes
---
**Statement.** Between two cubes $c^3$ and $d^3$ there are two mean proportionals, $c^2 d$ and $c d^2$:
$$c^3 : c^2 d = c^2 d : c d^2 = c d^2 : d^3 = c : d.$$
And $c^3 : d^3$ is the ratio $c : d$ *triplicated* ([[5.def.10]]).

**Proof.** Build @E $= C^2$, @F $= CD$, @G $= D^2$ as in [[8.11]]. They are in continued proportion in the ratio $C : D$. Then multiply once more: @A $= C \cdot E$, @H $= C \cdot F$, @K $= D \cdot F$, @B $= D \cdot G$. Using [[7.17]] and [[7.18]] exactly as in [[8.2]]:

- $A : H = E : F = C : D$ (both multiplied by $C$);
- $H : K = C : D$ ($C$ and $D$ multiply $F$);
- $K : B = F : G = C : D$ (both multiplied by $D$).

So $A, H, K, B$ is a progression in the ratio $C : D$. By the definition of triplicate ratio, $A : B$ is $C : D$ triplicated.

This is [[8.2]] again, with $C, D$ in place of a ratio in least terms. Nothing here requires $C, D$ to be prime to one another.

:::history Doubling the cube
Two means between two lengths is the geometric version of this proposition, and it is the key to the ancient problem of doubling the cube. Hippocrates of Chios reduced the problem to finding two mean proportionals $x, y$ with $a : x = x : y = y : 2a$, since then $x^3 = 2a^3$. For lengths, the means need not exist among constructible lengths, and the Greeks found them with conic sections and mechanical curves. For numbers, this proposition says they exist between cubes; [[8.21]] shows that they exist only between similar solid numbers.
:::
