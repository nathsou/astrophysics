---
title: If the first term does not divide the second
---
**Statement.** If $a_0, a_1, \dots, a_n$ are in continued proportion and $a_0 \nmid a_1$, then no term divides a later term.

**Modern version.** Write the ratio in least terms as $p : q$, so $a_i = k p^{n-i} q^i$. Then $a_0 \nmid a_1$ means exactly that $p > 1$. For $i < j$,
$$\frac{a_j}{a_i} = \left(\frac qp\right)^{j - i},$$
and this is never a whole number, because $p^{j-i}$ and $q^{j-i}$ are prime to one another and $p^{j-i} > 1$.

**Proof (Euclid's).** Suppose @A divides @C. Take the least numbers @F, @G, @H in the ratio of $A, B, C$ ([[7.33]]). Then:

1. $F \nmid G$, because $F : G = A : B$ and $A \nmid B$ ([[7.def.20]]). In particular $F$ is not the unit.
2. $F$ and $H$ are prime to one another, since they are the extremes of a least progression ([[8.3]]).
3. $A : C = F : H$ ex aequali ([[7.14]]). So $A \mid C$ forces $F \mid H$. But $F > 1$ is prime to $H$, so it cannot divide it.

The same argument works for any pair of terms.

:::gap Earlier terms only
Read literally, "neither will any other measure any other" is false for a decreasing progression. In $8, 4, 2$ the first term does not measure the second (it is greater), yet $2 \mid 8$. Euclid's proof only shows that no term measures a *later* one, and his hypothesis $A \nmid B$ is meant as "the ratio $A : B$ is not that of the unit to a number", that is $p > 1$. If also $q > 1$, as in the figure (try ratio $a = 3$, $b = 2$ for a decreasing progression), then no term measures any other in either direction.
:::
