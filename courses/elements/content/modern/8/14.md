---
title: Squares divide iff their sides divide
---
**Statement.** For numbers $c, d$:
$$c^2 \mid d^2 \iff c \mid d.$$

The direction $\Leftarrow$ is easy: if $d = kc$ then $d^2 = k^2 c^2$. The content is $\Rightarrow$. It can fail in other number systems, and it is not obvious even for integers. For example $6 \mid 4 \cdot 9$, although $6$ divides neither $4$ nor $9$: a number can divide a product without dividing either factor. Why can't $c^2$ divide $d \cdot d$ without $c$ dividing $d$?

**Proof (Euclid's).** Let @A $= C^2$ divide @B $= D^2$, and put @E $= C \cdot D$. Then $A, E, B$ is a progression in the ratio $C : D$ ([[8.11]]). The first term of a progression divides the last, so it divides the second ([[8.7]]): $A \mid E$. And $A : E = C : D$, so $C$ divides $D$ as many times as $A$ divides $E$ ([[7.def.20]]).

The converse runs the same chain backwards: $C \mid D$ gives $A \mid E$, and then $E \mid B$ as well, since $E : B = C : D$ too. So $A \mid B$.

Move the sliders: the readouts "A measures B" and "C measures D" always agree.

:::leads The view from unique factorization
Write $c = \prod p^{\alpha_p}$ and $d = \prod p^{\beta_p}$. Then $c \mid d$ means $\alpha_p \le \beta_p$ for every prime $p$, and squaring doubles every exponent:
$$c^2 \mid d^2 \iff 2\alpha_p \le 2\beta_p \text{ for all } p \iff \alpha_p \le \beta_p \text{ for all } p \iff c \mid d.$$
With the fundamental theorem of arithmetic the proposition is a one-liner. The same argument gives $c^k \mid d^k \iff c \mid d$ for every $k$.

::prime-exponents{a="12" b="18" k="2"}

Euclid never states the fundamental theorem. His route is the theory of least numbers in a ratio ([[7.20]]–[[7.22]]), which leads to [[8.3]], then [[8.6]], [[8.7]], and here. That chain does the same job as unique factorization. The fact it needs, that the extremes of a least progression are prime to one another, is in effect $\gcd(p^n, q^n) = 1$ when $\gcd(p, q) = 1$. That is what unique factorization gives for free.
:::

:::leads Irrational square roots
If $n$ is not a square number, $\sqrt n$ is irrational. Suppose $\sqrt n = d / c$. Then $d^2 = n c^2$, so $c^2 \mid d^2$, so $c \mid d$ by this proposition, and $n = (d/c)^2$ is the square of a whole number. The same argument appears in geometric form in [[10.9]], which uses [[8.11]] and [[8.26]]: two squares on commensurable lengths are in the ratio of a square number to a square number.
:::
